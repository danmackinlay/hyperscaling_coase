# Brief: technical spine for the article

Target file: `_plans/hyperscaling_coase_constitutional_complexity_3.qmd`

Title (verbatim, do not edit): **Computational and informational costs imply selfish delegated agent constitutional negotiation still need a benevolent leviathan**

The article answers Séb Krier, "Coasean Bargaining at Scale" (Cosmos Institute, 26 September 2025), <https://blog.cosmos-institute.org/p/coasean-bargaining-at-scale>.

This brief is my working notes. It is written in shorthand. None of its labels (R1, C3, E2 …) may appear in the article.

## 1. What Krier claims (quote accurately; fetch the URL to check wording)

- Transaction costs are why societies default to the planner. AI agents cut them: "What costs $10,000 in legal fees today might cost pennies to compute tomorrow."
- "AGI could enable hyper-granular contracting." Preferences conditional on context: loud music Saturday not Sunday; carpool if under ten minutes extra.
- Agents as coordination device: "instead of each actor guessing everyone else's move (what economists would call a Nash deadlock), they can condition their actions on shared signals and contracts, unlocking deals that were previously out of reach - a correlated equilibrium."
- Truck example: the truck's agent "simply sees a higher 'price' for entry onto your street, a signal broadcast by your agent".
- Developer example: "The developer's agent must discover the price at which every single homeowner would agree."
- Holdouts fixed by a *voluntary* bargaining club: "each person privately inputs the minimum they'd accept; the software aggregates that into a single take‑it‑or‑leave‑it offer … Overstating just risks killing the deal (you get zero), and if it clears, the payout is at the common clearing price - so padding your number doesn't boost your check. The group speaks with one voice without surrendering property rights".
- Guardrails: a Harberger-style "option fee"; "Any coalition that vetoes must then reimburse that quantified loss".
- Coalitions: 10,000 residents "can form a bargaining coalition in a nanosecond" and declare "Our collective price to accept this pollution is X million dollars, non-negotiable."
- Two modes, "economic negotiations where willingness-to-pay determines outcomes" and "democratic negotiations where each person gets equal voting weight"; "Agents can seamlessly switch between these modes depending on the issue at stake".
- Default entitlements: "The machinery runs either way. What does change is who ends up richer, which is why the initial allocation of these rights is a constitutional choice, not a technicality." Agents make the baseline "explicit, contestable, and cheap to renegotiate". Starting point: "centuries of jurisprudence", amended by democratic systems that agents improve.
- Remaining problems "are mechanism design and governance challenges, not fundamental obstacles".
- The state survives but shrinks: "it transitions its role from 'central planner' to 'framework guarantor'"; "it just makes it a lot leaner". It enforces contracts, criminal law, bans catastrophic risks.
- Hayek motivation: the planner "cannot possess the dispersed, specific knowledge of time and place".
- He concedes: wealth effects, endowment effect, Acemoglu's political Coase theorem ("The sovereign can always renege"), need for stability ("constant renegotiation is expensive even for AGI").
- Competition between agent providers keeps agents loyal: "in a competitive market with switching costs approaching zero, parasitic agents get quickly identified and abandoned."

Be fair to him. He does not say the state vanishes. The disagreement is narrower and sharper: he says the residual state is lean, a guarantor and not a planner, and that what remains is engineering. The article argues the residue is exactly the hard part, grows with the two quantities his proposal scales up, and requires an actor with a planner's knowledge, a sovereign's powers and a welfare objective.

## 2. Thesis in one paragraph

Take Krier's premise at full strength: computation and messaging are free, and every agent is perfectly loyal to its principal. Bargaining losses have a part that is proportional to the price of computation and a part that is not. The second part comes from (i) private information, (ii) the number of bits and operations the allocation problem itself demands, and (iii) spending on bargaining advantage, which rises to meet any fall in its unit price. For the public decisions that make up the essay's examples, the first of these gets worse as the number of parties $N$ grows. Every known remedy is a sovereign act: compelling a non-consenting party, taxing to fund a subsidy, assigning entitlements using knowledge of the distribution of preferences, restricting what contracts may say, choosing which equilibrium is played, deciding uncontracted cases. Loyal self-interested agents cannot supply these by bargaining, because bargaining over them is the same problem one level up. So the scheme still needs an actor that can coerce and that maximises welfare and not its own payoff. Call it a benevolent leviathan. Nothing in the scheme produces one.

## 3. Definitions (introduce each once, at first use, then never vary the term)

- **Principal / agent.** $N$ principals. Principal $i$ has a private type $\theta_i\in\Theta_i$ (everything about their preferences that others cannot observe), drawn from a prior distribution $F$. Payoff $u_i = v_i(x,\theta_i)+t_i$ for outcome $x\in X$ and money transfer $t_i$ (quasi-linear).
- **Selfish delegated agent.** Software that chooses messages and actions to maximise $u_i$ and nothing else. "Selfish" means indifferent to other principals. It does not mean disloyal. Perfect loyalty is assumed throughout: the best case for Krier. Agency slack would add to every cost below.
- **Constitutional negotiation.** Bargaining over the rules: who holds which entitlement, which mechanism aggregates reports, what a contract may say, who decides unforeseen cases. Distinct from bargaining under given rules. (Buchanan and Tullock 1962 make the same split between constitutional and post-constitutional choice.)
- **Mechanism.** A rule mapping the agents' messages to an outcome and transfers.
- The four standard properties, each defined in a clause the first time: **incentive compatible** (no agent gains by misreporting its type, given others report truthfully); **individually rational** / voluntary (every agent, knowing its own type, expects to do at least as well as by refusing to take part); **budget balanced** (transfers sum to zero: no outside money in or out); **efficient** (the outcome maximises $\sum_i v_i$).
- **First-best welfare** $W^\ast(\theta)=\max_{x}\sum_i v_i(x,\theta_i)$.
- $c$: price of one unit of computation. $\kappa$: price of sending one bit. Krier's premise is $c,\kappa\to 0$.
- $N$: number of parties. $m$: number of distinct items or rights being allocated. $k$: number of binary contingencies a contract can condition on.
- **Benevolent leviathan.** An actor with two properties. *Leviathan*: it can impose outcomes on parties who do not consent, including taxes. *Benevolent*: its objective is aggregate welfare $\sum_i v_i$ (or some agreed social welfare function), not its own revenue. The phrase deliberately joins Hobbes with the public-choice warning of Brennan and Buchanan (1980), who model the state as a revenue-maximising Leviathan precisely because benevolence cannot be assumed.

Suggested decomposition, stated once in the setup: expected loss relative to first best
$$L(c,\kappa)=\underbrace{L_{\text{friction}}(c,\kappa)}_{\to 0}+L_{\text{info}}+L_{\text{comp}}(c,\kappa;m,N,k)+L_{\text{contest}}$$
where friction is search, drafting and messaging that scale with $c,\kappa$; info is the loss forced by incentive compatibility under private types (does not depend on $c,\kappa$ at all); comp is the loss from being unable to compute or communicate the efficient outcome (depends on $c,\kappa$ only through how fast $m$, $N$, $k$ grow against them); contest is resources burned on bargaining advantage. Krier's argument treats $L$ as if it were $L_{\text{friction}}$.

## 4. Informational costs

All results here hold with $c=\kappa=0$.

**(a) Two parties: Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983).** Seller's cost $s\sim F_s$, buyer's value $b\sim F_b$, independent, with overlapping supports. No mechanism is at once incentive compatible, individually rational, budget balanced and efficient. Textbook case, both uniform on $[0,1]$: first-best expected gains $\mathbb E[(b-s)^+]=1/6$; the best feasible mechanism trades only when $b\ge s+1/4$ and realises $9/64$, a fraction $27/32\approx 0.84$ of first best. The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism (buyer pays $s$, seller receives $b$) is efficient, truthful and voluntary but runs a deficit equal to the whole gains from trade, $b-s$, on every trade. So in the bilateral case the loss is moderate, and efficiency can be bought by an outside subsidy of $1/6$ per pair. This is the truck against one resident.

**(b) Many parties, one decision: Mailath and Postlewaite (1990); Rob (1989).** A yes/no decision needs everyone's agreement. Each agent's value for "yes" (positive or negative) is private, independent across agents. Abstract, verbatim: "It is proved, under very mild conditions, that with independent types, as the number of agents increases, the probability of an affirmative efficient decision goes to zero. An example in which it is common knowledge that an affirmative decision is efficient and yet the probability of such a decision goes to zero is given." Rob (1989) gets the same limit for a firm compensating $N$ residents whose pollution damages are private. This is Krier's developer and his factory. Intuition to give (as intuition, attributed): what a mechanism can extract from agent $i$ is bounded by $i$'s influence on the decision; average influence across $N$ agents falls like $N^{-1/2}$ (Al-Najjar and Smorodinsky 2000), so total collectable contributions grow like $\sqrt N$ while the amount to be covered grows like $N$.
Reversing the entitlement does not help: if the polluter holds the right, the residents must jointly pay for abatement, which is public-good provision with the same theorem (free-riding replaces holding out).
Related complete-information results, same direction: Dixit and Olson (2000) show that when joining the bargain is itself voluntary, public-good outcomes range from very inefficient to efficient, the efficient equilibrium does not survive even very small transaction costs, and they "outline a kind of coercion that restores efficiency". Ellingsen and Paltseva (2016): with voluntary participation and more than two players "there are situations in which all consistent equilibria are inefficient … the provision of public goods tends to be inefficiently low due to strategic abstention from contracting."

**(c) Contrast: private goods in thick markets.** With $N$ buyers and $N$ sellers of a private good in a double auction, strategic misreporting is $O(1/N)$ and the efficiency loss is $O(1/N^{2})$ (Rustichini, Satterthwaite and Williams 1994). So scale helps exchange of rival goods and hurts decisions that bind many parties at once. This asymmetry is the organising observation of the section: externalities are the second kind. State it prominently.

**(d) Krier's bargaining club, analysed (our small derivation).** Members report minimum acceptable payments $r_i$; true minima $\theta_i$ i.i.d. with distribution function $F$ on $[\underline\theta,\bar\theta]$. Three ways to read "common clearing price", and each gives up one property.
 1. Price independent of reports (developer posts per-household price $p$; the project goes ahead only if every member accepts). Truth-telling is then dominant, as Krier says. But the project proceeds iff $\max_i\theta_i\le p$, which has probability $F(p)^N$, going to zero geometrically in $N$ for any $p<\bar\theta$. The efficient rule is to proceed iff $\sum_i\theta_i\le V$ where $V$ is the developer's value. Efficiency is lost.
 2. Price computed from reports so that all consent (e.g. $p=\max_i r_i$). Then the highest reporter sets the price, and padding does raise that member's payment. Truthfulness is lost. General fact behind this: the only efficient dominant-strategy mechanisms are Groves mechanisms (Green and Laffont 1979; Holmström 1979) and they do not balance the budget.
 3. The club binds dissenters: proceeds on an aggregate or majority test and pays everyone $p$. This can be truthful and efficient in the limit. But members with $\theta_i>p$ are expropriated. This is compulsory purchase. "Without surrendering property rights" is lost.
 Joining the club before knowing one's type is consistent with voluntariness (see (g)). Joining after is not, for high types.

**(e) Per-street prices: Cournot's complements problem (our small derivation).** Krier's truck sees a price broadcast by each resident. Let the truck's value for the route be $v$ with distribution $G$ (density $g$), private. Resident $i$ suffers harm $h_i$ and posts $p_i$. Total price $P=\sum_i p_i$, total harm $H=\sum_i h_i$. Efficient: the trip happens iff $v\ge H$. Resident $i$ maximises $(p_i-h_i)\,(1-G(P))$, giving $p_i-h_i=\frac{1-G(P)}{g(P)}$. Summing over $i$:
$$P-H=N\,\frac{1-G(P)}{g(P)}.$$
The markup over true harm is $N$ times the inverse hazard rate of the truck's value. For $v$ uniform on $[0,1]$ and $H<1$: $P=\frac{H+N}{N+1}$, so trips made are $1-P=\frac{1-H}{N+1}$ against the efficient $1-H$. Realised trips are a fraction $1/(N+1)$ of efficient trips. Merging the residents into one price-setter cuts the markup to one inverse hazard rate (a monopoly), not to zero. Caveat to state: where the truck has substitute routes, competition between routes disciplines $P$; the result bites where the affected group cannot be routed around, which describes an airshed, a neighbourhood's character, a shared wall. References: Cournot (1838); Buchanan and Yoon (2000) on the anticommons.
 Also Arrow (1969): a full set of externality markets has one commodity per (affected person, acting person, action) triple, of order $N^2K$ markets for $K$ kinds of action, each with one buyer and one seller, so nobody is a price-taker. And Starrett (1972): once pollution rights are traded, the victim's production set is non-convex in (output, rights held), because the victim can always shut down and so bounds its loss; a competitive equilibrium in these markets may not exist. Use this to answer "the externality … does get a price tag. And once a cost is made clear, the marvel of the market can solve it."

**(f) What restores efficiency.** The expected-externality mechanism (d'Aspremont and Gérard-Varet 1979; Arrow 1979) is incentive compatible, efficient and budget balanced for any $N$ with independent types. It fails individual rationality: some types would rather not take part. VCG keeps voluntariness and efficiency and fails budget balance. So under private information the efficient outcome is reachable iff some actor can do one of: bind non-consenting types; inject outside funds (which must be raised by taxation); or (next item) redraw the entitlements. Holmström (1982) is the classic statement that a team needs an outside "budget breaker".

**(g) Entitlements decide feasibility, not only distribution.** Cramton, Gibbons and Klemperer (1987): partners hold shares $r_1,\dots,r_N$ of an asset, values private and i.i.d. An efficient, incentive-compatible, voluntary, budget-balanced reallocation exists iff the shares are close enough to equal; with one party owning everything it does not (that is Myerson–Satterthwaite). Segal and Whinston (2011, 2016) generalise: whether efficient voluntary bargaining exists depends on the status-quo allocation of rights, and the allocations that work are typically shared or probabilistic, not the simple all-to-one property rights of common law. So "The machinery runs either way. What does change is who ends up richer" is false once types are private: for some assignments no efficient voluntary machinery exists. Choosing an assignment that works requires knowing $F$. Krier's proposed default, inherited jurisprudence, mostly assigns all-to-one rights, the least favourable case.

**(h) The veil of ignorance, and why a better advocate thins it (our small observation).** The expected-externality mechanism is voluntary if agents commit before learning their types (ex ante). That is Buchanan and Tullock's constitutional stage and Harsanyi/Rawls's veil. Krier's advocate "could also develop an increasingly sophisticated (though never perfect) model of who you are". Let the agent hold a signal $s_i$ about $\theta_i$ when it decides whether to join. Voluntariness requires $\mathbb E[u_i\mid s_i]\ge \mathbb E[\bar u_i\mid s_i]$ for every value of $s_i$, where $\bar u_i$ is the payoff from staying out. A more informative signal (in Blackwell's order) imposes a finer set of such constraints; each coarse constraint is an average of fine ones, so the set of mechanisms that remain voluntary can only shrink. The better the advocate knows its principal at the constitutional moment, the fewer constitutions can be agreed voluntarily. Same logic as Hirshleifer (1971): early information destroys insurable trades.

**(i) Honest caveat: correlated types.** If types are correlated and the designer knows the joint distribution exactly, Crémer and McLean (1988) show efficiency with voluntary participation is generically achievable through side-bets on others' reports. Agents with rich data make correlation plausible. But the construction needs a designer who knows $F$ precisely, can impose unboundedly large penalties, and it collapses under small misspecification (the motivation for robust mechanism design, Bergemann and Morris 2005). It strengthens the conclusion: it works only with a powerful, well-informed designer.

## 5. Communication and computation

**(a) Bits: Nisan and Segal (2006).** $m$ indivisible items, agents with arbitrary valuations over bundles. With two agents, any protocol that finds an efficient allocation, or even one that beats giving everything to one agent, must exchange at least $\binom{m}{m/2}\approx 2^{m}\sqrt{2/(\pi m)}$ bits in the worst case. With $N$ agents, beating the approximation ratio $1/N$ needs at least $\ln 2\cdot\exp\{m/(2N^{2})-2\ln N\}$ bits. The bound is on nondeterministic communication: it applies to merely verifying a proposed allocation, and equivalently to the size of the price system that supports it (one price per bundle per agent). This is Hayek's dispersed-knowledge problem as a theorem, and it binds decentralised agents and planner alike: it is a property of the allocation problem, not of the institution.
 Arithmetic to include: with a budget of $B$ bits the largest fully general problem solvable has $m\approx\log_2 B+\tfrac12\log_2(\pi m/2)$. A thousandfold fall in $\kappa$ buys about ten more items. Cheap communication moves the frontier linearly while "hyper-granular" contracting moves $m$ multiplicatively (each new distinction, weekday against weekend, doubles the rights in play).
 Fairness note: preferences separable across states of the world decompose state by state; the bound applies to the rights that interact within a state.

**(b) When simple prices exist.** One number per item supports an efficient allocation for every preference profile only when valuations are gross substitutes (Kelso and Crawford 1982; Gul and Stacchetti 1999). Define gross substitutes in a clause: raising the price of one item never lowers demand for another. Externality bargains are complements: a route needs every segment, a development needs every consent. Hurwicz (1960), Mount and Reiter (1974): the price mechanism is informationally minimal in *convex* environments, meaning preferences and production sets convex in the commodity bundle. Calsamiglia (1977): with increasing returns (a non-convex production set) no mechanism with a finite-dimensional message space is efficient. Starrett (above) says externalities put us in the non-convex case.

**(c) Operations.** Finding the welfare-maximising allocation from combinatorial bids ("winner determination") is NP-hard in the number of bids (Rothkopf, Pekeč and Harstad 1998) and cannot be approximated in polynomial time within $m^{1/2-\epsilon}$ in the number of items $m$ unless P = NP (Sandholm 2002; Lehmann, O'Callaghan and Shoham 2002, via Håstad 1999 and Zuckerman 2007). Say what this does and does not mean: worst case over all bid profiles; structured instances are routinely solved; the point is that no fall in $c$ closes the gap in general, because exact methods scale exponentially in $m$.

**(d) Incentives and computation compound.** VCG is truthful only if the allocation is exactly optimal; with an approximate optimiser it stops being truthful (Nisan and Ronen 2007). For submodular valuations (define: $v(S\cup T)+v(S\cap T)\le v(S)+v(T)$) a non-strategic algorithm reaches a $1-1/e$ fraction of optimal welfare (Vondrák 2008), but a truthful mechanism restricted to polynomially many value queries cannot beat $m^{1/2-\epsilon}$ (Dobzinski 2011; Dobzinski and Vondrák 2012). Flag that this gap is specific to the value-query model. One or two sentences only.

**(e) Coalitions.** Krier: coalitions form "in a nanosecond". Formation speed is not the constraint. $N$ agents have $2^N-1$ coalitions and a number of partitions (the Bell number $B_N$) that outgrows every exponential $a^N$. Sandholm, Larson, Andersson, Shehory and Tohmé (1999): any algorithm guaranteeing any bounded fraction of the best coalition structure must examine at least $2^{N-1}$ structures when coalition values are arbitrary. Stability: Aivazian and Callen (1981) give a three-party externality in which the core is empty (define core: the set of payoff divisions no coalition can improve on for its own members), so every agreement is undone by some coalition and Coasean bargaining cycles. Deciding whether the core is empty is NP-complete in compact representations (Conitzer and Sandholm 2006; Deng and Papadimitriou 1994). Faster agents find the blocking coalition faster; speed accelerates the cycle.
 And the "non-negotiable" collective price is a commitment; see §6(c).

**(f) Correlated equilibrium needs a correlator.** A correlated equilibrium (Aumann 1974) is defined relative to a signal source every player trusts, which privately recommends an action to each. Some correlated equilibrium can be found in polynomial time even in large compactly-described games, but finding the welfare-maximising one is NP-hard for most such classes (Papadimitriou and Roughgarden 2008). Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete (Daskalakis, Goldberg and Papadimitriou 2009; Chen, Deng and Teng 2009). Cryptography can replace the trusted source for the *correlation* (Dodis, Halevi and Rabin 2000), but not the *selection*: there are generally many correlated equilibria with different payoff divisions, and whoever designs the signal chooses among them.

**(g) Designing the mechanism is itself hard.** Conitzer and Sandholm (2002): given finite type spaces and a prior, finding the optimal deterministic mechanism is NP-complete, even with a single agent. The input, a prior over type profiles, has size $|\Theta|^N$.

**(h) Contracts.** A complete contingent contract over $k$ binary verifiable conditions is a function from $\{0,1\}^k$ to actions. There are $|A|^{2^k}$ such functions for action set $A$, so a typical one needs $2^k\log_2|A|$ bits to write; almost all Boolean functions on $k$ inputs need circuits of size at least $2^k/k$ (Shannon 1949). Contracts people actually want are far more compressible than typical, but the compression is where the dispute lives: a short contract is a rule plus an interpreter.
 Anderlini and Felli (1994): if a contract must be computable, some state-contingent agreements cannot be written at all. Rice's theorem: no algorithm decides non-trivial behavioural properties of arbitrary programs, so a contract language in which compliance is mechanically checkable must be a restricted one, or disputes need a judge with discretion. This bears on "near-instantaneous computational verification".
 Segal (1999): with $n$ possible trades of which one will turn out efficient, and renegotiation that cannot be prevented, the value of any long-term contract converges to that of no contract as $n\to\infty$. Greater granularity is larger $n$.
 Incomplete contracts leave residual control rights: someone decides what happens in the cases the contract does not cover (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart and Moore 1988, 1999). In a constitution the holder of residual control is the sovereign. Counter-argument to report fairly: Maskin and Tirole (1999) show indescribable contingencies are irrelevant if parties can foresee payoffs and can commit not to renegotiate. That commitment needs an enforcer outside the parties.
 Hadfield-Menell and Hadfield (2019) make the parallel point that instructing an AI agent is itself an incomplete contract. Recent evidence: Sajja et al. (2026, arXiv:2608.10475) find LLM agents reach efficient contracts when environmental uncertainty is low but "under high uncertainty, often fail to negotiate satisfiable, efficient, or mutually beneficial contracts", and frequently violate contracts for profit. Cite as early evidence, not as a permanent limit.

Whoever restricts the bidding or contract language to make (a)–(h) tractable decides whose preferences can be expressed. That is a constitutional choice with winners and losers.

## 6. Costs that rise when cognition gets cheap

**(a) Bargaining as a contest (our small model; flag the assumption).** Assumption: a party's share of a contested surplus $V$ increases with the computation it spends relative to its rivals. Evidence that relative agent quality moves outcomes: Zhu et al. (2025, arXiv:2506.00073) conclude agent-to-agent deal-making is "an inherently imbalanced game"; Vaccaro et al. (2025, arXiv:2503.06416), 180,000 automated negotiations. Tullock (1980) form: party $i$ buys $e_i$ units at price $c$ and gets share $e_i^{\,r}/\sum_j e_j^{\,r}$. Symmetric equilibrium for $r\le N/(N-1)$: $e^\ast=\dfrac{r(N-1)V}{N^{2}c}$, total spending
$$c\,N e^\ast = r\,\frac{N-1}{N}\,V,$$
which does not contain $c$. Halving the price of computation doubles the computation bought and leaves the dissipated share of surplus unchanged. With $r=1$ and two parties, half the surplus. $r$ is an empirical unknown; the claim is the invariance to $c$, not the level. So "$10,000 in legal fees … pennies" holds for the non-rival part of legal work (drafting, search) and fails for the rival part (out-arguing the other side). With binding budgets, shares follow budgets: the billionaire's phone is the same as mine, the inference budget is not. Prior statement of the inequality point to cite: Sharp et al. (2025, "Agentic Inequality", arXiv:2510.16853).

**(b) Over-complete contracts.** Tirole (2009): thinking about contingencies is costly, and part of what it buys is protection against, or advantage over, the counterparty; contracts can end up too complete, with cognition spent on rent transfer. Cheaper cognition means more of it.

**(c) Cheap commitment produces impasse.** A delegated agent with a fixed mandate is a commitment device (Schelling 1960). Ellingsen and Miettinen (2008), abstract: negotiators "can make binding commitments at a low positive cost"; "If commitment attempts succeed with probability $q<1$, the unique outcome that survives iterated strict dominance entails conflict with probability $q^{2}$." Agents make commitment cheap and reliable, pushing $q\to 1$ and the probability of impasse $q^{2}\to 1$. Krier's own example ends in the word "non-negotiable". (Use $\gamma$ for their commitment cost if it is mentioned, since $c$ is taken.) Petel (2025) raises the neighbouring problem of threats and extortion in agent bargaining; cite him there.

## 7. The democratic mode (short)

Who assigns an issue to the price mode or the vote mode is deciding the outcome; "seamlessly switch" hides a constitutional choice. Within the vote mode, Gibbard (1973) and Satterthwaite (1975): with three or more alternatives every rule that is not dictatorial can be manipulated by misreporting. Bartholdi, Tovey and Trick (1989) proposed computational hardness of manipulation as a shield. It was a worst-case shield to begin with, and it is the kind that cheap computation removes. Liquid democracy: Kahng, Mackenzie and Procaccia (2021) show local delegation rules can do worse than direct voting.

## 8. The regress, and what stops it

Each remedy above is a choice from a menu: entitlements, mechanism, permitted language, the correlating signal, a tax rate, the source of subsidy, the residual decision-maker. Different choices give different parties more. Selecting one by negotiation among informed self-interested agents with a veto is a many-party bargain under private information over a public decision: §4(b) applies to it directly. A negotiation about the rules of that negotiation has the same form. The sequence stops only at rules that were not chosen by unanimous bargaining: imposed, inherited, or adopted behind a veil that informed agents no longer have. Hurwicz (2008) poses this as "who will guard the guardians"; his answer is that the regress can stop if there are "intervenors", actors who uphold the rules for reasons other than payoff, or an arrangement in which self-interest happens to enforce the rules. He does not say it must fail; report him accurately. Aghion and Bolton (2003): when the social contract is incomplete, the ex ante optimal constitution is not unanimity; it lets a majority bind a minority. Krier stops the regress at jurisprudence plus improved democracy, which is a state. The question is what that state must be able to do and to want.

*Leviathan.* The powers the remedies require: to bind non-consenters (§4f), to tax (§4a, 4f), to assign and reassign entitlements (§4g), to forbid renegotiation (§5h), to fix the contract language (§5), to choose the equilibrium (§5f), to decide uncontracted cases (§5h). The guardrails in the essay already use them. A Harberger tax is a compulsory levy plus a forced sale at the declared price (Posner and Weyl 2017); it cannot be voluntary. "Any coalition that vetoes must then reimburse that quantified loss" is a liability rule in the sense of Calabresi and Melamed (1972): a third party sets the price of an entitlement and compels the transfer. "Verified emergencies" need a verifier. Agent vouchers need a tax base. So the essay's institutions are not voluntary in the sense its argument needs.

*Planner's knowledge.* Assigning workable entitlements, setting a Harberger rate (the efficient rate depends on turnover and investment elasticities), quantifying the diffuse loss, choosing the mechanism: each needs the distribution $F$ of preferences. The framework guarantor needs what Hayek said the planner lacks, in distributional form. What agents change is that $F$ may become cheaper to estimate. That helps the leviathan do its job; it does not remove the job.

*Benevolent.* The same powers held by a payoff-maximiser are used differently. Myerson (1981): a revenue-maximising seller trades only when the buyer's virtual value $b-\frac{1-F_b(b)}{f_b(b)}$ is non-negative, excluding efficient trades. In the uniform bilateral example a profit-maximising intermediary (Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983, their broker) trades only when $b-s\ge 1/2$: realised gains $1/12$, half of first best, against $27/32$ with no intermediary and all of it with a benevolent subsidiser. The agent platforms of Krier's second layer are candidate intermediaries. His answer is competition with low switching costs. That works where platforms are substitutes. A mechanism for a public decision has to include everyone affected; an airshed cannot be split between competing clearing-houses. Brennan and Buchanan's Leviathan and Acemoglu's (2003) commitment problem, which Krier cites, are the standing reasons not to assume benevolence. Transparency raises the cost of abuse; it does not change the objective.

So: the scheme needs a benevolent leviathan, public choice says we cannot count on one, and the scheme does not make one. The honest reading is that agent bargaining relocates the problem of good government and does not shrink it.

## 9. What survives

- Frictional costs do fall. Search, drafting, monitoring, routine verification.
- Thick two-sided markets in rival, excludable, substitutable goods: kerb space by the minute, electricity by the interval, delivery slots. Loss $O(1/N^2)$, simple prices exist. Krier is right here.
- Small-$N$ disputes: his own starting cases (office thermostat, flatmates). Bilateral loss is moderate (16% in the uniform example).
- Prediction that separates the views: holding technology fixed, success of agent bargaining should fall with (i) the number of parties whose consent a single decision needs, (ii) complementarity between the rights involved, (iii) the surplus at stake relative to compute cost, since contest spending scales with surplus. Krier's account predicts no such gradient.
- Agents may make the leviathan better informed and more auditable. That is a real gain and it is Krier's "automated governance" point. It is an argument for better planners.
- Note the difference from Petel (2025), "Price What You Can, Protect What You Must", who divides cases by whether a good can be priced without corrupting it, whether harms can be verified without surveillance, and wealth effects. The division here is mechanism-theoretic and orthogonal: rival versus public, substitutes versus complements, few versus many. Do not reprise Petel's arguments; cite him for them. Ngo (2026) objects from a different direction (efficiency reasoning erodes sociopolitical autonomy); one clause at most. Shahidi et al. (2025), "The Coasean Singularity?", is the sympathetic economic treatment and notes congestion and price obfuscation as new frictions.

## 10. Objections and limits (write these seriously)

1. Worst-case results. The hardness and communication bounds are over all instances. Real preferences have structure, and LLM agents are good heuristic solvers. Reply: agreed for §5(a)–(e); the article's weight rests on §4, which is not worst-case in that sense (Mailath–Postlewaite holds "under very mild conditions" on the distribution), and on the point that imposing structure is a constitutional act.
2. Independent private values and quasi-linear utility are assumptions of §4. Correlated types: §4(i). Interdependent values make things harder, not easier (Jehiel and Moldovanu 2001).
3. Repeated interaction and reputation. Folk theorems make efficient outcomes equilibria among many others; they do not select them. Program equilibrium (Tennenholtz 2004): agents that can read each other's code can sustain cooperation in one-shot games, a genuinely new capability; it enlarges the equilibrium set, so selection again.
4. Jackson and Wilkie (2005): free binding side-payment promises before play do not generally yield efficiency.
5. "Benevolent" is a strong word. The claim is about what efficiency requires, not that such an actor exists or that we should pretend one does. Second-best design with a non-benevolent sovereign is the real problem, and it is the old one.
6. The Tullock form is a modelling choice.
