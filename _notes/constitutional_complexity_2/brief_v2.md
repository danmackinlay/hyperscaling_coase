## Vocabulary (controlled; define each at first use; coin nothing else)

- *constitutional level* / *operational level*. Criterion to state: an operational outcome is the result of one negotiation (this truck pays this fee for this street today). A rule is constitutional when it fixes the terms of many later negotiations: who owes whom by default, how a bargaining club computes its offer, the formula for a fee, and how any of these may be changed. "Constitutional" is used in Buchanan & Tullock's sense and covers a neighbourhood's fee formula as much as a national constitution; say so early because the title invites the narrower reading.
- *status quo constitution* $s_0$: the entitlements, mechanisms and amendment rules in force when negotiation starts. Note that in existing democracies $s_0$ already contains a majoritarian amendment rule.
- *faithful agent*: maximizes its own principal's payoff.
- *granularity*: the number of distinguishable contingencies on which rules and contracts condition.
- *unit cost of cognition* $c$: the price of a unit of effective reasoning (drafting, modelling a counterparty, searching for objections, persuading). "Effective" matters: a more capable agent gets more done per unit of compute, so a capability difference between two sides is a difference in their unit costs $c_C \neq c_I$.
- *incumbent*: a party holding one or more of FOUR positions under $s_0$ (the first draft had three; the new one is the second):
  - *entitlement holder*: holds a baseline right; with it a veto over voluntary change and the benefits of $s_0$ for as long as negotiation lasts.
  - *organized party*: already constituted under $s_0$ as a single decision-maker with a single budget (a firm, an agent provider, an agency). Its stake in a rule is one stake. A diffuse group with the same total stake holds many small ones. A firm is in effect a standing coalition whose members are bound by existing company law; the residents would have to build theirs.
  - *drafter*: writes the text of proposed rules, protocols and standard forms.
  - *residual controller*: a party that decides what happens in contingencies the rules do not cover: owners of assets, providers of agents through their terms of service, and the courts and regulators who apply default law. (Parties only; do not list "the default rule of law" as a controller.)
- *challenger*: a party seeking a change from $s_0$.

## The claim, the benchmark, the scope (state all three in the Summary, plainly)

**Krier's two routes.** He offers two ways for the constitutional level to change. The *voluntary route*: agents leave the baseline in place and make it cheap to contest and renegotiate, with some rules hardening by mutual agreement and others staying perpetually negotiable. The *democratic route*: existing democratic systems amend entitlements, improved by agents through continuous deliberation and liquid delegation (handing one's vote on a topic to a trusted party, revocably). The article takes each route in turn. This two-part organisation is the spine of the second draft.

**Benchmark.** What Krier's argument needs at the constitutional level is that as $c \to 0$ outcomes come to depend on what each side has at stake and not on the position it occupies under $s_0$: on the voluntary route, every rule change that benefits all affected parties gets made; on the democratic route, each person's interest weighs the same whoever they are.

**"Favours".** Take two sides with equal total stakes in a rule and equally capable faithful agents; one side holds an incumbent position and the other does not. Delegated negotiation favours the incumbent if the position-holder gets the better outcome and the gap does not close as $c \to 0$.

**Claim.** On both routes the gap does not close. In three places the models say it widens, and the article labels the strength of each: (i) delegation turns political effort into compute bought at a linear price, which removes the property that lets a diffuse group match an organized party (a theorem in the model; the offsetting benefit of cheaper organizing is real and their relative size is unmeasured); (ii) the burden of auditing a drafted rule grows with granularity (a conjecture by analogy); (iii) explicit text loses force as granularity rises when renegotiation is cheap (an extrapolation from a bilateral model). Name these three once in the Summary and use the same three, in the same words, wherever they are referred to again. Do not use any other count of "results" or "channels" anywhere.

**Disagreement with Krier, stated without hedging.** We agree that non-voluntary institutions are needed; he says so. We disagree about what agents do to the constitutional level. He holds that agents make the baseline cheap to contest and materially improve the process that amends it. The article holds that on either route, who prevails tracks position under $s_0$ as much when cognition is nearly free as when it is expensive. Delete the first draft's closing remark that its conclusion is closer to Krier's stated position than to his rhetoric.

**Scope statements** (keep from the first draft): operational argument granted; no claim that present institutions do better; agents assumed capable, cheap and faithful, and no result depends on any agent being misaligned; cited results are theorems about models, not evidence about current agents, and the small experiments are reported separately.

### Drafting and auditing (drafter)
Order within the subsection: mechanism and the audit; why the audit has no general shortcut; the planted-structure result explained; who drafts; the transfer to rules, with the private-information analogue stated; the factory case; replies; the provider aside.

- Mechanism: a procedure that takes what the parties report and what happens in the world and returns an outcome (a clearing rule, a fee formula, an amendment procedure). For the computer scientist: the parties respond strategically to the mechanism, so what it does to a principal is not read off the text; it is what happens at an equilibrium of the game the text creates, a profile of strategies from which no party gains by deviating. To consent, a faithful agent must work that out. Call this the audit.
- No general shortcut (one short paragraph; these results now have a stated job, which is to show that the audit cannot be done by brute calculation and that they bind the drafter too). Finding a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete even with two players (gloss: PPAD is a class of search problems for which no efficient algorithm is known or expected) [Daskalakis, Goldberg & Papadimitriou (2009); Chen, Deng & Teng (2009)]. Krier appeals to correlated equilibrium, in which parties condition on a shared signal; one such equilibrium can be found efficiently in broad classes of games with many players whose payoffs can be written down compactly, but finding the best one, by total welfare or by any one party's payoff, is NP-hard in most of those classes (gloss NP-hard: no efficient algorithm is expected) [Papadimitriou & Roughgarden (2008)]. Designing an optimal deterministic mechanism is NP-complete [Conitzer & Sandholm (2002)]. These are worst-case results: they rule out a general method, not success on typical cases. And they are symmetric: the drafter cannot solve an arbitrary mechanism either. So a drafter's advantage, if there is one, must come from somewhere else. DROP the Dütting et al. and Nisan & Segal citations entirely.
- The planted-structure result, explained for the reader who knows no finance and the reader who knows no complexity theory. [Arora, Barak, Brunnermeier & Ge (2011)]: a seller pools many assets into many derivatives, each of which pays out according to a subset of the assets. The textbook view is that such structuring reduces the damage done by the seller's better information, because buyers can take the parts of the cash flow that are insensitive to it. The seller knows which assets are bad and chooses which assets go into which derivative. It can over-represent the bad assets in a few derivatives. Whether it has done so is visible only as an unusually dense cluster in the table of which derivative references which asset, and finding such a planted dense cluster in an otherwise random table is a problem conjectured to be intractable on average, that is for typical instances and not only contrived ones. So a buyer with the same computing power as the seller cannot tell a tampered product from a clean one; complexity makes the seller's information advantage worse where structuring was supposed to make it better. Then their factoring illustration, with the point made explicit: the author can value a payoff conditioned on a factor of a huge integer because the author built the integer from its factors.
  State what the result has two ingredients: private information held by the party who constructs the object, and a construction that hides the use of it from any efficient check.
- Who drafts (moved up, before the example). Anyone may draft, and a faithful agent on either side would use this advantage if it had it; the residents' agents can send a counter-proposal with structure of their own. Three things decide who drafts more of the text that ends up in force. Operators of protocols and providers of agents draft by default, because their protocol and their terms are the text everyone else's agents work within. And repeat players have more private information worth building in: [Galanter (1974)] observed that parties who recur in a legal system play for rules as well as for outcomes, because their stake in a rule is added up over many transactions while a one-shot party's stake is in one case. (Do not write "title thesis".)

The drafter does not need to solve an arbitrary mechanism: it starts from the outcome it wants in the contingencies it expects and builds a mechanism for which that outcome is an equilibrium, which is the easy direction, as building the integer from its factors is. The auditor is handed the mechanism and must find what it implies, which is the hard direction. ### Incompleteness and residual control (residual controller)
- Any constitution is silent on some contingencies. Two foundations that do not weaken as agents improve, one that does:
  - Describability: [Anderlini & Felli (1994)] treat a contract as an algorithm and show that requiring contracts to be computable can make the best contract impossible to write; [Al-Najjar, Anderlini & Felli (2006)] construct events the parties understand and yet cannot specify in any finite text. These concern what can be written, so capability does not remove them. Say plainly that these show only that gaps exist, not who benefits.
  - Complexity with renegotiation. Gloss hold-up first: a party that sinks an investment before terms are fixed gets only a share of its return in the bargaining that follows, and so under-invests. [Segal (1999)]: a buyer and a seller can describe $n$ possible trades in advance, only one of which will turn out to be the efficient one; they may write any long-term contract, including one that depends on messages they send later; they cannot stop themselves renegotiating. As $n$ grows, the outcome under any such contract converges to the outcome with no long-term contract at all, where the parties simply bargain once the efficient trade is known. [Hart & Moore (1999)] obtain the same in a related model (fold into this sentence; no separate sentence). [Maskin & Tirole (1999)] show the opposing result, that contingencies which cannot be described in advance need not matter if the parties can foresee their payoffs; Segal and Hart & Moore show that conclusion fails when renegotiation cannot be prevented and the environment is complex enough. Much of the disagreement in this literature turns on whether renegotiation can be prevented. (Do not call it "the dividing line".)
  - Costly thinking: [Tirole (2009)] derives incompleteness from the cost of thinking through contingencies. Say honestly that this foundation weakens as $c$ falls, unlike the other two. DROP the first draft's sentence that contracts can be "too complete" and its remark about rent-seeking cognition.
- Krier's design and Segal's conditions: cheap, continuous renegotiation (he describes governance moving from statute to something like a thermostat) and high granularity (prices conditioned on day of week, minutes of detour, hour of use) are the two conditions of Segal's limit: renegotiation that cannot be prevented, and many distinguishable contingencies. State the label WITH the claim: if the result carries over from Segal's two-party model to many-party rule-making, which is an extrapolation and not a corollary, explicit long-term text loses force as granularity rises (worsening-channel (iii)).
- The missing step, now supplied: when the text is silent or has lost its force, the outcome is settled by bargaining after the fact, and each side's share in that bargaining depends on what it can do without the other's agreement. [Grossman & Hart (1986)] and [Hart & Moore (1990)] define ownership of an asset as the right to decide its use in every contingency no contract covers, and show that this right, called residual control, sets those fallback positions and hence the division.
- Who holds residual control in Krier's architecture, by his three layers (already introduced in the Summary): owners of assets, since he takes existing entitlements as the starting point; the agent's provider, since the agent acts under terms of service the user accepts and the provider's training and terms settle whatever the user's instructions leave open. Third, courts and regulators applying default law.

### Costly negotiation (entitlement holder)
- Distinguish two kinds of cost at the outset, because the democratic-route half will say the level of $c$ does not matter and this subsection says a small positive cost matters a great deal: $c$ is the price of a unit of cognition, of which each side chooses how much to buy; a participation cost is a fixed amount each side must spend for a round of bargaining to take place at all (for an agent, the least work needed to read and assess a proposal). The second is positive whenever $c$ is.
- [Anderlini & Felli (2006)]: when parties must sink costs before a Coasean negotiation can take place, a hold-up problem arises (the party that pays them recovers only a share in the bargaining), so some mutually beneficial negotiations never start; and there is no Coasean cure, because negotiating over who should bear those costs is a new negotiation with prior costs of its own. Implication stated: some rule changes that would benefit everyone are never negotiated, and $s_0$ stays.
- [Lee & Sabourian (2007)], explained. Setting: two players bargain by alternating offers over a surplus that recurs every period; in every period without agreement they play an underlying game whose payoffs they receive meanwhile (in our setting: they go on dealing with each other under $s_0$). Strategies are carried out by finite-state machines (automata), and a strategy's complexity is the number of states its machine needs. Players care first about payoffs and, between strategies that pay the same, prefer the simpler. Results as stated by the authors: without transaction costs, this preference eliminates the inefficient equilibria, because the strategies that sustain delay are more complicated than they need to be, and the Coase theorem holds; with any positive cost of taking part in a round of bargaining (the cost structure of [Anderlini & Felli (2001)]), every equilibrium has perpetual disagreement; the same is true in the plain alternating-offers model of [Rubinstein (1982)] once such a cost is added. Then one sentence of intuition, marked as my gloss and not the authors' proof (I worked from the published abstract and the companion working paper, not the full proof): never paying to bargain is about the simplest strategy there is, and against a counterpart who plays it, paying is wasted.
- What it does and does not establish: a discontinuity at zero (the outcome at a tiny positive cost is not near the outcome at zero cost), so "small costs, therefore nearly Coasean outcomes" is not a safe inference once complexity is in the model. It is one two-player model with a specific form of simplicity preference; not evidence about deployed agents; I do not use it to predict deadlock.
- Relation to Krier, corrected (the first draft claimed a close match; the cold reader pointed out that Krier's remark predicts stability by agreement). Krier expects agents to settle on stable arrangements so as to save computation. In this model the arrangement that takes least computation to sustain is the one already in force. DROP the wordplay about "asymptotically, which is to say positive".

### Proposal power (drafter)
