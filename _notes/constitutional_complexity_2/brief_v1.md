## Thesis and scope

Krier's argument has two levels (the distinction is [Buchanan & Tullock (1962)](https://doi.org/10.3998/mpub.7687)).
At the operational level agents price externalities inside given rules.
He concedes that efficiency there does not settle distribution: who starts with which entitlement decides who ends up richer, and he calls that "a constitutional choice, not a technicality".
He then applies the same technology one level up: agents leave the baseline in place but make it explicit and cheap to contest and renegotiate; existing jurisprudence is the starting point; democratic systems amend it, improved by agents doing continuous deliberation and liquid delegation; some rules are left to harden by mutual agreement while others stay perpetually negotiable; and he expects agents to settle on stable arrangements because constant renegotiation costs computation.
His anti-holdout devices live at this level too: voluntary bargaining clubs with a single take-it-or-leave-it offer, fading vote weight or a Harberger-style fee for chronic over-bidders, and a requirement that a vetoing coalition reimburse the diffuse losers.

The article grants the operational argument in full and asks what happens at the constitutional level when faithful agents negotiate the rules for their principals.
Claim: there, a falling unit cost of cognition does not erode the advantage of incumbents, and in two channels the advantage grows with delegation and granularity.

Scope statements to make explicitly, early:
- Not claimed: that operational gains are illusory, or that present institutions do better. The claim is non-convergence to the frictionless benchmark Krier invokes, plus two named channels that get worse.
- The agents are assumed as good as Krier hopes: capable, cheap, perfectly aligned with their own principal. None of the argument relies on misaligned or incompetent agents (one subsection notes that the alignment assumption is itself unauditable, but the main results do not need that).
- The results cited are theorems about models; they are not evidence about what current language-model agents do. Keep that bridge explicit wherever a theorem is used, and report the small amount of experimental evidence separately.

Definition of incumbent (by position, not identity).
A party is an incumbent to the extent it occupies one or more of three positions under $s_0$:
*entitlement holder* (holds a baseline right, hence a veto over any voluntary change and the flow of benefits while negotiation continues);
*drafter* (writes the text of proposed rules, protocols and standard forms);
*residual controller* (decides what happens in contingencies the rules do not cover: owners of assets, providers of the agents through their terms of service, and the default rule of law).
"Favours" means: the incumbent's share of the gains from constitutional change does not fall towards the frictionless benchmark as $c \to 0$.

Numbered summary for the top of the article (rephrase, keep the content):
1. Voluntary amendment is unanimity rule, with $s_0$ as the disagreement point; the anti-holdout devices are not voluntary, so the proposal needs a non-voluntary constitutional process and the question is how agents behave in it.
2. A uniform fall in the unit cost of cognition leaves the shares in an adversarial negotiation unchanged and the resources burnt unchanged. What moves shares is relative cost and concentration of stakes. Buying effort as compute at a market price removes the convexity of individual effort costs, which is the property that lets large diffuse groups match concentrated ones.
3. Writing a rule is computationally easier than auditing it. The advantage belongs to the drafter, holds between parties with equal compute, and grows with granularity.
4. A constitution for a high-granularity world is incomplete, and with cheap renegotiation the explicit text matters less as granularity rises; outcomes in the gaps go to the residual controller.
5. With any positive cost per round of negotiation and a preference for simple strategies, the one formal model that puts complexity into the Coase theorem predicts perpetual disagreement, which here means $s_0$ persists.

### 2. Voluntary amendment is unanimity
- A constitution here has three parts: baseline entitlements; admissible mechanisms (the clearing rule of a bargaining club, a Harberger rate, vote weights); the amendment rule (which rules can be renegotiated, by whom, how).
- Buchanan & Tullock: the choice of decision rule trades decision costs against the costs a decision imposes on dissenters; as decision costs fall the optimal rule moves towards unanimity. Krier's argument is this logic with agents driving decision costs down.
- [Rae (1975)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1955287): unanimity is not neutral. It privileges whatever is already in place; it protects everyone only if staying at the status quo counts as imposing no cost on anyone.
- In bargaining terms, under voluntary change $s_0$ is the disagreement point ([Nash (1950)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1907266); [Binmore, Rubinstein & Wolinsky (1986)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2555382)), so every reachable outcome leaves each entitlement holder at least as well off as under $s_0$. Incumbent rents are a floor by construction. This is not a discovery; it is the baseline the rest builds on.
- Krier's anti-holdout devices (fading vote weight, Harberger fee, reimbursement by vetoing coalitions) each remove or tax a veto. They cannot be adopted voluntarily by the holders they bind, so they presuppose a prior non-unanimous decision. He says as much: democratic systems are the mechanism, to be improved by agents.
- [Aghion & Bolton (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1162/154247603322256765) give the precise hinge: behind a veil of ignorance, some majority rule beats unanimity whenever compensating transfers carry deadweight costs; the optimal rule trades minority protection against flexibility. So if agents made compensating transfers costless at the constitutional level, unanimity (pure voluntary bargaining) would be fine. Everything turns on whether the costs of negotiating and compensating at the constitutional level go to zero with $c$. The next four sections argue that the relevant costs do not.

### 3. Contests over rules
Model. A proposed rule change is worth $v_C$ to a challenger and its defeat is worth $v_I$ to an incumbent. Each buys cognition $x_C, x_I$ (drafting proposals, modelling the other side, searching for objections, persuading third parties' agents) at unit costs $c_C, c_I$. The challenger prevails with probability $p_C = x_C/(x_C + x_I)$, the contest success function of [Tullock (1980)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4757-5055-3_2); any success function homogeneous of degree zero in efforts behaves the same way for this purpose ([Skaperdas (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf01213906) axiomatizes the family). A deterministic division of surplus in proportion $p_C$ gives the same algebra.

Derivation (show it). First-order conditions $v_C x_I/(x_C+x_I)^2 = c_C$ and $v_I x_C/(x_C+x_I)^2 = c_I$. Write $\theta_i = v_i/c_i$. Then
$$p_C = \frac{\theta_C}{\theta_C + \theta_I}, \qquad c_C x_C + c_I x_I = (v_C + v_I)\, p_C (1 - p_C).$$
With equal unit costs $c$: $p_C = v_C/(v_C+v_I)$ and total spend $v_C v_I/(v_C+v_I)$, neither of which contains $c$. Effort in physical units is $x_C + x_I = \theta_C\theta_I/(\theta_C+\theta_I)$, which scales as $1/c$.

Reading. Krier's cost argument (what costs ten thousand dollars in legal fees today may cost pennies of compute; a billionaire's phone is no better than anyone else's) is a statement about the level of $c$. In an adversarial setting the level cancels. Both the split and the resources burnt are homogeneous of degree zero in $(c_C, c_I)$: only the ratio matters, and the claim that costs approach zero says nothing about the ratio. His own remark that constant renegotiation is expensive even for AGI is what the model predicts: spending is pinned by the stakes, not by the unit cost.
Related: [Tirole (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.99.1.265) models thinking about contingencies as costly and shows that part of that thinking is rent-seeking (finding where one could be held up or could hold up the other), so contracts can be too complete relative to the social optimum; cheaper cognition buys more of the adversarial part as well. [Anderlini & Felli (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0297.2006.01054.x): if parties must sink costs before a Coasean negotiation can take place, a hold-up problem arises, and it has no Coasean remedy, because negotiating over who pays those costs is a new negotiation with its own prior costs. That regress is the constitutional regress in miniature.

Evidence on the ratio (label clearly as small experiments on current models, operational not constitutional bargaining). Anthropic's Project Deal ([Troy et al. (2026)](https://www.anthropic.com/features/project-deal)): 69 employees, agents trading real items; in runs mixing a stronger and a weaker model, the stronger model's users closed about two more deals each and sold the same items for a few dollars more on average, and the participants' fairness ratings were indistinguishable between the two groups, so the people on the losing side did not perceive it. [Zhu et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00073) report that agent-to-agent consumer negotiation is an imbalanced game in which different models obtain significantly different outcomes for their users. So capability ratios do move surplus, and competition between providers disciplines only the differences users can perceive.

#### Subsection: Numbers and the cost of effort
Setting. Krier's example: ten thousand residents facing a factory. Let a rule be worth $v$ in aggregate to $n$ residents, $v/n$ each, and $v$ to one concentrated incumbent. The rule is a public good among the residents. Residents contribute effort $a_i$, $A = \sum a_i$, each at cost $a^{1+\alpha}/(1+\alpha)$ with $\alpha \ge 0$; the incumbent contributes $B$ at unit cost 1; the residents prevail with probability $A/(A+B)$.

Derivation. Resident: $(v/n)\,B/(A+B)^2 = a^{\alpha}$. Incumbent: $v\,A/(A+B)^2 = 1$.
- Linear cost, $\alpha = 0$ (effort bought at a market price): dividing the two conditions gives $B = nA$, so the residents prevail with probability $1/(n+1)$. This is the result of [Katz, Nitzan & Rosenberg (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00139290) that a group contesting a public good exerts the effort of a single member's valuation, restated with the aggregate stake held fixed. For $n$ = 10,000 it is about one in ten thousand, against a factory whose stake is no larger than theirs in aggregate.
- Quadratic cost, $\alpha = 1$: substituting gives $B = A^2$ and $A(1+A)^2 = v$, which does not contain $n$. A diffuse group does as well as a single party with the same aggregate stake.
- General: $A = n^{1 - 1/\alpha}\,\bigl(v B/(A+B)^2\bigr)^{1/\alpha}$, so the direct effect of group size on group effort has exponent $1 - 1/\alpha$: negative below $\alpha = 1$, zero at 1, positive above. This is the threshold in [Esteban & Ray (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0003055401003124): the group-size paradox of [Olson (1965)](https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674041660) is reversed when the marginal cost of effort rises fast enough.

Reading. Human political effort has steeply convex individual cost (an hour of attention from each of ten thousand people is cheap for each; ten thousand hours from one person is not), and that convexity is what lets numbers offset concentration. Delegation replaces personal effort with compute bought at a price, which is linear. The transaction-cost story says agents help diffuse groups because finding each other becomes cheap. The contest model says the same substitution removes the property that made diffuse effort cheap in aggregate. The two effects have opposite sign and I know of no measurement of their relative size; state that plainly.

Flash coalitions. Krier's answer is that residents' agents form a coalition instantly and name one price. That needs each member bound to contribute or to accept the common position. Search and communication costs prevented this among humans, and agents remove those. Free-riding is a different obstacle: it is an incentive constraint, not a transaction cost. [Mailath & Postlewaite (1990)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2298018): with private valuations and voluntary participation, the probability that any mechanism provides a public good goes to zero as the number of agents grows; [Rob (1989)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0531(89)90022-7) shows the same for residents compensated for a polluting plant, which is Krier's case. [Dixit & Olson (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0047-2727(99)00089-4): even with complete information, if joining the bargain is voluntary, the Coasean outcome fails because each party prefers that the others strike the deal. These results assume zero communication cost; cheaper cognition does not relax them. A faithful agent is built to pursue its own principal's payoff, and free-riding is in that payoff. Conjecture, labelled as such: human collective action leans on motives that are not instrumental (norms, identity, anger), and whether a delegate reproduces them is a design decision that Krier leaves open in a neighbouring passage (whether agents should correct or faithfully represent their principals' psychological quirks).

Vouchers. Krier proposes compute credits. Two forms, with different effects in the model.
- A fungible allocation that people spend as they see fit is income; the opportunity cost of a unit spent on a constitutional contest is still its market price, cost stays linear, and the $1/(n+1)$ result stands.
- An earmarked, non-transferable quota $q$ per person for civic contests makes individual cost zero up to $q$, which is convex again. If each resident uses the quota, $A = nq$; the incumbent's best response gives $A + B = \sqrt{vA}$, so the residents prevail with probability
$$p = \sqrt{nq/v} \quad (nq < v).$$
Even odds need $nq = v/4$: a public voucher budget of a quarter of the stake, for each contested rule. That is not a modest fixed civic outlay; it scales with what is at stake in each constitutional question, and someone must decide which contests qualify and how large each quota is. Krier himself observes that earmarking by category recreates a planner. The model says the fungible alternative he offers to avoid that does not work for this purpose. (Members do not top up beyond the quota unless quotas are negligible; a footnote may note the condition $nq > v/(n+1)^2$.)
Also cite [Acemoglu & Robinson (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.98.1.267): a change in de jure power can be offset by increased investment in de facto power by those who lose from it; the contest model is one concrete mechanism. Krier already engages [Acemoglu (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jce.2003.09.003) on commitment by the sovereign; this is the companion point about elites.

### 4. Drafting and auditing
- At the constitutional level the object of negotiation is a mechanism: a program from reports and states to outcomes (a clearing rule, a fee schedule, a pricing protocol, an amendment procedure). To consent on a principal's behalf an agent must work out what the mechanism does to that principal in equilibrium across contingencies.
- Worst-case hardness of that question. Computing a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete, even with two players ([Daskalakis, Goldberg & Papadimitriou (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1137/070699652); [Chen, Deng & Teng (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1516512.1516516)). Krier appeals to correlated equilibrium, and one such equilibrium can be found in polynomial time in broad classes of compactly described games; but finding the best one, by welfare or by any party's payoff, is NP-hard in most of those classes ([Papadimitriou & Roughgarden (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1379759.1379762)). Some coordinated outcome is cheap; which one is a distributional question and the expensive part, and whoever proposes the coordinating signal chooses. Designing an optimal mechanism is NP-complete in general ([Conitzer & Sandholm (2002)](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0205075)); computing near-optimal contracts in compactly described principal–agent problems is hard even to approximate when the agent has many actions ([Dütting, Roughgarden & Talgam-Cohen (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1137/20m132153x)). Efficient allocation with general preferences over bundles needs communication exponential in the number of items, whatever the computing power ([Nisan & Segal (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jet.2004.10.007)); that one is a bound on messages, not computation, so no improvement in agents removes it.
- Caveat to state up front: these are worst-case results. They limit what can be guaranteed, not what typically happens, and heuristic solvers often do well on natural instances.
- The result that matters more is adversarial and average-case. [Arora, Barak, Brunnermeier & Ge (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1941487.1941511): the seller who assembles a set of derivatives can concentrate bad assets in a pattern such that detecting the tampering is as hard as finding a planted dense subgraph, conjectured intractable on average. A buyer with the same computing resources cannot price the product; complexity amplifies the cost of asymmetric information where the usual argument said structuring reduces it. Their illustration (paraphrase): a payoff conditioned on a factor of a very large integer is trivial for its author to value and impossible for the most sophisticated buyer.
- Transfer to rules. A drafter who builds a rule around structure it knows (an equilibrium it constructed, a clause that binds only in a contingency it engineered) holds a trapdoor. The asymmetry is between construction and audit. It therefore holds between parties with identical compute, which is the case the billionaire's-phone argument was meant to settle, and it grows with the description length of the rule, that is with granularity. This transfer is an argument by analogy from financial contracts to mechanisms; say so, and say that I know of no measurement of how often negotiated rules fall in the hard regime.
- The delegate itself. [Goldwasser, Kim, Vaikuntanathan & Zamir (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06974): whoever trains a model can plant a backdoor that no computationally bounded observer can detect; in one construction this holds even with full access to the weights and training data. So a principal cannot in general verify the faithful agent it was supplied; the provider is the drafter of the agent. Krier's second layer relies on competition to expose agents that serve someone else; exposure is what this result says cannot be guaranteed, and Project Deal suggests users do not notice even unconcealed differences. (This is the one place the alignment assumption is questioned; the other sections do not need it.)
- Who drafts. [Galanter (1974)](https://doi.org/10.2307/3053023): repeat players in a legal system play for rules, not only for outcomes; they write the standard forms and choose which disputes become precedent, because their stake in the rule is aggregated over many transactions. The contest section says who can afford the fixed cost of drafting: concentrated stakes. Add protocol operators and agent providers, who draft by default.
- Empirical trace, labelled as a lab finding in consumer search on current models: [Bansal et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25779) find that all tested models show a strong first-proposal bias, giving speed of response a ten- to thirty-fold advantage over quality. If anything like it carries over to rule proposals, the advantage goes to whoever is placed to propose first.
- Replies. (a) Proofs: a drafter can attach a zero-knowledge or formal proof of a stated property. But the property "this rule does not disadvantage my principal in any contingency" must first be written down, which is the incompleteness problem of the next section; and whether proofs are offered depends on the counterparty's leverage to insist, which is its outside option, $s_0$. (b) Simplicity: the rational defence against text one cannot audit is to refuse it and demand simple standard rules, as Arora et al. suggest for financial products and as the literature on shrouded contract terms implies ([Gabaix & Laibson (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.2006.121.2.505)); [Kaplow (1992)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1372840) is the standard treatment of when simple rules beat tailored standards. Simple standard rules are the coarse one-size-fits-all rules the proposal set out to replace. So at the constitutional level there is a trade-off between granularity and auditability: the rules stay coarse, or they favour the drafter.

### 5. Incompleteness and residual control
- Any constitution is silent on some contingencies. Three foundations, each of which survives capable agents:
  1. Describability. [Anderlini & Felli (1994)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2118357) model a contract as an algorithm and show that the requirement that contracts be computable can make the first-best contract unwritable; [Al-Najjar, Anderlini & Felli (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-937x.2006.00399.x) construct events that are understood by the parties yet cannot be described in finite terms. These constraints apply to any algorithmic drafter.
  2. Complexity with renegotiation. [Segal (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-937x.00078): a hold-up model in which only one of $n$ potential trades will be efficient ex post and renegotiation cannot be prevented; as $n \to \infty$ the outcome under any message-contingent long-term contract converges to the incomplete-contract outcome in which nothing about trade could be fixed ex ante. [Hart & Moore (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-937x.00080) reach a similar conclusion. [Maskin & Tirole (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-937x.00079) show the other side: indescribable contingencies need not matter if the parties can commit not to renegotiate. The dividing line in this literature is renegotiation.
  3. Costly cognition: Tirole (2009) and Anderlini & Felli (2006), already cited.
- Krier's design sits on the Segal side on both counts. Cheap, continuous renegotiation is the selling point (he describes governance moving from statute to something like a thermostat), and granularity is the other selling point (prices conditioned on day of week, minutes of detour, hour of use). In Segal's limit the explicit long-term text loses its force exactly as the number of distinguishable contingencies grows. Label this as extrapolation: Segal's model is bilateral and specific; the multilateral constitutional case is an analogy, not a corollary.
- What decides outcomes where the text is silent: residual control rights ([Grossman & Hart (1986)](https://doi.org/10.1086/261404); [Hart & Moore (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1086/261729)). The holder has the bargaining leverage in every uncontracted contingency.
- Who holds residual control in Krier's architecture, using his own three nested layers: existing owners, since he takes the entitlements accumulated by existing jurisprudence as the starting point; the agent's provider, since within the law the agent acts under terms of service the user accepts, and the provider fills every gap in the mandate ([Hadfield-Menell & Hadfield (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3306618.3314250) treat alignment as an incomplete contract whose gaps are filled by external structure; here the structure is the provider's); and the state.
- Conclusion of the section: higher granularity plus cheap renegotiation means a larger share of outcomes is settled by default ownership and a smaller share by negotiated text. That runs opposite to the intuition that finer contracting displaces defaults.

### 6. Costly negotiation
- [Lee & Sabourian (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jet.2006.03.014) put complexity into the Coase theorem directly. Players care first about payoffs and, between strategies with equal payoff, prefer the simpler one (complexity measured on automata). In negotiation games with a repeated surplus and endogenous disagreement payoffs, the Coase theorem holds if and only if transaction costs are zero: without them complexity considerations select only efficient equilibria; with any positive cost of taking part in a round of bargaining (the cost structure of [Anderlini & Felli (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00196)) every equilibrium has perpetual disagreement. The same holds in the Rubinstein alternating-offers model with such costs ([Rubinstein (1982)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1912531) is the frictionless baseline).
- The result is a discontinuity at zero: the outcome at a very small positive cost is not close to the outcome at zero cost. Krier's two premises are that costs approach zero asymptotically (so are positive) and that agents will settle on stable arrangements to save computation (a preference for simple strategies). Under those premises this model predicts no agreement, and at the constitutional level no agreement means $s_0$.
- Bridge, explicit: it is one model, two players, with a lexicographic taste for simplicity; it is not evidence about deployed agents. Its use here is to show that "small costs give nearly Coasean outcomes" is not a safe inference once complexity is in the model.
- While negotiation continues the entitlement holder collects the flow of benefits under $s_0$, so delay costs the challenger and pays the incumbent; and by the drafting section each proposal is costly to audit, so a respondent can lengthen the process cheaply.

### 7. Who the incumbents are
- The three positions tend to be held by the same parties. Entitlement holders have the veto and the flow. Drafters are those with stakes concentrated enough to pay for drafting, plus protocol operators. Residual controllers are owners and providers. A large agent provider holds all three at once: it owns the infrastructure, writes the protocol and terms, and fills the gaps.
- Galanter's title thesis was that the "haves" come out ahead through structural position, not better lawyers alone. Krier's proposal equalizes the lawyers. The four results say the advantage did not rest only on that: it rested on stake concentration, drafting position, residual rights and the flow from the status quo.
- State the conclusion at its warranted strength: *voluntary* delegated constitutional negotiation favours incumbents; the remedies visible in the models (binding coalitions, earmarked quotas scaled to stakes, mandated simplicity, non-unanimous amendment) are all non-voluntary and all need an authority to set them. That is closer to Krier's own hybrid position than to his rhetoric about leaving the planner behind, and the article should say so fairly.

### 8. Objections
Short paragraphs, each stating the objection at full strength first.
- Worst-case hardness rarely matters in practice. Fair for the PPAD and NP results. The planted-structure results concern instances chosen by an interested party, and constitutional drafting by an interested party is that situation. How often it happens is an empirical question.
- Democratic mode with equal vote weight. Krier separates willingness-to-pay bargaining from equal-weight democratic decisions and says agents switch between them by issue. Equal votes do not equalize drafting or agenda: in [Romer & Rosenthal (1978)](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf03187594) an agenda setter facing voters whose fallback is the status quo extracts more the worse that fallback is for them, and in [Baron & Ferejohn (1989)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1961664) the proposer takes a disproportionate share. And the assignment of issues to modes is itself a constitutional choice.
- Cooperative rather than faithful agents; conditional commitments. Agents that can read or verify each other's commitments can sustain cooperation ([Tennenholtz (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2004.02.002); [Kalai, Kalai, Lehrer & Samet (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2009.09.008)). These are folk theorems: they make a large set of outcomes supportable, $s_0$ among them, and do not say which is selected. An agent built to cooperate against its principal's narrow interest is no longer the fiduciary of the essay, and someone sets its norms.
- Incumbents dominate today as well. Agreed, and outside the claim; the claim is non-convergence plus the two channels (linear effort costs, audit burden growing with granularity).
- Coalitions large enough become concentrated players. Yes; if membership binds, the contest result flips in their favour. Binding membership is the non-voluntary element.

### 9. Tests
Things that would change my mind, as literal TODO items or a short paragraph each:
- In agent-negotiation experiments of the Project Deal kind, shares should be invariant to a uniform capability upgrade on both sides and sensitive to the capability ratio. A finding that uniform upgrades compress outcome gaps would count against the contest section.
- Flash-coalition experiments with privately valued stakes and faithful-advocate instructions: contribution per member should fall as $n$ grows. Sustained full contribution at large $n$ without binding rules would count against the free-riding argument.
- Audit experiments: plant an advantage in a long mechanism specification and measure detection by an equal-capability auditor as a function of length.
- Vary a small per-round cost in repeated agent bargaining over rules and look for the discontinuity.
