# hyperscaling_coase — what the red-team found, in the river's terms

Written 2026-10-15, revised the same day after a readability test, for a reader who has read the current draft of `notebook/hyperscaling_coase.qmd` and nothing else. Every paragraph under "The findings" is meant to stand alone: it quotes the sentence of the essay it is about, says what is wrong in plain words, runs the point on the river's numbers where numbers exist, defines each technical term in the sentence that uses it, and ends with what the essay should do. The river's symbols are used as the essay uses them: $\pi$ for the factory's profit from polluting, $h_i$ for resident $i$'s harm, $k$ for the number of consents the factory needs, $n$ for the number of residents, $m$ for the number of clauses a contract can carry, and $L$ for the strategic loss in the decomposition section. The river's numbers throughout are the essay's: harms $(2,3,9)$, profit $10$ or $20$, and in the weighted example frontages $(1,1,3)$ with a quota of $4$.

The findings come from five readers who were asked to attack the essay's argument and who did not see one another's work. They are referred to below by their briefs: **the economist**, briefed on contest theory, bargaining theory and political economy; **the cooperative game theorist**, briefed on cores, values and coalition formation, who checked the river's arithmetic by linear programme; **the complexity theorist**, briefed on communication and computational bounds; **the advocate**, briefed to argue Krier's side and who read Krier's essay and the Shahidi chapter in full; and **the cold reader**, who was given the essay alone and no brief. Where a finding says "two readers", it means two who reached it independently. Their full reports are in `hyperscaling_coase_redteam.md`, which does not need to be read to follow this. Provenance notes are in HTML comments and can be ignored.

Some paragraphs of the essay were added or rewritten in the revision of 2026-10-13; where it matters, they are called "the paragraph added in this revision" and located by quotation.

## A thesis for the rebuilt essay, proposed, not yet approved

<!-- The direction four of five readers described unprompted. It has not itself been attacked. Revised after the readability test to say "commitment" where the first version said "binds someone who did not agree", because two of the three fixes bind nobody who did not agree. -->

Krier's claim is that AI agents cut the costs of bargaining so far that private deals can do the work of regulation. On the river, the costs that agents cut are real and they fall: finding each other, drafting, monitoring, and the arithmetic of who is owed what, which turns out to be cheap at every size in the essay, including its ten thousand residents. What does not fall is the one thing the river's three failures have in common. At $k=1$ the factory can buy one signature before the residents have combined; at $k=0$ each resident waits for the others to pay; at $k=n$ a resident paid to overstate a harm will. Each has a fix, and every fix binds somebody against a later temptation: a pact among the residents that the factory cannot break into while it forms, an escrow that cannot hand over the money until all of it is raised, a rule adopted before harms are known that cannot be declined once they are, or consent that must be asked for again each period rather than bought once. The fix is commitment, and enforcing commitments is what the courts Krier keeps in the background are for. Cheap cognition's effect on who gets what therefore runs through commitment and through information rather than through counting or through the number of operations a side can afford. Frequent offers favour the side that holds private information, which on the river is the residents, because an offerer who cannot bind itself loses its edge as offers come faster. Cheaper screening, meaning cheaper inference about what each resident will settle for, favours whoever makes the offer. Cheap, verifiable commitment turns bargaining into a race to commit first, and in that race the speed that compute buys matters, as a ratio of the two sides' speeds that uniform cheapening leaves unchanged. A better model is a separate matter from more operations: it knows more, and what it knows is information, which is the other channel. Cheap observation of what the other residents have signed lets the residents hold a refusal together. Which of these wins is not decided by the price of compute. It is decided by who can bind whom, and that is the question the rebuilt essay is about.

## The findings, in the order of the current essay

### The intro's three-way answer

The intro's paragraph added in this revision, beginning "How cheap, then, does bargaining need to be?", says that part of the bill for a bargain "comes from complexity, a count of operations and messages", that part "is a contest, in which cheaper compute lets both sides burn more of the surplus", and that part "is a floor, surplus lost even with messaging free and nobody computing, which no price of compute touches". All five readers reject the first two parts on the essay's own model, and four reject the way the third is put. The count, as the findings below on the clause count, on the pick-off and on exact play show, is never the thing that limits a bargain on the river at any size the essay names; what remains of it is the extra communication that a mechanism needs to make everyone honest, which is a strategic cost and is described in the clause-count finding. The contest, as the finding on the pick-off shows, is one in which cheaper compute does not increase the surplus burnt, and in the one case the essay works out, the pick-off, which is the essay's name for the firm making offers to the residents who have underestimated their own bargaining position, cheaper compute shrinks both the money spent and what the firm takes. The floor is real, but it is described as untouched by compute, and of the three failures it contains, the two at $k=1$ and $k=0$ are fixed by a pact and an escrow among the residents, contracts that agents make cheap, and the one at $k=n$ is escaped by a rule adopted before harms are known. The paragraph should be replaced by the thesis above or something like it, and the frame of count, contest and floor goes with it.

### The river at $k=1$ and $k=0$: the pact the residents were never allowed to sign

In "Someone pollutes our hypothetical river", the $k=1$ paragraph says "We residents could have refused together but could not refuse apart", and the paragraph added in this revision after it says "the *core*, the set of divisions no coalition would walk out of, is empty". The arithmetic holds. The cooperative game theorist verified by linear programme that the core is empty at $k=1$ whenever any single consent is worth buying, that is whenever $\pi$ exceeds the smallest harm, which covers both $\pi=10$ and $\pi=20$; and it stays empty under every standard way of defining what a group can guarantee itself when a deal among some parties harms the others. The lesson drawn from it is what two readers, the cold reader and the advocate, object to independently. The residents were never allowed the one contract that would let them refuse together: a pact among themselves that binds only if all three sign. With that pact available, the factory's token payment to any one resident fails, because signing the pact is at least as good for each resident as being the one who sells out, and the river stays clean, welfare $0$ instead of $-4$. The same device at $k=0$ is an escrow: each resident pays in, and the money goes to the factory only if the full $10$ is raised, otherwise it is refunded. Under that rule contributing is safe, and the deal the essay says collapses closes; Bagnoli and Lipman 1989 is the general result, that with a refund rule the contributions that fund the good are an equilibrium when everyone knows everyone's harm, and Krier's own proposal for this case, which the advocate reports from his essay, is an escrow run by the agents. There is also a route with no pact at all, which the economist supplies: if consent is asked for every period rather than sold once, the residents' joint refusal is self-enforcing, meaning no resident gains by breaking it, provided each values next period's outcome at least $0.8$ as much as this period's, the figure being $1-h_1/\pi$ at $\pi=10$ for the resident with the least to lose, and provided each can see whether another has signed, so that a defection is punished; agents bargaining at machine speed are that patient and that observant. Why you care: writing and enforcing a pact among residents is a transaction cost in exactly Coase's sense, the kind that cheap agents cut, so the essay's floor "which no price of compute touches" contains two failures that compute does touch. What survives is narrower and still true. The pact binds its signatories by their agreement and works only if the factory, which did not agree to it, cannot sign one resident before it closes; the escrow works only if whoever holds it cannot keep the money; the repeated route works only if the factory cannot buy consent once and for all. In each case somebody is held to something against a later temptation, and in the first and third that somebody is the factory, bound by a rule it did not agree to. Under such a commitment the cooperative game theorist finds that bargaining at $k=1$ can reach the clean river at a division in which the residents pay the firm not to pollute: splitting the $4$ of welfare the clean river recovers evenly, one each, gives the firm $11$ against the $10$ it earns by polluting, and residents $-1$, $-2$ and $-8$ against $-2$, $-3$ and $-9$, so every party, the firm included, does better than under divide and conquer. The section should keep its arithmetic and change its lesson: the residents' failure is the absence of a binding pact, not a fact about computation, and the question is who can bind whom.

### The river at $k=n$, and all three failures as one fact

The private-harms paragraph of the river section says that at $k=n$ "no voluntary, budget-balanced, honesty-respecting procedure reaches the efficient action for certain", and that the odds fall to zero as $n$ grows. Two readers, the economist and the cooperative game theorist, point out that the theorem behind the first clause, Myerson and Satterthwaite's, assumes each resident may decline to take part *after* learning their own harm. Fix participation *before* harms are known, which is what a rule adopted in advance does, and there is a mechanism, the expected-externality mechanism of d'Aspremont and Gérard-Varet and of Arrow, both 1979, that reaches the efficient action, pays for itself, and gives nobody a reason to lie; it works by charging each resident the expected harm their report imposes on the others. Its cost is that some residents will turn out, once their harm is known, to be worse off than if there had been no rule. The cooperative game theorist then makes the observation that reorganizes the essay. The $k=n$ failure is the right to decline after the fact. The $k=0$ failure is the right not to join. The $k=1$ failure is the right to deal bilaterally while the others are still combining. All three are one fact: a deal holds only those who agreed to it and only while they want to stay, and the efficient outcome needs somebody held against a later temptation to leave, sell out, or decline. Call a rule anything that holds a party who did not agree, or holds a party to what it agreed after it has changed its mind. The pact among residents is a deal plus a rule on the factory; the escrow is a deal plus a rule on whoever holds the money; the mechanism adopted in advance is a rule. Why you care: this is the one result in the essay that every reader left standing, it is what the essay has been circling since it first asked "who chose $k$?" and deferred the answer to its close, and it is also what Krier concedes when, as the advocate reports from his essay, he keeps the courts as the enforcer of whatever the agents agree. The three failures should be presented as one, and the rebuilt essay should draw its line between deals and rules here.

### The decomposition: a loss that was said to grow as compute cheapens

The section "What makes up transaction costs" writes the cost of a sure deal as price times count plus a strategic loss $L$, says $L$ is "the loss no procedure avoids" and that "nothing lowers $L$ except changing the game". The paragraph added in this revision just after it, beginning "The three parts of the bill named at the start now have symbols", says the contest is "the loss growing as $p$ falls", with $p$ the unit price of compute, and "The rulebook" repeats that "dissipation is $L$ growing as $p$ falls". The economist and the cold reader both point out that these cannot both hold, since the decomposition writes the loss as $L(k,F)$ with no $p$ in it, and that the second is false under the model the essay cites for it. In Tullock's model of a contest, two sides each spend a fixed fraction of the prize, in money, whatever the unit price of effort: cheaper compute buys more operations for the same money and burns no more surplus. The only kind of contest in which spending rises as the unit price falls is one where what matters is how far ahead one side is rather than the ratio of what the two spend, and in that kind a uniform fall in price also tilts the division toward the richer side, which is a further claim the essay would have to make and does not. No model of a contest gives both a division that uniform cheapening leaves unchanged and dissipation that uniform cheapening raises. Why you care: "cheaper compute lets both sides burn more of the surplus" is the intro's second claim, and it has no mechanism. The decomposition should keep price, count and loss, and the essay should stop saying the loss grows as the price falls.

### The clause count: whose information is it

The section "How big is a contract?" says that a resident's preferences over $m$ clauses "take $2^m$ numbers rather than $m$" because clause values interact, and that the human cost of telling an agent what we want is "$2^m$ numbers per bargain when the clauses interact"; the decomposition section, under its entry for computational costs, says the efficient deal needs "a number of bits to cross between the holders of private information that grows exponentially in the clauses". The complexity theorist's objection, which the cold reader reaches independently, is about what is private. The essay's four clauses, night discharge, summer discharge, the odour threshold, monitoring waived, describe one outfall, and every resident faces the same configuration of them. A resident's harm from a configuration has two parts: how much pollution that configuration delivers to their stretch of river, which is physics and can be published, and how much a given amount of pollution hurts them, which is private but is a curve of a few parameters. The interactions the essay points to, summer discharge hurting more when monitoring is waived, live in the physics, not in the resident. So what has to leave a resident's head is a handful of numbers whatever $m$ is, and the $2^m$ is a computation that one datacentre runs on public inputs, which is where the essay itself says compute helps. The theorem the decomposition cites, Nisan and Segal's, is about dividing private goods, goods whose use by one party affects nobody else, among bidders with arbitrary valuations, and it does bound the river's problem if valuations are arbitrary; but a human cannot hold $2^m$ independent values for any $m$ beyond a dozen or so, so by the essay's own "fixed biological hardware" no resident has them. Krier's own examples of hyper-granular terms, which the advocate reports from his essay and which the draft does not quote, music tolerated on Saturdays but not Sundays, carpooling only under a ten-minute detour, are threshold rules on a few dimensions, not dense interactions. Why you care: the count was the intro's first claim and the clause section's reason for the standard form. What survives is strategic, not computational. One: a drafter who knows a clause's cost when the reader cannot price it gains by adding it, which is real in contracts nobody reads, with Gabaix and Laibson 2006, a model of how sellers profit from terms that inattentive buyers do not price, as the theory and the steady growth of software licences as the evidence. Two: the handful of numbers above is what it takes to find the efficient configuration when everyone reports honestly; a mechanism that has to make honesty each resident's best policy needs, in the worst case, communication exponential in the number of clauses; that is a price of honesty, not of clauses. The section should be rewritten around those two, and it should concede that an agent reads a clause as readily as it drafts one, so the drafting weapon, the essay's "open clause menu is a weapon", is the thing cheap reading disarms.

### The substitutes concession contradicts the river

"Can prices save us?" says that at $k=1$ "signatures are substitutes, and the market in them clears efficient, at a price of nearly zero", and that the market's groping toward prices "works precisely on the side of the line where the core was never in trouble", the substitutes side. "What survives" then concedes that where consents are substitutes "nothing in this post bites", and "Point the agents at the rulebook" expects agents there to be "a plain delight". The economist and the cold reader both notice that $k=1$ *is* the substitutes case in the essay's own model, and it is the case with the empty core and welfare $-4$. The reason is that the theorems about prices and substitutes, Jordan's and Kelso and Crawford's, are about private goods, where a trade affects only the traders; at $k=1$ each signature sold harms the residents who did not sign. So the concession is made on ground the river shows is not safe, and once it is restated correctly, as "substitutes, and no harm to anyone outside the trade", it covers none of Krier's externality cases. The advocate adds two things. Krier's starter cases, the shared thermostat, the apartment block, household chores, parking, are shared decisions among a few people, and the essay's own explanation of why they work, in "Point the agents at the rulebook", is that custom pins the division, not that consents are substitutes. And the markets the concession does cover have their own troubles: pricing agents working for competing sellers reach prices above the competitive level on their own in simulation, and in the one field study, German petrol stations, margins rose only where every station had adopted them. Why you care: "What survives" concedes "most of daily economic life" to Krier, and "Point the agents at the rulebook" then calls the gains from agents "small beside" existing institutions; the first is made on unsafe ground and the second does not follow from it. The concession should be narrowed to markets without externalities, the critique should rest on Krier's own large cases, and the claim that agents transform daily life should not be made without argument.

### Prices: what the no-uniform-price arithmetic does and does not show

"Can prices save us?" shows that at $k=3$ with $h=(2,3,9)$ and $\pi=20$ no single price per signature works, since resident 3 signs only for at least $9$ and the factory, paying three of them, can afford at most $20/3$ each, and it says beforehand that "a coalition is a complementarity by construction". Later it says that Krier's mechanism, a coalition offering each resident a tailored transfer, is "bargaining wearing the market's clothes", and "The contest over the division" notes that his bargaining club pays its members "a common clearing price". The arithmetic survives every reader, and the advocate concedes it is the essay's best point against the club, since a common price cannot clear harms of $2$, $3$ and $9$. The cold reader narrows what it shows. The impossibility comes from the entitlement being consent, one signature per resident. Under a liability rule, meaning a rule that lets the factory pollute without anyone's consent but obliges it to pay each resident a set compensation, or under a charge per unit of harm, there is a single anonymous price on the harm, and it implements the comparison of $\pi$ against $\sum h_i$ directly, leaving the problem of measuring the harms, which is a problem of information. And "a coalition is a complementarity by construction" is false in general: in the games Shapley and Shubik studied, where buyers and sellers pair off, coalitions form and prices support every stable division. Why you care: the essay's model has transfers only by agreement, so it cannot express a liability rule or a charge, and its conclusion in "What survives", under "Fourth, equity", that residents at $k<n$ are "harmed and unpaid" is an artefact of that. The section should keep the arithmetic and say what it shows: a consent entitlement needs personalized prices and a liability rule does not. The river section should say that liability rules are outside the model.

### Pivotality: Shapley is not what anyone can hold out for

"The contest over the division" says "a reservation price is a fact about pivotality", measures pivotality by the Shapley–Shubik index, and says that "at fifty residents with heterogeneous weights, computing pivotality is the #P-complete problem". The cooperative game theorist's point is that the index is an average over every order in which the residents could sign, including the orders in which this resident's signature changes nothing, and an average is not something anyone can hold out for. What a resident can hold out for is what the entitlement lets them block. In the essay's own weighted example, frontages $(1,1,3)$ and a quota of $4$, the factory needs resident 3 and at least one of the other two; resident 3 can block alone and residents 1 and 2 cannot, because either can be replaced by the other. So the only divisions that no group could improve on by walking out give residents 1 and 2 nothing above their harm and split the rest between resident 3 and the factory; the index assigns residents 1 and 2 one sixth each, but the rules give them no way to keep it. When the essay says the firm "knows *which* members of the club are underpricing themselves, and those are the doors it knocks on", the firm is paying residents 1 and 2 less than an average they were never entitled to, which is a complaint about the fairness of the yardstick, not a result about computation. Three other readers reach the same place by other routes. The economist cites the result that in legislative bargaining under weighted voting, what players are paid is proportional to their weight, which is public and costs nothing to read, not to the index. The cold reader notes that the index measures a share of a fixed prize, not whether a refusal changes anything. The complexity theorist notes that in a game where a deal among some parties harms the others, which the river is, the index's general form, the Shapley value, is not even uniquely defined. Why you care: the section rests on pivotality being expensive to compute and therefore worth fighting over with compute. Checking who can block is cheap in weighted games, and if what residents can hold out for is their blocking power, there is nothing left to fight over. The section should instead ask what a resident can hold out for under the entitlement, which is cheap, and where the firm's edge comes from if not from arithmetic, which the finding on commitment below answers.

### The pick-off shrinks as compute cheapens, and the one real race

Under "Fourth, equity" in "What survives", the essay says "sampling error shrinks with the square root of the budget on both sides, and the pick-off still goes to whoever holds the tighter error bars", and a few paragraphs earlier calls a compute subsidy "a bigger shovel against an exponential mountain". Two readers derived the size of the pick-off from the essay's own picture, one put today's prices into it, and one simulated it. The picture: each resident estimates their own index by sampling and names a price with some error; the firm estimates too, more precisely, and makes offers to the residents whose errors happen to run low. The economist's derivation: the firm's gain per resident is proportional to the *resident's* error, not the firm's, and once the firm is more precise than the resident, more precision buys it almost nothing: at equal budgets the firm already captures 71 per cent of the most it could ever take from that resident, which is set by the resident's error, and at a hundred times the resident's budget, 99.5 per cent. The complexity theorist puts today's prices into it. Write $W$ for the surplus, $B$ for a resident's compute budget in dollars and $p$ for the price of one operation; the resident's error in money is then $W\cdot\tfrac12\sqrt{np/B}$. At $B$ of one dollar, $n=50$, and $p$ of about $10^{-16}$ dollars, roughly what a cloud GPU costs at poor utilization, that is about thirty-five dollars per resident on a billion-dollar surplus, and summed over the fifty residents the firm's whole gain from the pick-off is about one part in a million of the surplus. The economist adds that if spending is chosen rather than fixed, the money each side spends on the race also falls as the price falls, so cheaper compute shrinks the burning as well as the take. The cooperative game theorist's simulation, with fifty residents and ten consents needed, shows the firm's take flat as its own budget grows and halving each time both budgets are quadrupled. And the exponential mountain is not there. Under "*The division*" in "How big is a contract?" the essay cites a dynamic programme whose cost grows with the number of distinguishable weight levels, and says pivotality is "#P-complete on one surveyed in cents"; but at cent precision for frontages worth up to a million dollars each, that programme costs about $6\times10^{12}$ operations for fifty residents, which is cents of compute, and for the ten thousand residents of the essay's own example, a number Krier also uses, dollars. The sentence about cents is wrong on the essay's own citation. Why you care: this was the essay's only worked example of a contest that cheaper compute makes worse, and in the essay's own model cheaper compute makes it smaller. What is left of the idea inside the river is one thing the cooperative game theorist supplies. Where the core is empty, the factory races to sign one resident before the residents finish the pact described in the finding on $k=1$ above, a pact that binds only if all three sign; if the speed of searching and messaging is proportional to compute, the chance of finishing first depends on the ratio of the two sides' compute and is unchanged when both get cheaper. That race is about speed of commitment, it lives where the river already fails, and it is what Krier, as the advocate reports, calls a flash coalition, residents' agents combining in an instant, seen from the factory's side. "The contest over the division" should replace the pick-off with the race.

### Pinning the division at unanimity removes the veto

"*Third, pin the division*" in "The rulebook" says a formula division "deletes the count, and with it the incentive to run the pivotality-estimating contest", and gives its cost at $k=n$: "a formula that ignores differences in harm may offer somebody less than the harm they bear, and they refuse". The cooperative game theorist completes the account. A formula on observables, heads or frontage, closes every efficient deal at unanimity only if it pays every resident at least their harm, which means paying everyone what the most-harmed resident with their observables would need. On the river at $\pi=20$ a per-head formula must pay everyone $9$, which is $27$ against a profit of $20$, so it either underpays and fails or overpays and cannot be afforded; a per-frontage formula at $3$ per unit happens to fit, $(3,3,9)$ for $15$, because in this example frontage tracks harm. So a formula on observables either fails deals, as the essay says, or overpays, which the essay does not say, and which it does depends on whether the observable tracks the harm. The formula that closes every efficient deal without either failure is the one that asks each resident to report their harm and sets the transfers from the reports, the expected-externality mechanism described in the finding on $k=n$ above, which charges each resident the expected harm their report imposes on others, and it closes deals by removing the right to decline after the fact. Why you care: the essay offers the pinned division as the rule that makes compute harmless while, in its own words elsewhere, keeping the deals "that should close from failing". At unanimity it cannot do both: the version that closes every deal is the version that takes away the veto, so keeping every resident no worse off and closing every efficient deal trade against each other rather than arriving together. And a formula on observables makes the observables the thing to game. The rule should be presented with that trade stated, and the Budish precedent dropped, since batching stock-market time removed a race to be fastest at no cost to trade, and coarsening the division is not free.

### Bounding the bargain is a theorem, and the chain's gatekeeper is the prize

"*First, bound the bargain*" in "The rulebook" says "The physics of the river is a chain" and that the rule of procedure should make the groups who can contract coincide with it: "this bargain admits the residents of this reach". The cold reader objects that the river's decision is one decision harming everyone, so a chain of separate bargains bounds the bargain but not the harm, and the game among the reaches is the $k$-of-$n$ game again one level up. The cooperative game theorist finds the result that makes the rule a theorem rather than a rule of thumb, and it is about who may combine with whom rather than about the water. If the groups allowed to form are restricted to a chain, factory to resident 1 to resident 2 to resident 3, the core is never empty, by results of Le Breton, Owen and Weber 1992 and of Demange 2004. On the river at $k=1$ and $\pi=10$ a stable division then exists: residents 2 and 3 pay resident 1 a total of $8$ to refuse the factory, which is what resident 1 and the factory could keep between them by polluting, $\pi-h_1=8$; the river stays clean, and nobody can do better by walking out. Why you care: this is the one place where "bound the bargain" has a proof, and the proof shows what the rule costs. Which chain is drawn decides who sits next to the factory, the gatekeeper of the heading, and collects the payments for refusing, and that is the whole prize of the constitutional question the essay keeps deferring. The rule's justification should be rewritten on this result, with the treewidth paragraph dropped, since the cold reader and the complexity theorist both note that the theorem it cites, Courcelle's, gives a running time whose dependence on the graph's width is far worse than the $2^{O(t)}$ the essay writes, and that the river's problem is a sum, not a graph problem.

### Exact play selects nothing

The second-to-last paragraph of "The rulebook" says that with the caps set low enough "every party can work the whole game out exactly" and "exact play is the ceiling", and that "Unpin the division and the pivotality race restarts inside bargains that are otherwise still small". The cooperative game theorist and the complexity theorist both note that exact play is affordable at every size the essay names, so the first claim is true without any cap; and that when it is affordable the division is not thereby settled. At $k=3$ and $\pi=20$ every division of the $6$ that pays each resident at least their harm is one no group could improve on by walking out, so knowing the game exactly selects none of them, and what remains is haggling; at $k=1$ and $\pi=10$, exact knowledge tells everyone there is no stable division at all, unless who may combine with whom is restricted as in the finding on bounding the bargain above. Why you care: this paragraph credits the caps with a ceiling, exact play, that compute reaches on its own, and it names a race that the finding on the pick-off shows is not there. The sentence about the race restarting should say that the fight over the division restarts, which is a fight over a known surplus, and the paragraph should stop attributing to the caps a ceiling that compute reaches on its own.

### Commitment is the mechanism, and the essay had it

The second half of "Institutions as contest-enders" says the residents' best defence may be "an agent that is verifiably crude — a single take-it-or-leave-it schedule, published in advance and checkable afterwards", and its first half says the firm freezes membership and the division. Four readers, the advocate, the economist, the cold reader and the cooperative game theorist, say this is the right mechanism in the wrong place, the wrong place being a section that presents institutions as a saving on computation. The advocate points out that Krier proposes exactly it: in his essay, ten thousand residents' agents form a coalition, name a collective price they will not negotiate, and become what he calls a digital union; and under the standard account of a take-it-or-leave-it offer, which the essay does not state, the side that makes it, if the other can only accept or refuse, keeps everything above the other side's walk-away value. The economist cites the results on what happens when both sides can commit cheaply and verifiably: the outcomes that remain once each side has discarded moves that can only hurt it give all the surplus to one side or carry a high chance of no deal, and with frequent offers the bargain becomes a standoff that ends when one side gives up, settled by who is believed to be committed. The economist adds two further channels. An offerer who cannot commit loses power as offers become frequent, because the other side can wait for a better one; on the river the residents hold the private information, their harms, and the factory does not, so the side whose edge erodes at machine speed is the factory, unless it can bind itself. and cheaper screening lets an offerer locate each resident's walk-away point more precisely, which is the essay's own "from a price toward a quote". The cold reader adds the older point, Crawford 1982, that incompatible commitments produce impasse, and notes that a crude residents' agent presupposes the residents have already bound themselves to one another, the pact the river section never allows for. The advocate's evidence from agents in practice says the same: what decides where populations of negotiating agents settle is who proposes first, who commits, who is cohesive and whose model is stronger, with the small cohesive party favoured, which on the river is the factory. Why you care: this is the answer to who gains, and it does not run through the size of compute budgets at all, except as speed in the race to commit first described in the pick-off finding above. One correction before the recommendation: the essay cites Rubinstein 1986 for the claim that bounding a player's machine enlarges the set of cooperative outcomes, and two readers, the cold reader and the economist, independently note that Rubinstein shows complexity costs restrict that set; the enlargement result is Neyman 1985. "Institutions as contest-enders" should be rebuilt around commitment as something available to both sides: cheap verifiable commitment is a weapon for whoever uses it first; the mechanism is Olson's, the small organized party beating the large unorganized one, rather than Tullock's, in which the outcome tracks what each side spends; and the question of who can bind whom returns. A stronger model is not more operations; it is more information, and it belongs with screening above.

### Agents converge, and what they converge on is chosen by speed and cohesion

The paragraph of "Institutions as contest-enders" added in this revision says agents that stop renegotiating "have not found the stable point, and on the river at $\pi=20$ and $k=3$ there is no single one to find: every division of the $6$ is in the core", and that they freeze "wherever the contest stood when the spending stopped, which favours whoever had more to spend". The advocate's evidence is against both halves. Populations of language-model agents settle on shared conventions without being told to; pricing agents reach prices above the competitive level on their own; and where several divisions are stable, bargainers who adjust to what worked last time select one anyway, the equal split in the symmetric case, which the essay itself calls the natural division. Knowing the game exactly does not pick a division, as the finding on exact play above says, but the process of bargaining does. What the same evidence says about *which* convention is selected is that committed minorities overturn conventions, first proposals beat better ones by a wide margin, and the stronger model extracts a few per cent from the weaker side without the weaker side noticing. None of that is the number of operations spent; the stronger model is a matter of what it knows. Why you care: the paragraph's mechanism, abandonment where the money ran out, is an assertion against Krier's, and his is better supported. The paragraph should say that agents will converge, and that the selection favours whoever moves first and holds together, which on the river, as the finding on commitment says, is the factory, the one party on its side of the table.

### Krier as he wrote it

The essay's statement of Krier's argument in the decomposition section, "more deals leads to more efficiency, and that leads to more equity", its claim in "What survives" that his anti-holdout devices each let "the deal close over the objection of somebody whose consent was formerly required", and its conclusion there that "He can have universal compensation or he can have his anti-holdout machinery", were written against a summary of his essay prepared for the author during drafting rather than against his text. The advocate read his essay and the Shahidi chapter in full and finds the notes wrong in what they filed and left out. His option fee is a tax on the price a holdout claims, a Harberger-style charge, meaning the claimant pays tax on the value they name and must sell at it; he describes it as making stonewalling carry "a real, rising cost". It needs no consent and owes compensation at the claimed value, so it is a liability rule, the kind described in the prices finding above, not a lower $k$. His reimbursing veto makes any coalition that blocks a deal owe the public its quantified loss, which binds exactly the holdouts who would never join a voluntary club. He keeps the courts and calls them non-negotiable, says the initial entitlement is a constitutional choice to be changed through democratic politics even while, elsewhere in the same essay, he calls legislatures ossified and regulators captured, a tension that is his, raises the political Coase theorem himself and proposes transparency and automated auditing against it, says that constantly renegotiating property rights would destroy investment and proposes pricing stability against flexibility, says transaction costs are "massively reduced" and not zero, and never proposes $k=1$: his defaults are a right to pollute, the river's $k=0$, or a right to be free of it, its $k=n$, and his worked cases, a developer against every homeowner, a beach closure against everyone who loses access, use unanimity. The Shahidi chapter is about consumer digital markets, says agents are not guaranteed to improve efficiency where there are externalities, and devotes a section to how regulation must evolve; it is company for the essay, not a target. Why you care: the three sentences quoted above, the intro's "drive the transaction costs of bargaining toward zero", and the equity passage's reading of the option fee all attack positions he does not hold. The summary and the essay's statement of his argument both have to be corrected before anything is rewritten.

### What survives of the critique of Krier

Everything above removes claims. This is what nobody removed. Krier's distinctive claim is scale: in his essay he carries the mechanisms of parking disputes up to catchments, cities and interstate disputes, and his large cases, as the advocate reports them, are a factory against ten thousand residents, a developer against every homeowner, a beach closure against everyone who loses access. Those are unanimity games over complements, the river at $k=n$, and the advocate concedes that the essay's arithmetic holds there. A club that pays its members "a common clearing price", as "The contest over the division" says his does, cannot clear harms of $2$, $3$ and $9$ against a profit of $20$, since resident 3 needs $9$ and three of anything costs at most $20/3$ each, and a club that pays each member their own stated minimum rewards overstating it, which the footnote there says the club cannot escape, since no club can be honest, efficient and self-funding at once. His two anti-holdout devices, the option fee and the reimbursing veto described in the previous finding, price refusal, and pricing a refusal needs the loss the refusal causes, which the people who benefit from the deal hold privately and are paid to overstate; and neither device caps how many parties a bargain admits, how many clauses it carries, or how the surplus is split between polluter and residents. What the advocate asks in return is that the essay say which Krier it is arguing with: the long tail of small shared decisions, where his agenda largely wins because, as the substitutes finding above says, custom pins the division, or the scale claim his essay is sold on, where it is contested; and that it stop presenting the first as a concession that costs nothing.

### The rulebook is exempted from the contest

"Point the agents at the rulebook" says to point them there "rather than at each other", that "Design is amortized" and "Design is low-dimensional", and that what is left is "the half that was never computational". Three readers, by three routes, say the rulebook is exempted from the contest the essay describes everywhere else. The cold reader: if cheap cognition intensifies contests, then by the essay's own amortization argument the biggest prize of all is the rule, since it governs every bargain under it, and pointing the agents at it sends the contest to its largest prize; the cold reader's example is the millions of fabricated public comments in the 2017 American net-neutrality docket. The advocate: Krier's reason, in his essay, for preferring bargaining to regulation is that regulators are captured and legislatures ossified, so the rule-maker the essay's close relies on is the one he distrusts, even while he keeps the courts to enforce whatever is agreed; and the essay concedes his point at every turn, the cap "chosen by the same parties who then bargain beneath it", a cap set high "not a safety rail but a moat". The comparison is then between real bargaining with its contests and a rulebook with none, which is the comparison Demsetz called the nirvana fallacy and Coase's section VI, which the essay cites, warned against. The economist: the argument the essay needs and never makes is that a contest over rules is less extractive, per unit of compute, than a contest inside bargains; the candidate reasons are that rules are general, public, few in number and chosen by people who do not know which side they will be on, and each has to be argued. The economist also supplies the one thing in the essay's favour here: a rule adopted before harms are known escapes the private-harms failure, as the finding on $k=n$ says, at the cost of some residents being worse off after the fact, so the choice between rules and deals is a trade in which more of one means less of the other, not a free improvement. Why you care: this is the essay's constructive ending, and as written it is conditional on the proposition Krier denies. The close should say that the rule-maker is a party, that cheap cognition cheapens the contest over rules too, that the essay has no model of that contest, and what it would take to have one.

## A proposed order for the rebuilt essay

1. **The claim and the river.** Krier's claim; the river as the one model on which it is tested; the thesis paragraph above as the answer, stated once.
2. **The river.** The model as it stands; the three failures, presented as one fact about commitment; the pact, the escrow, repeated consent and the rule adopted in advance as the things that fix them, each holding somebody against a later temptation.
3. **What agents make cheap.** Finding each other, drafting, monitoring, and the arithmetic of consent at every size in the essay, with the dynamic programme and sampling by random orderings as the evidence; the plain statement that these were the transaction costs and they fall.
4. **What prices cannot do, and what Krier's club is.** The no-uniform-price arithmetic; consent entitlements need personalized prices and liability rules do not; the club's common price; the scale critique, stated as the one claim against Krier that stands.
5. **Who gets what.** Commitment and information: the race to sign one resident where no stable division exists; frequent offers favouring the residents, who hold the private information; cheaper screening and the drift from price to quote; cheap verifiable commitment as a first-mover weapon; cheap observation of who has signed and the residents' refusal. Agents converge, and the selection favours whoever moves first and holds together.
6. **Rules.** Pinning the division as the mechanism that closes deals by removing the veto, with the trade stated; bounding the bargain as the chain theorem, with the resident who sits next to the factory as the prize; the standard form as a rule against drafting for readers who cannot price what they sign.
