# Coasean street stories

Ten sketches toward the essay. They are not the essay itself.
Each is built on one or two results in `hyperscaling_coase_humanized.md` and ends with the condition that would have had to be in place for the story to go well.
The cast is the street's, kept for traceability back to the model: Alice, mildly bothered and out by day; Bob, mildly bothered and home all day; Carol, who lives beside the road, sleeps badly, minds most and is poorest; and the delivery company, which works a thousand streets.

## 1. The honeymoon

The protocol for street bargaining comes out of a consultation, and the people who turn up to the consultation are mostly the ones who will use the protocol every day: the logistics firms' industry association, two platform vendors, and a law firm on retainer to both.
Their main request is modest and sounds like common sense.
Whoever wants to use the street makes the offer, the way a planning applicant lodges a plan and the neighbours object or don't.
So the company's agent proposes a schedule and a payment to each household, and each household's agent says yes or no.

At first the company's agent knows so little about how much each household minds that many of its offers are refused, and the vans mostly don't run.
Then it learns enough to close deals but not enough to close them tightly, so it leaves a safety margin on each offer, and that margin is the neighbours' gain.
These are the good years; the local paper runs a piece about Bob buying a bike out of van money.
Then the company's agent, which has now seen a thousand streets, gets better still, and the margin shrinks toward each household's bare harm.
No rule changes.
The neighbours' agents improve too, but an agent that can only say yes or no to a well-aimed offer has nothing to compute.
The night slot is never even proposed, since an offer that invites refusal puts the whole deal at risk.
Anyone who drove for a ride-hail company through its early bonus years will probably recognize the curve.

The town that avoids this does something most people find odd.
A lottery decides, deal by deal, which party makes the final offer — sometimes the company, sometimes Alice, sometimes Carol, whose agent then writes a schedule that the company may only accept or refuse.
To the firm this looks like licensed hold-up; to the neighbours it looks like a casino.
It is nonetheless the one arrangement in the model under which better agents help everyone at every step.
The lottery is a rule, somebody has to run it, and none of the agents would have proposed it.

*Rests on:* "When the company always makes the final offer", "When it works", "When holding the right is not enough".
*Precondition:* the final offer rotates among persons, by a rule set outside the bargain.
In the model the neighbours' take peaks at a middling estimation budget and then falls.

## 2. Seventy-five percent

A few holdouts make the news.
A homeowner's agent, tuned for maximum extraction, names a sum that would sink a new ambulance depot, and the story runs for a week.
Krier anticipated this kind of ransom, and the pro-housing lobby, the logistics association and the developers find themselves on the same side of a reform: let a supermajority consent on behalf of the street, two signatures out of three.
The case for it is good, since holdouts do block useful things.
The developers fund the campaign, and it passes.

Under the new rule the company's agent stops bargaining with the street and starts bargaining with pairs.
Alice and Bob mind least, so it offers them a little more than they would get by holding out together, and they sign.
Carol, who minds most, is outvoted, and her harm drops out of the accounts altogether; the deal is priced as if she were not there.
The neighbours saw this coming and formed a pact, nobody signs unless everybody does, and it falls apart within weeks.
Nobody in the pact behaves badly.
Because Carol's harm is not counted in any pair's deal, every pair can be offered more than the pact can give it, so there is no way of dividing the pact's proceeds that keeps every member in.
Playing the same game every month does not rescue it, because the defectors get paid every month too.
Once the neighbours start competing to be one of the two signatures, the price of a signature falls to nothing, all four slots run, and every neighbour ends worse off than with a quiet street.
The complaints that followed New South Wales' 2016 strata renewal laws, which let 75% of owners force the sale of a building over the objections of the rest, had a similar shape.

There are two ways out, and both cost something.
The council can restore unanimity, and with it the holdout problem the reform was meant to fix.
Or someone can enforce the neighbours' pact, and the enforcer, in practice a platform, becomes a party with its own price, able to take most of what the vans earn unless something binds it in turn.
Well-meaning neighbours cannot hold this pact on trust alone; they need an institution, and the institution needs a leash.

*Rests on:* "When two signatures out of three are enough".
*Precondition:* the consent rule is set by someone who is not a party, and any enforcer of a residents' pact is itself bound.
In the model the pact's expected life falls to almost nothing as the company's estimates sharpen.

## 3. The form that cannot say "but not both"

Carol can sleep through one van a day.
Two, the morning run and then the evening run, and she has lost the day.
The bargaining schema, written by an industry consortium for interoperability, describes each household by one number per slot, so that a household's harm from a schedule is the sum of its harms from each slot.
Nothing that can be written in that schema says "either, but not both".
Disability assessments have struggled with fluctuating conditions for the same reason: the form asks whether the claimant can walk to the shops, and the claimant can, once.

The company's agent fits Carol's numbers from her history, proposes morning, midday and evening, and pays her for three separate slots.
The better its data, the more confidently it underpays her, because nothing the schema records can reveal the extra harm.
Carol's agent refuses every time, and since nothing runs without her, the company gets nothing either.
More computation means fewer deals: in the model the share of the company's offers that close falls from about half to none as its estimates improve.
The consortium could instead group the slots into clauses that are accepted or refused whole, but every grouping suits some households and fails others, and the consortium has to choose before it knows which households it is writing for.

Here the optimists' picture does work, once the form is fixed.
If Carol's agent records how much she minds every possible schedule, all sixteen ways of running the four slots, the company's agent finds the best schedule whatever the shape of her harm.
Agents searching over schedules and remedies together also find things people don't.
Double glazing on Carol's window, bought as part of the deal, makes the night run worth running.
Nobody finds this by haggling over the window and the schedule separately, since on its own the glazing saves Carol exactly what it costs; found together, it raises the street's total gain by about a quarter, and every party is better off.
Two caveats stand.
The table doubles with each slot added, so an hourly day is out of reach and someone still chooses what a preference is allowed to look like.
And the bigger gain is still divided by whoever holds the final offer.

*Rests on:* "When Carol's problem does not fit the form", "When the platform writes the clauses", "When the agent records every schedule", "When the agents search schedule and insulation together".
*Precondition:* a preference language able to carry every party's harm, written by someone answerable to the people whose harms are hard to describe.

## 4. Everybody's child has asthma

This one is Krier's own example.
Carol's son has asthma, and her agent broadcasts a high price for diesel vans on the street.
The company pays it.
Within a season every agent on the street is broadcasting a high price, Alice's and Bob's included, because their agents are competent and nobody can check.
Alice minds a little, and there is no way to show that she does not mind a lot.
The mechanism designers' answer is a deal in which nobody gains by exaggerating, and such deals exist, but they work by paying the mildly bothered what exaggeration would have brought them and paying the badly bothered just enough to agree.
Under the company's preferred truthful deal, Alice and Bob collect extra for what they might have claimed, and Carol, whose son does have asthma, is paid exactly her harm and gains nothing.

So the street retreats to Krier's bargaining club, which pays every member one price so that padding a claim cannot raise anyone's cheque.
To buy Carol's consent, the common price must cover her harm, so Alice and Bob are paid as if they minded as much as she does, and the company can only just afford it.
Raising the price to leave Carol something gives the same rise to all three, so Alice, who minds least, does best and Carol does worst.
Equal shares feel fair, and the club's designers meant well; it still transfers to whoever minds least.
On the polluted-river version of the problem the club cannot clear at all.

The way out is to pay each household according to how much it does mind, which means somebody verifying how much it minds: a doctor's certificate, an assessor with access to medical records, an appeals tribunal.
That is a bureaucracy that sees inside households and holds the kind of local knowledge Krier, after Hayek, says a planner cannot hold.
The other way out is a standing instruction signed before anyone knows who will be badly affected, a kind of insurance.
It raises everyone's expected outcome, but it works only for harms drawn afresh after signing, never for a lasting condition like a child's asthma.
Most people would find both remedies intrusive or bizarre, and the alternative is a system in which the household with the worst harm is the one that gains nothing.

*Rests on:* "When nobody can prove how much she minds", "When the club pays one price".
*Precondition:* harms verified or common knowledge, so that payments can be tailored, which brings back an assessor and the politics of who assesses.
In the model Carol gains nothing under the company's best truthful deal at every estimation budget.

## 5. Two towns, one depot

The company can serve the region from a depot in either of two towns, or send its vans the long way round on the ring road.
Every agent on the street knows the most the company will pay: the cost of the detour.
The neighbours' agents can estimate as well as they like, but there is nothing left to estimate.
The bargain covers only the small gain the street offers over the ring road, and the company keeps the value of the detour on top.
In the model each neighbour ends with about a quarter of what the fair deal gave them before the ring road existed.

When the two towns compete for the same depot it gets worse.
Each town's agents, loyal to their own town, undercut the other town's offer, and both end with nothing, every veto intact.
Amazon's HQ2 contest drew bids from more than two hundred cities, and Wisconsin promised Foxconn billions of dollars in incentives for a plant that was mostly not built.
Cheap agents do not change the logic of a bidding war; they run it faster.
Krier's option fee, a tax on inflated claims, prices the company's threat to leave but does not remove it.

What stops a bidding war is a rule above the bidders: a regional compact in which towns agree not to undercut each other, or a central prohibition such as the EU's state aid rules, which come from Brussels.
That is a planner of the kind the Coasean programme was meant to retire.
Without one, mobile firms collect the surplus of every immobile community.

*Rests on:* "When the company can go round the street", "When the street is one of many" (two streets competing).
*Precondition:* no outside option held by one side alone, or coordination among the communities that side can choose between.

## 6. The boundary road

Krier lived in a London low-traffic neighbourhood and suggests that agents could let taxis pay to cut through.
The standing complaint about such schemes is a different one: the traffic does not vanish, it moves to the boundary roads, which critics say are where poorer people live.
Put the street's bargain in agents' hands and the same thing happens by arithmetic.
The company and the three households can all make themselves better off by sending the vans down the next street, whose residents are not in the bargain.
Every party's gain rises, the harm lands next door, and the company gains most, because the detour is its option.

Krier's answer to diffuse harm is that agents give the diffuse, unorganized public a voice.
But someone decides which agents get called when a bargain opens.
The parties to a deal have every reason not to invite the people the deal will dump on, and the next street's agents cannot object to a bargain they were never told about.
In the essay's developer example it is every homeowner whose consent is bought; the renters on the same street, and everyone on the boundary road, are not in the room.

*Rests on:* "When the street is one of many" (exported harm).
*Precondition:* a rule, set and enforced by someone without a stake, defining who is affected and therefore a party, plus liability for harm to non-parties, which is to say a court.
In the model the next street absorbs harm equal to the whole gain the street was bargaining over.

## 7. Branch stacking

Bob works out that the protocol counts agents, not people.
He registers one for himself, one for his partner and one for his adult son.
Under the everyone-must-agree rule each of those agents must be paid to sign, so the company pays Bob's household three times over; in the model a household split three ways takes half the whole gain.
Under a supermajority rule it is worse.
If three of five agents suffice, Bob's agents are the only ones the company needs, and Carol ends far below where she would be with a quiet street.

Australian party machines call this branch stacking, and every body that votes by head has had to fight some version of it, from stacked proxies at owners' corporation meetings to shell companies at shareholder votes.
The company can play too, with a subsidiary per depot, and so can the family trust that owns Bob's house.
The fix is the rule Petel calls one human, one principal, one agent, and someone has to administer it.
That means an identity registry, which is a state function, and decisions about who counts — whether a renter is a party to a street, whether a child is, whether a trust is a person — which are the political decisions the bargain was supposed to make unnecessary.

*Rests on:* "When Bob's household signs as three".
*Precondition:* one human, one principal, one agent, enforced from outside, with someone deciding who counts as a human for the purposes of the street.

## 8. On commission

Krier proposes publicly funded agents for people who cannot afford good ones, on the model of public defenders.
The cheapest agents on the market are cheap for a reason: their vendor is paid per closed deal, or takes a referral fee from the other side.
Such an agent accepts any offer that leaves its client no worse off than no deal, because a closed deal is what it is paid for.
All three neighbours use it, since it comes free with the voucher.
The company takes the whole gain, the neighbours get nothing, and the veto is gone without anyone touching the consent rule.
A vendor paid in proportion to its clients' wealth gives up Carol's claim first.

Krier expects competition to clear out parasitic agents, since switching costs approach zero.
But the client never sees what the agent could have got.
A deal at zero gain arrives with a payment attached, and from the inside it looks just like a deal at full gain.
Australia's banking royal commission spent much of its time on mortgage brokers paid by the lenders whose loans they recommended, and Levitt and Syverson found that real estate agents sell their own houses for more, after longer on the market, than they sell their clients'.
Neither problem was solved by customers switching.

*Rests on:* "When the street is one of many" (vendor paid per deal).
*Precondition:* agents' loyalty audited and enforced from outside: fiduciary law, disclosure of how agents are paid, and someone able to compare outcomes across clients.

## 9. Repeat players

Marc Galanter's 1974 essay on why the haves come out ahead in court turned on one distinction, between repeat players and one-shotters.
The company bargains on a thousand streets; each neighbour bargains on one.
The company's record is public, and every street's agents can see that it has never raised an offer after a refusal.
So they believe it, and it approaches the whole gain on every street.
Its agent has also learned from a thousand streets' worth of refusals, which is more memory than any household's agent will have, however capable.

The platforms that host the bargains keep records too, and score households by how often their agents refuse.
A refusal now costs a household something in its other dealings, its insurance or its next lease, so the company's agent can offer each household its harm less the cost of refusing, and every neighbour ends up worse off than with a quiet street.
Tenancy databases and gig-work ratings already work this way.
The company also delivers the neighbours' groceries; once no deal on the vans means no groceries either, the party with more than one thing to trade gets to choose what "no deal" means.
And a platform paid per closed deal tunes its forms toward whatever closes deals, which suits its best customer.

None of this needs bad faith from anyone; each is the ordinary advantage of being in many bargains when the other side is in one.
The fixes are old ones from consumer protection and competition law: limits on what may be scored and shared, rules against tying unrelated services, equal access to the record of past deals.
Legislatures write them.

*Rests on:* "When the street is one of many" (many streets, score for refusals, company as supplier, platform with its own payoff).
*Precondition:* equal memory, limits on scoring across bargains, and no tying of unrelated dealings.

## 10. The poor street

Carol has almost no savings, so a hundred dollars matters more to her than to Alice or Bob, and far more than to the company.
Her agent, faithful to her, sells her quiet cheaply when the rent is due, as Krier's own poor farmer sells pollution rights to the factory for cash now.
Repeat that across every street in the region and the vans, depots and data centres settle where quiet is cheapest to buy, which is where people are poorest.
Nobody planned it.
The memo that went out over Larry Summers' signature at the World Bank in 1991 made the same argument about toxic waste and low-wage countries, and called the economic logic impeccable.

Measured in wellbeing rather than money, wealth also decides which schedule is efficient.
If the neighbours hold the right to quiet, Carol alone asks for almost everything the daytime slots earn, and only the midday run goes ahead; if the company holds the right to run vans, the neighbours cannot afford to buy back their quiet, and all four slots run.
Krier says as much: the initial allocation of rights is "a constitutional choice, not a technicality".
And when things get bad, Alice and Bob can move away, while Carol cannot pay the cost of moving, so she has no floor at all.

No agent capability touches any of this.
Someone has to decide whether the system counts gains in dollars or in what the dollars are worth to the person receiving them, and who starts with which right; even counted in wellbeing, the fairest division of the street's gain gives Carol the smallest share of anyone.
Krier hands these decisions to existing jurisprudence and democratic politics, which the essay itself calls ossified and captured, and which agents are to repair.
That leaves the hardest choices with the institutions the agents were meant to improve on.

*Rests on:* "When Carol is poor", and exit priced by wealth under "When the street is one of many".
*Precondition:* a stated choice of units and an initial allocation of rights, made before the bargain by someone accountable for whose suffering the criterion can see.
