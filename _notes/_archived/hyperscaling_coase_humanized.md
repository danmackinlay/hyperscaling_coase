# More computation on one street

## The street

A delivery company wants to run vans down a street in four slots: morning, midday, evening and night.
Alice is out by day, home at night, and only mildly bothered by noise.
Bob is home all day and also only mildly bothered.
Carol lives beside the road, sleeps badly, is bothered most, and is poorest.
Each neighbour, and the company, has a negotiating agent.
Without a deal no vans run, so every gain is measured against a quiet street.
In an earlier setting the company's factory would pollute a river, harming the three by 2, 3 and 9.

Outside the bargain are a platform that writes the agents' contract; the council, which sets and enforces rules; and Krier's devices: a bargaining club that pools the neighbours' minimum asks into one offer at one common price, an option fee that taxes an inflated claim, and the courts.

The agents are meant to find the best schedule and the compensation for each neighbour.
In the fair deal, the company and each neighbour gain equally from the deliveries going ahead.
Outcomes are scored by the product of all four gains, which is zero when anyone gains nothing and highest at the fair deal.
More computation buys an agent speed in taking a binding position, estimation of what it cannot see, reliable commitment, and adaptation to the other side's play.
The Coasean bargaining optimist agenda, Krier's, is that cheap, capable agents for everyone let this bargain replace regulation and reach good outcomes by default; below it is called the optimist agenda, and "the thesis" is reserved for the essay's own claim, which is not stated here.
The regulation it would replace is the council's ordinance, which keeps nights quiet and lets the vans run by day without paying anyone, leaving Alice, Bob and Carol 1, 2.5 and 4 worse off than a quiet street.

## When it works

The council rules that the vans need all three neighbours' agreement.
The platform's contract draws by lot, among the four parties, who makes the next offer.
Every agent learns from the same amount of past dealing, and the company may pay each neighbour a different sum.
The company's agent cannot play Alice and Bob against Carol, who can refuse, nor count on speaking first, since next time Bob may.
In the street model, with the offer drawn by lot among persons, nobody loses at any step and the product of gains rises toward its best, 5.06.

Somebody supplies each condition.
A veto for every neighbour is a consent rule, set by the council or written into the form.
Nobody always speaking first is an order of offers drawn by lot, which an institution must run, since otherwise the company is quicker than three neighbours who must first agree.
Equal learning is equal memory of past dealings, which a company on many streets has and a one-off neighbour lacks.
Tailored payments are a clause in the contract.
Krier's agent vouchers equalise estimation but not speed, memory or the right, and a random order is still a rule someone imposes.
Dan, you need to decide whether these settings count for the essay or for Krier.

## When the company always makes the final offer

The optimist agenda fails when the contract lets one side make every final offer.
The platform's contract lets the company's agent propose a schedule and payments, which each neighbour can only accept or refuse.
While the company's agent knows little about each neighbour's harm, many of its guesses are refused and nobody gains.
As its estimates improve, offers close with a safety margin, and that margin is the neighbours' gain.
Once its estimates are very good, the margin disappears and each neighbour is offered her bare harm.
The neighbours' agents gain nothing by estimating, since they can only say yes or no, and the company never proposes night deliveries, since a slot that invites refusal endangers the whole offer.
In the street model, the neighbours' expected take peaks at a middling estimation budget, at 1.56, then falls.
The neighbours pay, gaining from the company's computation up to a point and losing beyond it.
The fix is a rule, written by the platform or the council, that rotates the final offer by person; the agents do not produce it.
Krier's reply, that the neighbours' agents estimate too, concedes that in a single take-it-or-leave-it round their estimation buys nothing.

## When the company's books are closed

The optimist agenda fails when the neighbours' agents must guess the company's margin while the company's agent knows their harms well.
The platform's contract draws by lot, among the four parties, who makes a single offer the others can only accept or refuse, and the vans need all three neighbours' agreement.
When Alice's agent draws the offer, it must guess what the deliveries earn the company and leave a safety margin so the company does not refuse, and the company keeps that margin.
When the company's agent draws it, its good estimates of the neighbours' harms let it offer each close to her bare harm.
So the company takes more than its fair share, but only when its own guess is much the better one; when every side guesses about equally badly, the company earns what each neighbour earns.
In the street model, the company's edge appears only once its error about the neighbours falls below about two thirds of their error about its margin.
The three neighbours pay, each falling short of the fair deal by the same amount, and nobody falls below the ordinance.
The fix is a duty, imposed by the council, to open the company's books: neighbours who know its margin leave it nothing when they make the offer, and it can take at most the whole gain when it does, so the lot holds it to at most its fair share by construction.
Disclosure that runs one way only reverses the asymmetry: with the company's books open and the neighbours' harms still private, the neighbours take more than their fair share and the company less.
So the rule must make both sides' numbers checkable at the table; the agents do not supply it.

## When two signatures out of three are enough

The optimist agenda fails when the vans need only two of the three neighbours to agree.
The council's rule lets the company run its schedule once any two sign.
The company's agent offers each neighbour the same clause: a token for signing if everyone signs, and her full harm only if hers is the deciding signature.
With three signatures none decides, so whatever the other two do, a neighbour does no worse by signing; all three sign, nobody is paid beyond the token, and all four slots run.
If the company goes door to door instead, it pays the signature that completes a pair its full harm, buys the other at close to what refusing would leave that neighbour, and never reaches Carol's door; a faster company squeezes Alice and Bob harder, but Carol's outcome is the same.
A pact among the neighbours to hold out together breaks too, because Carol's harm drops out of any pair's accounts, so every pair can claim more than the whole gain, and repetition does not help, since the defectors are paid each time.
In the street model, Carol ends 8 worse off than a quiet street, with the night slot running, under every reading checked.
Carol pays: she is left out under every proposer but herself, and ends twice as far below a quiet street as the ordinance left her.
The fix is for the council to require all three signatures, or for the neighbours to join a club whose members are bound all-or-none before any offer arrives, binding near-certainly; only that club reaches Carol.
A club that pays one price does not, leaving her below the ordinance, and a club with a common minimum but no all-or-none terms leaves her where she was.
Nor is joining a club always good for the neighbours: under the all-must-agree rule the same club costs them.
This failure needs no computation at all, since the clause and the door-to-door visit work for an agent that estimates nothing; it is a failure of the consent rule, which the agents do not set.
Krier's reply, an enforcer, concedes that the enforcer must itself be bound, since one enforcing platform can take most of what the vans earn.

## When Carol's problem does not fit the form

The optimist agenda fails when a neighbour's harm is not the sum of separate harms from each slot.
Carol can sleep through a morning van or an evening van, but not both on one day.
The company's agent describes each neighbour by one number summed over the slots, and no data within that description reveals the extra harm.
So it proposes morning, midday and evening and pays Carol for three separate slots, and the sharper its estimate of her one number, the more confidently it underpays her.
Carol's agent refuses, and in the end the company gains nothing either.
In the street model, the share of the company's offers that close falls from 54% to none as its estimation budget rises.
Carol pays first, underpaid until she refuses, and then everyone pays as the vans stop.
The fix is a rule giving the side who knows the harm, here Carol, the report or the offer; someone outside the bargain decides how preferences are described, and the agents do not.

## When the platform writes the clauses

The optimist agenda fails when the contract form decides which schedules can be agreed.
The platform's contract groups the slots into clauses, each accepted or refused whole.
Morning-to-evening plus a separate night clause loses nothing when Carol's harm is a plain sum and loses gain when her morning-and-evening problem is real.
Morning-and-midday plus evening-and-night does the reverse.
No two-clause contract is right in both cases, and which is right depends on Carol's private harm, which the platform cannot see when it writes the form.
In the street model, a single all-or-nothing clause cuts the available gain by a fifth when Carol's combination harm is real.
Everyone loses the gain the form excludes, and Carol carries the risk.
The fix is a form that carries every harm, written outside the bargain; Krier leaves such protocols to others and Petel expects platforms to set them by default, so the author is chosen before the agents begin.

## When the club pays one price

The optimist agenda fails when every neighbour must be paid the same.
The neighbours join Krier's bargaining club, which offers their consent at one price per head.
To buy Carol's consent that price must cover her harm, so Alice and Bob are paid as if they minded as much as she does.
The company can only just afford it, and at that bare price Carol gains nothing.
A higher price helps, but every rise goes to all three, so Alice collects most and Carol least.
In the street model, the single price that does best by the product of gains leaves Carol a gain of 0.28.
Carol and the company pay, and the neighbours who mind least gain.
The fix is a contract that lets the company pay each neighbour a different sum, which the platform must allow; adopting Krier's club concedes that its single price favours whoever minds least, and on the river it could not clear at all.

## When nobody can prove how much she minds

The optimist agenda fails when only each neighbour knows how much she minds.
Alice is only mildly bothered, but she will still ask for large compensation, because there is no way to show that she does not really mind.
So any deal in which nobody gains by exaggerating must pay the mildly bothered what claiming more would bring, and the badly bothered just enough to agree.
The company's agent picks the honest deal that suits it best: Alice and Bob collect extra for what they might have claimed, and Carol, who really is badly bothered, is paid exactly her harm.
Sharper estimates by the company make even the most even-handed honest deal worse for the neighbours.
In the street model, Carol gains exactly nothing under the company's best honest deal at every estimation budget.
Carol pays, as the neighbour whose harm is real and largest.
The fix is a standing instruction signed before anyone knows how bothered she will be, which raises everyone's expected outcome but takes from whoever turns out badly bothered.
It works only where sensitivity is drawn afresh after signing, never for a lasting trait like Carol's bad sleep, and somebody other than the agents must arrange the signing.

## When the street becomes a town

The optimist agenda fails when there are many neighbours and the company can serve a slot another way.
On a street of three hundred households, each with a veto, every agent is unsure within a factor of two how much its neighbour minds.
A slot still goes ahead wherever what the company earns per household exceeds the most that slot could harm any household.
The company's sharper estimates close most remaining deals, but the rescued gain goes to the company.
When the company can serve any slot another way at a fixed cost, the margins become thin and deals vanish as the street lengthens.
In the street model with the other route available, the share of the possible gain kept falls to almost nothing at three hundred households.
The neighbours pay, in lost deals or in gains moved to the company.
The fixes on offer are a posted price per household, set at the most anyone could mind, which keeps the deal but loses part of the gain, or a court that assesses each harm, which restores the deal by taking away the right to refuse; the agents do neither, and Krier's reply, that real margins are often wide, concedes that a walk-away option makes them thin.

## When the company can go round the street

The optimist agenda fails when one side can walk away to something better than a quiet street.
The company can send its vans along the ring road at a known cost.
Every agent knows the most the company will pay, the detour's cost, so the neighbours' estimation is worth nothing.
The bargain covers only the small gain left once the detour is counted.
The company keeps the detour's value on top, and the neighbours, bargaining as a bloc, get the same small sum at every estimation budget.
In the street model, the other route raises what the company gets from no deal from nothing to 6.5.
The neighbours pay; each is left with a quarter of what the fair deal gave them before.
Krier's option fee is his fix; adopting it concedes that the fee prices the threat without removing it, and a threat to walk away is not something more speed, estimation, commitment or adaptation changes.

## When Carol is poor

The optimist agenda fails when wealth differs and harm is a loss of wellbeing, not money.
The company is rich, Alice and Bob have modest savings, and Carol almost none.
If lost sleep is a loss of money, the best schedule is the same whoever holds the right, and the fair deal splits the gain evenly in money.
If it is a loss of wellbeing, the same noise weighs more on Carol, and who holds the right decides the schedule.
If the neighbours hold it, Carol alone asks almost everything the daytime slots earn, and with Alice's and Bob's asks the total exceeds it, so only midday runs; if the company holds it, the neighbours cannot afford to buy back quiet, so all four slots run.
Carol takes the least money under every arrangement, as Krier says himself of a poor farmer who sells cheaply for cash now.
In the street model, measured in wellbeing, the product of gains is highest at a division that gives Carol the smallest share of anyone, 1.296.
Carol pays under either reading, and the money reading leaves her in debt when the company holds the right.

Dan, you need to choose the units, and the choice decides whose suffering the criterion can see.
Counted in money, Carol's gain looks like Alice's and Bob's; counted as the sum that would leave her as well off at her own wealth, she keeps only a third of it, since a harm counts for more the poorer its bearer.
Under uncertain estimates the outcome is a gamble, worth least to Carol.
No agent chooses between money, that money-equivalent sum, and wellbeing from a common start; the choice comes before the bargain.

## When holding the right is not enough

The optimist agenda fails when the council gives the neighbours the right to refuse but leaves the company the final offer.
Nothing runs without all three neighbours, so the right sits with them.
Yet the company's agent, with good estimates, offers each barely more than her harm.
Neighbours whose agents estimate ten times better than the company's gain nothing from it.
Under the two-signature rule, whoever proposes decides who in effect holds the right by choosing whom to leave out, and leaves out Carol unless she proposes.
In the street model, with all three neighbours holding the right to refuse, the company's offer drives each of them to about 0.03.
Every neighbour without the final offer pays, Carol most.
The fix is for whoever grants the right also to rotate the final offer, since the right alone fixes only what happens without a deal; the agents do not supply the rotation.

## When Bob's household signs as three

The optimist agenda fails when one household can run several agents and each counts as a party.
Bob registers three agents on the platform, one for each member of his household.
Under the all-must-agree rule each must be paid to sign, so the company pays Bob three times over.
As more households split, the product of gains per person falls.
The company can do the same with twenty agents if the platform counts agents and prices extra ones cheaply.
That depends on the platform's rule for dividing the gain, not on the agents, and the rule that stops it has no natural reading for a firm.
If the council sets consent at three of five agents, Bob's are the only agents that count, and Carol ends far below a quiet street.
In the street model, Bob's split household takes half of the whole gain under the all-must-agree rule.
Carol pays, most under the three-of-five rule.
The fix is the rule Petel calls one human, one principal, one agent, which the platform or the council must impose; the agents do not produce it, and adopting it concedes that the rule comes from outside the bargain.

## When the deal comes as one package

The optimist agenda fails when only the company can choose whether consents are bundled.
Snow's bike-lane case suggests bundling every slot into one agreement.
A package priced over all sixteen schedules catches Carol's morning-and-evening problem, which slot-by-slot agreement misses half the time.
It also commits, because a neighbour who reopens one slot risks her stake in the rest.
But it binds each party in proportion to its stake, and under the company's package offer the neighbours hold almost nothing, so only the company is bound, and the company, doing better slot by slot, offers a package only when the form requires one.
When a change in one slot's profits can reopen the bargain and the company's agent re-haggles faster, a package is reopened whole each time, so the fair deal wears away in a quarter of the time each slot's would take under four separate contracts, though in the long run the shares are the same, which makes it a loss over the contract's lifetime.
In the street model, reopening the evening slot costs a neighbour three and a half times as much under a package as slot by slot, when delay is costly.
Carol pays when there is no package, and whoever accepted poor terms pays when there is one.
The fix is a contract form that imposes bundling, which the platform must write; adopting Snow's device concedes that the commitment lasts only while renegotiation is slow and that it fixes whatever terms were agreed.

## When the street is one of many

The optimist agenda fails when the company bargains on many streets and the neighbours on one.
The first two cases below were computed; the rest were each worked out once by hand, unchecked.

Two or more streets can carry the same deliveries, and the company's agent offers one street the fair price and every other street only its harms.
Each street's agents commit to a demand, but a commitment does not always hold, so a street's holding out protects its share only while every other street holds too.
Each added street is one more commitment that must hold, so the neighbours' share falls geometrically with the number of streets.
In the model, a street's neighbours get nothing at all unless each neighbour's commitment holds at least 37 times in a hundred.
The neighbours on every street pay, in their share of the fair deal, because a veto protects little once the company can choose its street.
The fix is a club spanning every street the company can reach, formed before the first offer, or a rule against bargaining with several streets at once; the agents supply neither.

The company deals with street after street, its record public, so every street's agents see that it has never raised an offer after a refusal.
Even a slight suspicion that its final offer is really final then becomes a reputation, and each new street accepts close to nothing rather than test it.
In the model, with the company on a thousand streets, a street's neighbours end with about a ninth of what they would take if theirs were its only street.
The neighbours pay everywhere, and ten streets also give the company ten times their experience.
The fair deal returns only if the record is hidden, which the platform or the council must arrange, since here more public information lowers the neighbours' take.

In both cases nobody on any street falls below the ordinance; what the neighbours lose is their share of the fair deal, which is why the council sees no complaint.

The company also delivers the neighbours' groceries and can withhold them, so no deal now means no groceries either.
In the model, the fair deal over the larger package leaves Carol, who values the groceries most, worse off than a quiet street with groceries.
Carol pays, because the party with more than one thing to trade chooses the point the fair deal is measured from.

A platform score makes each refusal cost a neighbour in other bargains, so the company's agent offers each her harm less that cost.
In the model, every neighbour ends 1 worse off than a quiet street.
The neighbours pay, since a few platforms that see every bargain score each neighbour, while only one-off neighbours score the company.

All three neighbours hire one vendor, paid per closed deal, which accepts any offer that leaves them no worse off than a quiet street.
In the model, the company takes the whole 6 and each neighbour nothing; a vendor paid by client wealth gives up Carol's claim first.

The platform that writes the form is itself paid per closed deal and carries its choices to the next street.
In the model, it prefers whatever closes most deals over what does best by the product of gains, which suits its repeat customer, the company, at the neighbours' cost.

Part of the detour's cost is noise on the next street, which has no say, so when the company's own share is low the four parties send everything next door.
In the model, harm of 6 lands on the next street, which pays, while the product of gains among the four rises, most for the company, which owns the detour.

A neighbour may move away at a cost paid from her savings.
In the model, Alice and Bob can never be pushed below the cost of moving, and Carol, with wealth of 2, has no such floor, so Carol pays.

In each case the company, the party in many bargains, is favoured.

## Two things the agents do get right

### When the agent records every schedule

The optimist agenda holds when each agent records how much its neighbour minds every possible schedule, not one number per slot.
Carol's agent writes down her harm from each of the sixteen ways of running the four slots.
The company's agent reads that preference table and finds the best schedule, whatever shape Carol's harm takes.
The cost is estimation: the count of entries doubles with each slot added, so an hourly day is out of reach.
Real noise preferences, expressed as indices and event counts, fall between one number and the full preference table.
In the street model, the agent with the full preference table reaches the best product of gains available once Carol's combination harm is present, 2.44.
Carol gains most, because her harm is finally counted.
The limit is that someone other than the agents still chooses how preferences are described.

### When the agents search schedule and insulation together

The optimist agenda holds when the agents can trade in remedies as well as schedules.
Carol's window can be insulated, halving her exposure at a cost.
Decided on its own, insulation is never bought, because it saves Carol exactly what it costs.
Decided with the schedule, it makes night deliveries worth running.
Only a search over every combination of slots and insulation finds this, which is easy for an agent and hard for a person.
In the street model, the joint search raises the total gain from the deliveries by a quarter.
Everyone gains, under the fair deal and even where the company holds the final offer.
The limit is that the search enlarges the gain without changing who divides it.

## What the set says

Capable agents reach the fair deal only once a list of things they do not supply is in place: a veto for every neighbour, offers drawn by lot among persons, equal memory, tailored payments, a contract form and a description of preferences that carry every harm, verified harms, no walk-away option for one side, a decision about units, and one agent per person.
Somebody outside the bargain supplies each: the council, the platform, a court, or a club the neighbours join.
Each carries a distributional choice made before the bargain begins: who joins the club, who writes the form, who counts as one person, which damages a court uses.
Where any item is missing, more computation for the agents helps whoever already holds the advantage, usually the company, which speaks first, remembers most and bargains on many streets.
The first to lose is Carol, who is harmed most, is poorest, and has the harm that fits the form least.
Everything that computation adds, beyond the consent rule, takes the neighbours' share of the fair deal and leaves nobody worse off than the ordinance left them, so the agenda's failure against regulation is the consent rule's, and its failure against its own promise is everywhere else.
