# A referee letter on "Coasean street stories"

Dear Dan,

We read Krier first and the sketches second, and we liked the sketches more than we expected to. They do the thing we have spent much of our careers asking economists to do: they ask, of each exchange, who holds the short side, who can walk away, and who has to enforce what was agreed. But they share one assumption that we think weakens the whole set, and we will come back to it at the end. Everyone in these stories is self-regarding, and everyone's preferences are fixed before the bargaining starts. That is the picture Krier draws, and the sketches take it over without question. As a result they miss both the strongest resource available to the optimists and the strongest objection available to the critics.

## 1. The honeymoon

This is a sound story about proposer power, and it is the economics of the ultimatum game told as social history. The company's agent learns each household's reservation value and pushes its offers down to it. That is short-side power in our sense: the party whose "no" costs the other side more sets the terms. The rotating proposer is a known remedy; it is Baron and Ferejohn's random recognition rule (1989) given a civic setting. Your sketch is right that no agent would propose it.

But the story assumes that a household's agent accepts any offer above bare harm. Real responders do not. Since Güth, Schmittberger and Schwarze (1982), and across the fifteen small-scale societies that Henrich and colleagues studied (2001), people reject offers they consider insulting, at a cost to themselves. That is strong reciprocity, and proposers who learn about it offer more. So a faithful agent, one that carries Carol's sense of fairness as well as her harm, would put a floor under the company's learning curve. The real question is whether delegation keeps that motive or strips it out. De Melo, Marsella and Gratch (PNAS, 2019) found that people programming agents for social dilemmas chose more cooperative strategies than people who played directly. That gives the optimists something here. The sketch should face this evidence rather than leave the responder as a calculator. Verdict: credible, and fair to Krier, but it overstates how inevitable the slide is.

## 2. Seventy-five percent

This is one of the strongest sketches. The divide-and-rule logic is correct: once a pair's signatures are enough, the company prices out the household that minds most. Reference to the NSW strata laws is apt. We would push back on the pact's collapse, though. The sketch says the pact cannot survive "on trust alone" and needs an external enforcer. Ostrom, Walker and Gardner ("Covenants with and without a sword", APSR 1992) and Fehr and Gächter (AER 2000) show that groups keep agreements going without a sovereign when members can talk to each other and punish defectors at a cost to themselves. Neighbours who share a fence do exactly this. The sharper version of your story is that the agent layer removes the channels that make such sanctions possible. Alice's agent signs at three in the morning, and Alice never has to meet Carol's eye. On this reading the pact fails because the street has been made faceless, not because it lacks a platform to police it. That is a stronger claim, and it is ours to make rather than Krier's to rebut.

## 3. The form that cannot say "but not both"

This is a good story about incomplete contracts, and the disability-assessment comparison is apt. The precondition follows from the story. We would add the point about endogenous preferences. A schema that records harms one slot at a time does more than misrecord Carol. Over years it teaches households and their agents to think about harm in separable pieces, because those are the pieces that get paid. The double-glazing passage is the best thing in the sketch, but it pulls the other way and could be its own story. As it stands, the sketch ends up arguing both that the form fails and that the form, once fixed, works. Verdict: sound, but split in two.

## 4. Everybody's child has asthma

On this sketch we disagree most. The mechanism-design reasoning is correct: Carol is paid exactly her harm under the truthful deal, and a club paying one price favours whoever minds least. But two premises are weak.

The first is that Alice and Bob pad their claims "because their agents are competent". On a real street the neighbours know whose child has asthma. That is the kind of information communities hold and neither markets nor states can get, which is the argument of our "Social capital and community governance" (Economic Journal, 2002). The sketch says the remedy needs a bureaucrat who sees inside households, and that this is the planner Hayek warned about. But a third remedy is missing: the neighbours themselves, who already know. The trouble is that the protocol has no place for what they know.

The second is that padding is a fixed fact about Alice. Liberman, Samuels and Ross (2004) found that the same prisoner's dilemma, called the "Wall Street Game", produced far less cooperation than when it was called the "Community Game". Whether Alice pads depends partly on whether the street has been framed as a market. So the sketch is right that padding happens, but wrong about the reason. Padding is something the system brings about, not something it merely finds. Verdict: technically strong, but it lets Krier off the deeper charge.

## 5. Two towns, one depot

This is credible and long known: mobile capital collects the surplus of immobile communities. It is the tax-competition literature in the shape of a story, and it bears on the bargaining power that comes from the ability to exit, which we have written about in the setting of capital mobility. The HQ2 and Foxconn examples serve it well. Our one complaint is the claim that a regional compact is "a planner of the kind the Coasean programme was meant to retire." Krier keeps the state as "framework guarantor", and a rule against bidding wars is arguably exactly that. The sketch would be fairer, and harder to dismiss, if it argued that the framework the state has to guarantee is much larger than Krier assumes.

## 6. The boundary road

This is a clean story. The point that whoever opens a bargain decides who is a party is Ostrom's first design principle, clearly defined boundaries, turned against the outsiders. The renters left out of Krier's developer example are well observed. As in sketch 5, though, the remedy ("a court") is one Krier already concedes. The sketch should say what is new: that harm to non-parties becomes systematic and cheap once bargaining is cheap, so courts would face a flood of cases rather than the odd one.

## 7. Branch stacking

This is the weakest of the ten from where we stand. It is a Sybil attack, a real problem, but nothing about it is specific to Coasean bargaining. Krier's own examples are homeowners, and title registries already exist to settle who counts as one. The deeper material is in the final clause, about whether a renter or a child is a party. That belongs with sketch 6, where who counts is a question about power and not about fraud.

## 8. On commission

This sketch is strong, and it bears directly on contested exchange. Krier assumes the agent's loyalty can be bought on the market and policed by switching. We argued (Bowles and Gintis, "The revenge of homo economicus", JEP 1993) that when the promised performance cannot be verified by a third party, it is enforced endogenously, by the threat of termination. That threat works only if the principal can observe what the agent did. Your line that "a deal at zero gain arrives with a payment attached" states the problem exactly: the client cannot see the counterfactual, so the threat of switching carries no force. Levitt and Syverson (2008) is the right citation. This sketch is fair to Krier and goes beyond what he concedes.

## 9. Repeat players

This is the strongest sketch, and the one closest to our own work. The scoring of refusals is the employment rent written in another domain. In our model of the labour market, the employer gets work done without a complete contract because losing the job costs the worker something. In your street, the platform gets consent without a complete contract because refusing costs the household something in its other dealings. "The party with more than one thing to trade gets to choose what 'no deal' means" is as clear a statement of short-side power as we have read. Galanter (1974) is the right ancestor. We would cut the final paragraph's claim that none of this needs bad faith, or move it to the top, because it is the sketch's thesis.

## 10. The poor street

This is right and important, and it is what Bardhan, Bowles and Gintis (2000) argued about wealth constraints: who can be a residual claimant, and who can afford to wait, depends on wealth, and efficiency is not separable from distribution. The Summers memo is a fair example to use, and Krier's poor farmer makes the sketch fair to him. Because Carol cannot afford to move away, she ends with no floor at all. That point is the most important in the sketch and deserves more space. Our quarrel is with the last paragraph. "Even counted in wellbeing, the fairest division ... gives Carol the smallest share" depends on which fairness criterion you choose, and the sketch does not name it. A reader will want it named.

## Overall

The strongest sketches are 9, 2 and 8, and the weakest are 7 and the second half of 3. Many sketches end the same way: someone outside the bargain must set the rule. That is true, but it is the conclusion Krier half-accepts already. The essay would hit harder if it separated what Krier overlooked from what he conceded but undercosted.

The missing story is crowding out. Before the agents came, Carol could ask the driver to skip her side on bad nights, and he usually did. Bob took in her parcels. Once quiet has a price, this goodwill becomes a bargaining position. Frey and Oberholzer-Gee (AER 1997) found that offering Swiss residents compensation to host a nuclear waste site roughly halved their willingness to accept it. Gneezy and Rustichini's Haifa daycare fine (2000) increased the lateness it was meant to deter, and lateness stayed high after the fine was removed. A Coasean street may lose the civic motives that made the problem bearable before anyone priced it, and those motives may not return when the prices are withdrawn. This is the critique in The Moral Economy (Bowles, 2016), and none of your sketches makes it.

A second missing story is about monitoring. Krier treats enforcement as "near-instantaneous computational verification". Somebody has to check whether the van idled, whether it ran at 6:05, whether it was diesel. Whoever does the checking has power, and that is where contested exchange begins.

Does the set convince? As an account of how power re-enters a world of cheap bargaining, largely yes. As an argument that the preconditions are fragile, it is weaker than it should be, because it shares Krier's homo economicus. Having accepted that premise, the sketches cannot show the worst outcome, which is that pricing the street changes its people, nor the best defence, which is that people are not only the self-interested agents the model assumes.

With warm regards,
"B. & G."

## Three changes we would ask for

Add a story in which neighbourly goodwill is priced, and therefore crowded out, so that the essay confronts preferences that are formed by the institutions themselves rather than fixed in advance. Let at least one agent carry its principal's reciprocity, as in sketches 1 and 2, and show honestly whether that rescues the street or merely becomes one more parameter the company learns to price. Sort the remedies into those Krier already grants (courts, vouchers, baseline rights) and those he does not foresee (equal memory, limits on scoring, faithful representation of what neighbours know), and aim the essay at the second group.
