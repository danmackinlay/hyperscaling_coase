# A referee's reading of the ten street sketches

## Preliminary remark

Dear Dan, I read the sketches asking of each one what game is being played: who moves, who knows what, who can commit, and what happens on refusal. Almost every sketch, once its game is written down, turns out to be a known result. I do not mean that as a dismissal. A known theorem is a better foundation for a story than a simulation artefact, and you should say which one you are standing on. But it changes what the essay is claiming. The common thread is the one Farrell drew in "Information and the Coase Theorem" (1987): Coasean bargaining breaks down because of private information, limited commitment, and the extensive form, not because of transaction costs. Cheap agents lower transaction costs. They leave those three things where they were, or make them worse. That is a sharper thesis than "the preconditions are fragile," and the sketches would read better if they argued it.

I also have a fairness worry that applies throughout. Krier does not propose a stateless world. He wants the state as "framework guarantor": it sets property rights, contract law and the consent rule, and it runs the courts. Several of your preconditions are framework rules of exactly that kind: a lottery over proposers, unanimity, an identity registry. Calling each of them "a planner of the kind the programme was meant to retire" overstates the case, and Krier can simply concede them. The sketches hurt him only when the institution they need must hold *local* knowledge (an assessor who sees inside households) or must override the distributional result of the bargain. Keep those two cases apart and the set gets much harder to answer.

## 1. The honeymoon

Here the company makes take-it-or-leave-it offers to agents with private harms, and the rents follow a hump as the company's estimates improve. That is the textbook screening problem. While uncertainty is large, the proposer distorts and some efficient trades fail. As uncertainty shrinks, information rents fall to zero and the proposer ends up with first-degree price discrimination. The hump is real, but it is not a story about learning dynamics. It is comparative statics across information structures.

There are two problems. First, the sketch assumes the company can commit to one round. With fast agents and no commitment, the relevant result is the Coase conjecture (Gul, Sonnenschein and Wilson 1986): a proposer who can make a new offer moments after a refusal competes against its own future offers, and its power drains away. On that logic, cheap agents *help* the responders. The honeymoon needs a commitment device, and sketch 9 supplies one (reputation). Join the two sketches, or the story will be read as resting on a false premise. Second, "the night slot is never proposed" has to be an artefact of the incomplete-information region. Under complete information the proposer is the residual claimant and proposes the efficient schedule. If night running is efficient, a fully informed company proposes it and pays Carol her harm. Rotating the proposer is Baron–Ferejohn random recognition, where expected shares follow recognition probabilities. Krier should find it unobjectionable as a framework rule. Verdict: sound core, wrong dynamics, and missing the commitment assumption that drives it.

## 2. Seventy-five percent

This is my own territory, and I think it is the best sketch in the set. A principal contracts with several agents under a majority rule, and that rule imposes an externality on the people who do not sign. This is the setting of Segal (1999) "Contracting with Externalities" and Segal and Whinston (2000) on naked exclusion, following Rasmusen, Ramseyer and Wiley (1991). The clause "a token if everyone signs, full harm only if yours is the pivotal signature" is the divide-and-conquer scheme of Segal (2003): discriminatory offers make signing the unique equilibrium, and they do it cheaply. The pact unravels because, once Carol's harm drops out of a pair's accounts, the core of the residents' game is empty. That is a theorem. It does not depend on how good anyone's agent is, and the humanized note says as much. You should say so in the sketch, because it makes the point stronger: the failure comes from the consent rule, not from computation.

What is missing is the standard legal remedy. Supermajority schemes usually pay outvoted owners some compensation (the strata collective-sale regimes set compensation floors, and eminent domain uses market value). The interesting question is whether a compensation floor for dissenters restores Carol's position, and at what holdout cost. Kominers and Weyl (2012) on holdout in the assembly of complements study exactly that design space and belong here. The New South Wales example works. The "enforcer needs a leash" coda is right but quick.

## 3. The form that cannot say "but not both"

The final paragraph is the strongest part, and you undersell it. That an exhaustive preference table doubles with each slot is not a modelling inconvenience. It is the communication lower bound of Nisan and Segal (2006): when valuations have complementarities, any mechanism that reaches efficiency must in the worst case communicate an exponential amount. Prices are enough only when goods are substitutes. This answers Krier's Hayekian claim at its root. He says agents will communicate tacit preferences "with a nuance and specificity that is currently impossible." The theorem says that for preferences with complementarities, no protocol can do it cheaply, however clever the agents. Lead with that.

The middle paragraph is weaker. A company agent that keeps fitting an additive model while Carol refuses every offer is not learning. Any sensible learner would treat repeated refusal as evidence that its model is wrong. The fall from 54% to zero tells us about a fixed, misspecified hypothesis class, not about computation, and a referee would say so. The double-glazing result is a fair concession to Krier and an honest one. Keep it.

## 4. Everybody's child has asthma

This is the strongest sketch against Krier specifically. Carol, the highest-harm type, gets exactly her harm under the company's preferred incentive-compatible deal. That is the standard result that the least efficient type earns no information rent, and information rents go to the types with good private information. Nothing about agents changes it. The public-good version is the decisive one. A clean street that needs every household's consent, with private harms, voluntary participation and a balanced budget, is the setting of Mailath and Postlewaite (1990), where the probability of efficient provision goes to zero as the number of households grows. Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983) is the two-party case. Your unused town-scale result is Mailath–Postlewaite in miniature. Put it here.

You can push harder on the club. Krier says that in his bargaining club "padding your number doesn't boost your check" because everyone is paid the common clearing price. But if the price has to cover the largest ask, the largest asker sets everyone's price and has every reason to pad up to the buyer's willingness to pay. The holdout problem returns intact. The sketch argues the club is unfair to Carol. The prior point is that it is not incentive-compatible.

The two remedies are both known. Verification is costly state verification (Townsend 1979). The "standing instruction signed before anyone knows" is the move from interim to ex ante participation. Under ex ante participation, the expected-externality mechanism of d'Aspremont and Gérard-Varet (1979) achieves efficiency with a balanced budget, and Myerson–Satterthwaite no longer binds. You have that right, including that it fails for persistent types. The assessor is where the Hayekian argument turns back on Krier, because this institution needs exactly the local knowledge he denies the planner. That is the sketch's real force.

## 5. Two towns, one depot

The first half is the outside-option principle (Binmore, Shaked and Sutton 1989): an outside option affects the split only when it binds, and when it binds the holder keeps it. That is fine. The second half confuses distribution with efficiency. Towns competing for a mobile firm until each receives its bare harm is ordinary competition among sellers. It is efficient, and Tiebout-minded readers will call it healthy. The harm is distributional, and you should say that. HQ2 and Foxconn are about subsidies beyond compensation, which this model does not produce. Verdict: correct, but fair to Krier only as a claim about who gets the surplus.

## 6. The boundary road

This is externalities on non-traders again, now outside the coalition rather than inside it. It is Segal (1999) with the next street as the party left out. The Coase theorem needs everyone affected to be at the table, and the parties at the table can profit by leaving people out. The low-traffic-neighbourhood debate gives it an institutional anchor that rings true. The precondition, a liability rule enforced by a court, is Calabresi and Melamed (1972) on property versus liability rules, and naming them would help. One caution: "harm equal to the whole gain" is a parameter, not a result. Present it as an illustration. This is one of the strongest sketches, because who counts as affected cannot itself be bargained over.

## 7. Branch stacking

Correct and old. Under unanimity, splitting into more veto players raises a household's share, but only because per-head equal division rewards cloning; a per-person weighting would not. The renter question is the strongest line and belongs with sketch 6. Krier would grant the identity registry without much fuss.

## 8. On commission

This is a common-agency and credence-good problem. The client cannot observe the counterfactual, so competition does not discipline quality (Darby and Karni 1973). Levitt and Syverson (2008) is the right empirical anchor, and the Hayne royal commission's material on mortgage brokers fits. It answers Krier's "switching costs approaching zero" directly: switching needs observable performance. The sentence about wealth-proportional fees is asserted rather than shown. Strong.

## 9. Repeat players

The core is the reputation theorem of Fudenberg and Levine (1989). A long-lived player facing a stream of short-lived opponents can secure approximately its commitment payoff. That is the missing piece of sketch 1, and it is why the honeymoon's commitment assumption holds up. Cross-bargain scoring of refusals lowers each household's disagreement payoff. Tying deliveries to groceries is leverage of the Whinston (1990) kind and changes what "no deal" means. Each mechanism is sound, but four mechanisms in one sketch read like a list. Split out the reputation story and merge it with sketch 1.

## 10. The poor street

Without quasilinear utility, the allocation depends on the initial assignment of rights. This is the oldest qualification to Coase, and Krier states it himself, poor farmer included. So this sketch criticizes Krier least, though as a story about siting it is moving. The claim that "even the fairest division gives Carol the smallest share" depends on the solution concept. Under Nash bargaining, greater risk aversion lowers a party's share (Kihlstrom, Roth and Schmeidler 1981), and poverty works like risk aversion here. Cite that, or the reader will suspect an artefact. Your strongest line is the last one: Krier passes the hard choices to institutions he has just called captured.

## Overall

The strongest sketches are 2, 4 and 6. Each rests on a theorem rather than a parameter, each fails whatever the agents' quality, and 4 in particular needs an institution with the local knowledge Krier says no planner can have. The weakest are 5, which presents a distributional loss as a failure; 10, which Krier has already conceded; and 1 in its current form, which needs commitment it does not state.

The story I miss most is hold-up. Krier promises continuous renegotiation and in the same breath admits that "constant renegotiation of property rights would destroy investment incentives." Carol will not buy double glazing, and the company will not build a depot, if every agreement can be reopened next minute by faster agents. That is Grossman–Hart–Moore territory. Whether cheap renegotiation destroys the commitments that investment depends on is a question agents make *worse* by construction. I also miss a story built on correlated information. A company with data from a thousand streets sees harms that are correlated across neighbours. Crémer and McLean (1988) show that a principal can then extract the full surplus using side-bets on the other neighbours' reports. That is the darkest outcome of "better estimation," and it is a theorem.

Does the set convince? It convinces me that Krier's diagnosis is wrong: the binding frictions are information, commitment and the definition of who is a party, not transaction costs. It does not yet show that the cure is a planner. Most of the remedies are framework rules, which Krier welcomes. The case against him rests on the few that are not.

## Three changes I would ask for

Name the known result under each sketch (divide and conquer, Mailath–Postlewaite, Nisan–Segal, Fudenberg–Levine), and make clear which outcomes are theorems and which are parameters of the toy model. Separate the preconditions Krier's framework guarantor can supply from those that need local knowledge or a distributional override, and aim the essay at the latter. Repair sketch 1 by stating the commitment assumption and merging in the reputation half of sketch 9, and add a hold-up story about renegotiation and investment.
