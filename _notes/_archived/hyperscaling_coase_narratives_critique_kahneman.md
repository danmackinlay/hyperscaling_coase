# A psychologist reads the Coasean street stories

Dear Dan,

I read Krier first and then your ten sketches, and I will start with my general reservation, because it changes how I read almost every story. Your model, like Krier's essay, treats each household's harm as a number the agent can learn, estimate or misreport. Carol minds the vans by 9, Alice by 2. The agents' job is to find that number, and the question is who gets to exploit what they find. My field has spent fifty years showing that for goods like quiet, clean air and a neighbourhood's character there is often no such number waiting to be found. Lichtenstein and Slovic's work on preference reversals, and their 2006 collection *The Construction of Preference*, show that stated values depend on how the question is asked. Kahneman, Ritov and Schkade (1999) argued that contingent-valuation answers about public goods express attitudes more than they report prices. Willingness to accept a loss exceeds willingness to pay for the same good by large factors. The gap is widest for non-market goods (Horowitz and McConnell's 2002 meta-analysis), so whether Carol's harm is 9 depends on whether she is selling her quiet or buying it back. Krier sees this. He asks whether agents should "debias" negotiations or faithfully represent our quirks, and then he leaves the question open. Your sketches leave it closed. That is the largest gap in the set, and I come back to it at the end.

My second reservation follows from the first. The principals in these stories hardly ever react as people. They are paid, outvoted, underpaid and scored, and nobody gets angry, refuses on principle or goes to the newspapers. Real responders reject unfair ultimatum offers at a cost to themselves (Güth, Schmittberger and Schwarze 1982, and many replications since). Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler (1986) found that the public treats a firm's exploitation of a shift in its bargaining power as unfair, even when the price is still above the reference price. Those reactions cut both ways for your argument, as I explain below.

## 1. The honeymoon

The mechanism is well observed, and the ride-hail comparison is apt. From my standpoint the sketch understates its own point and then misses a consequence. Once the margin has paid for Bob's bike, it becomes the reference point. When the margin shrinks back toward bare harm, nobody experiences that as a return to a fair baseline. It is experienced as a loss, and losses loom larger than gains of the same size. The dual-entitlement principle of Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler predicts that the street will judge the company's tightening offers unfair, because the company is using an advantage it has gained to cut a payment people have come to count on. So the political reaction comes earlier and louder than the sketch implies.

The consequence is a dilemma the model avoids. If the neighbours' agents faithfully carry their principals' preferences, those preferences include a distaste for being offered bare harm, and the agents will reject such offers as human responders do. In that case the company's extraction is bounded by the street's sense of fairness, and Krier is more right than the sketch allows. If the agents do not carry those preferences, they are not faithful agents, and the problem lies in the specification of loyalty, not in who makes the final offer. You need to say which horn you are on.

I also doubt that people find the rotating lottery "odd". Taking turns is about the oldest fairness procedure there is, and lotteries are widely accepted where desert cannot be assessed. The neighbours would more likely see it as fair than as a casino. Note too that in Krier's own asthma example the household broadcasts a price, which means the residents make the offer. Your consultation story explains how the protocol came to be inverted, and that is fair to him, but say plainly that the inversion is the point.

## 2. Seventy-five percent

The New South Wales parallel is good and the coalition logic is sound. The weak step is the pact. You say it collapses within weeks without anyone behaving badly, because no division of the proceeds keeps every member in. That holds for payoff-maximizers. Groups of human beings sustain cooperation well beyond what payoffs alone predict, through conditional cooperation and the willingness to punish defectors at a cost to themselves (Fehr and Gächter 2000), and Ostrom documented exactly such communities. What makes the pact fragile in your world is the agents. Hamman, Loewenstein and Weber (2010) showed that people who delegate a choice to an agent end up more self-interested than people who choose for themselves, because the delegation absorbs the moral discomfort. A pact among neighbours who see each other at the bus stop can survive. A pact among their agents has none of the shame or anger that holds human pacts together. Tell it that way and the sketch becomes stronger and more original.

## 3. The form that cannot say "but not both"

This is the best sketch in the set from where I sit, and its best sentence is the one about disability assessments: the claimant can walk to the shops, once. The schema decides what a preference may look like, and that is the construction-of-preference point told as an institutional story. My objection is to the cure. You write that once Carol's agent records her harm for all sixteen schedules, the optimist picture works. That assumes Carol has sixteen stable numbers. She does not. Asked to rate sixteen schedules, she will produce answers driven by contrast effects, by the order of presentation and by whichever slot she pictures first, and asked again next week she will produce different ones. The table has a more basic limit than combinatorial explosion: its entries are made up at the moment they are elicited. The glazing example is good, but notice that it depends on Carol's discomfort being measurable in the same units before and after the window, which is a strong assumption.

## 4. Everybody's child has asthma

The arithmetic is correct, and the conclusion that a common price transfers to whoever minds least is valuable and not widely known. The behavioural premise, that within a season every agent claims asthma-level harm, is more plausible with agents than it would be among people. Delegation makes exaggeration feel like someone else's act, and that strengthens your story. But the proposed remedy, verification, needs a psychologist's warning. Assessors are noisy. In *Noise* (Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein 2021) we documented how much professionals vary when judging identical cases, from sentencing to underwriting. An assessor of how much a household minds would be both intrusive and inconsistent, and you should say so. The insurance remedy, signed before anyone knows who will be harmed, also runs into the fact that people are poor at forecasting their own reactions to noise. I return to this under sketch 10.

## 5. Two towns, one depot

This is correct, and it is the old story of tax competition. I have little to add as a psychologist, apart from the winner's curse and the overconfidence of town negotiators who believe their bid will pay off, which only reinforce your point. It is fair to Krier. It is also the sketch where agents matter least, because they only make the bidding war run faster, and that makes it weaker as a criticism of his particular claim.

## 6. The boundary road

It is credible and it fits the low-traffic-neighbourhood debate well. One psychological point strengthens it. People judge harm caused by commission more harshly than equal harm caused by omission (Spranca, Minsk and Baron 1991), and they respond more to identified victims than to statistical ones. The next street's residents are unnamed, and their harm follows from leaving them out, not from any act anyone performed. The parties to the bargain will not feel they have done anything, which is why the exclusion will hold. The precondition does follow.

## 7. Branch stacking

The institutional point is sound and the Australian name is useful. Psychologically it is the least worrying sketch, because people readily detect and resent this kind of cheating, and outrage at stacking is what drives the rule changes you describe. The more interesting question you raise in passing is whether renters and children count as parties, and that deserves more space than it gets.

## 8. On commission

This is the second strongest, and its key sentence is that the client never sees what the agent could have got. Hsee's (1996) work on evaluability explains why. An outcome judged on its own, with no comparison, is judged by its easily evaluated features: a deal closed and a payment received. Its size relative to what was possible cannot be seen. Levitt and Syverson (2008) is cited correctly. Krier's claim that competition will clear out parasitic agents assumes that customers can evaluate their agents, and this sketch shows that they cannot.

## 9. Repeat players

Galanter is the right ancestor and the story is credible. Scoring households by their refusals is the most behaviourally pointed device in the set. It turns refusal from a neutral option into a loss, and loss aversion makes that penalty work out of proportion to its size. The tying point is true but familiar from competition law. Of the fixes you list, equal access to the record of past deals is the one that addresses the psychology.

## 10. The poor street

The wealth point is fair to Krier, who raised it himself. What the persona adds concerns the unit you ask someone to choose. You frame the choice as dollars against what the dollars are worth to the person receiving them. There is a third option, which is the one that matters for Carol: experienced utility, meaning how her nights actually go, as against the decision utility her agent acts on when the rent is due. Mullainathan and Shafir's work on scarcity (2013) suggests that a pressed decision maker focuses on the immediate shortfall, so a faithful agent will sell the quiet at exactly the moment Carol's judgment of its value is worst. Noise is also one of the few nuisances to which people do not fully adapt, so the sleepless nights she sells now will not feel easier later. This is a strong sketch with a missing sentence: the agent is faithful to the wrong self.

## What is missing, and the verdict

The story I want is the one Krier himself points to and then sets aside. Call it "The price of quiet". Bob does not much mind the vans until a fee arrives for them. Once his annoyance has a price it becomes something he owns, and he notices every van and resents each one. Paying people for a nuisance changes the nuisance. Frey and Oberholzer-Gee (1997) found that support among Swiss residents for hosting a nuclear waste repository roughly halved when compensation was offered, and Gneezy and Rustichini (2000) found that a fine for late pickup at day-care centres increased lateness, because the fine turned a moral obligation into a price. Krier's whole programme assumes that putting a price on an externality reveals preferences that already exist. This literature shows that the price can create or destroy those preferences. That is a deeper problem for the optimist agenda than any of your ten, and it needs no model of agents at all.

The strongest sketches from this standpoint are 3, 8 and 1. In each, the trouble arises because a person cannot see, state or keep hold of her own interest in the form the system demands. The weakest are 2, whose pact collapses only because its members are assumed to be indifferent to fairness, and 5, which is true but owes nothing to agents. As a set they convince me of the claim that every good outcome needs a rule set from outside the bargain. Read together, they do not convince me of something they imply without stating it: that the trouble lies in the institutions while the preferences are fine. Each sketch treats the agent as a faithful courier of a number. My literature says the number is partly made at the moment the courier asks for it.

## Three changes I would ask for

First, add a sketch in which pricing a nuisance changes how much people mind it, built on the crowding-out evidence, because it attacks Krier's premise rather than his institutions. Second, decide in sketch 1 whether agents carry their principals' sense of fairness, and say what follows either way, since one answer weakens your argument and the other moves the problem into the definition of loyalty. Third, in sketches 3, 4 and 10, replace the assumption of stable known harms with elicited ones that are noisy and depend on framing, so that the fixes you propose (complete tables, assessors, wellbeing units) are shown at their real cost.
