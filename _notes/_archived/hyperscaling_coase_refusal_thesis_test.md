# Referee test: the refusal thesis

Written 2026-11-18 by a sceptical referee agent. It tests one candidate thesis for the Krier response essay against Krier's text, the three published critiques, and the literature. It edits nothing else.

## 6. Verdict and narrowed statement

As stated, the thesis fails. Its central sentence, that the asymmetry "is created by delegation, not merely amplified by it", is wrong in standard theory, where delegation is the textbook way a weak bargainer *acquires* commitment. Its empirical premise, that an agent built for its principal's payoff "accepts anything better than no deal", is true of one deployed model in one study and false of most. And its mechanism is not new. Fershtman and Gneezy showed in 2001 that a responder who delegates *unobservably* gets a smaller share, because proposers expect the delegate to swallow tough offers that the principal would have refused. The thesis is that experiment restated for language models.

A narrower thesis survives, and I would defend it:

> Refusing an insulting offer is a commitment people hold without choosing it and cannot easily revise; a delegated agent turns it into a setting that the principal chooses and can change at any moment. A setting commits only if the other side can see it and believes it will not be changed mid-bargain, so delegation strengthens the weak side where agent instructions are attested and locked, and weakens it where the company's agent faces a household agent it believes to be a revisable payoff-maximiser. Krier's open question, whether agents should "debias" negotiations or carry our quirks, is therefore not a question about psychology but about who supplies the attestation, and it decides the split by default.

For this to matter, three things must be true of deployed agents: household agents must default to near-sequentially-rational acceptance (some do, many do not); their instructions must be invisible or revisable to the counterparty; and the company's policy must be credible, through a public record or a locked rule. The cheapest test is a 2×2 run of the street with LLM agents, described at the end of section 4.

## 1. Theory

Schelling's point in *The Strategy of Conflict* was that the power to bind oneself is bargaining power, and that sending an agent with limited authority is one of the standard ways to bind oneself. Frank (1988) added that emotions such as anger at unfairness work as commitment devices precisely because they are hard to fake and hard to switch off: the angry responder's threat to refuse is credible because refusing is not a calculation. The thesis rests on Frank, and that part is sound. A sequentially rational responder in a one-shot ultimatum game accepts any positive offer; the human responder's temper is what keeps proposers honest.

The strategic delegation literature then asks what happens when a principal sends an agent. Fershtman and Judd (1987) showed that when the agent's incentive contract is observable, a principal gains by instructing the agent to be more aggressive than the principal would be. Katz (1991) showed the limit: if the contract is unobservable, delegation gives no commitment in Nash equilibrium outcomes, because the opponent cannot condition on what it cannot see, and renegotiation further erodes it. So the theory does not say delegation weakens the weak side. It says delegation strengthens whoever delegates *if the instruction is observable and hard to revise*, and does nothing otherwise.

The thesis can be rescued only by adding something the theory treats as exogenous: what the opponent *believes* about an unobserved agent. Fershtman and Gneezy (2001) supply exactly that. In their ultimatum experiment, a proposer who delegated gained share; a responder who delegated with an unobservable contract lost share, because proposers expected the delegate to be less willing to punish an unfair offer made to someone else. A third-party-responder treatment confirmed the mechanism. That is the thesis's asymmetry, and it is not "created by delegation" in general. It is created by delegation under unobservability combined with a belief that the agent lacks the principal's spite. With observable, committed instructions the sign flips: Chlaß and Miettinen (2026) let responders pre-commit to a minimum acceptable offer before the proposer moves, and responders gained, though less than theory predicts.

The company's side needs the same treatment. Its "reputation for never raising" is a commitment built across bargains, and it is the Boulwarism that the National Labor Relations Board condemned and the Second Circuit upheld in *NLRB v. General Electric* (1969). That case shows a legal system once treated a repeat player's fixed offer as a bargaining wrong, but only under a statutory duty to bargain in good faith that has no counterpart in Krier's private deals. When both sides can commit cheaply, Ellingsen and Miettinen (2008) predict conflict rather than a clean win for either, which is close to the essay's existing "race" finding.

## 2. Evidence

What the claims list says about LLM responders is mostly mis-sourced. The 0.23 per cent figure is real but does not come from a survey of negotiating agents. It is the estimated constant for GPT-5 mini's *stated* minimum acceptable share in Araujo and Uhlig's NBER working paper (w34919, revised November 2026), where models are asked "what is the lowest share … I should accept". The same table gives DeepSeek R1 about 16 per cent, Gemma 3 about 12, the two Llama models about 36 and 10, and Mistral 7B about 47, insisting on an even split. The authors describe a general tilt toward "Spock mode", but most models keep a non-negligible floor, and the floors move with stakes and with whether the counterparty is described as human or AI. This is one study, of stated thresholds rather than play, on mostly small open-weight models. Earlier work found GPT models rejecting unfair offers much as humans do (arXiv 2305.05516, search abstract only).

The claim that consumers' assistants accept "the first proposal far more often than the best one" is a fair reading of Microsoft's Magentic Marketplace (arXiv 2510.25779; I read the blog post), which found that all models tested showed a strong preference for the first proposal received. But that is a search failure, not responder rationality; it says the household's agent may accept early, not that it accepts anything above zero. I found no source for the claim that firms' agents are "limited in what they may concede" while consumers' accommodate; treat it as unverified.

The contrary lead, Liang and Xu (arXiv 2608.07538, abstract and introduction read), does not say what the claims list reports. It finds agreement in 98.9 per cent of 9,840 LLM-to-LLM supply-chain negotiations; provider identity rather than capability predicting who captures surplus (buyer shares of 40, 50 and 70 per cent in self-play for OpenAI, Google and Qwen); weak models accepting individually irrational contracts 19.2 per cent of the time; and the prompted patience parameter explaining 90 per cent of the designed variation in surplus division. I could not confirm "nearer an even split than theory predicts". What it does show cuts against the thesis: the principal's prompt is a cheap, powerful strategic lever, which is exactly strategic delegation at work. It also shows a failure the thesis does not name: weak agents that accept *losing* deals, which is worse than accepting any positive offer.

Established, then: model behaviour as a responder varies a lot by provider and prompt, and the prompt moves outcomes more than anything else measured. One study: GPT-5 mini's near-zero stated floor. Unverified: the firm-versus-consumer concession claim, and the even-split claim.

## 3. Is the human refusal really there in Krier's cases?

The lab figures are roughly right. Offers below about a fifth of the pie are rejected around half the time in standard ultimatum games. But the claim that refusals "fall away when refusing no longer hurts the proposer" is contested: Bolton and Zwick (1995) found near-equilibrium play in their impunity game, while Yamagishi and colleagues (2009, PNAS) found substantial rejection persisting in impunity and private impunity games, and read it as emotional commitment rather than punishment. That supports Frank but undermines the earlier review's tidy story.

More damaging is stakes. Andersen and colleagues (2011, AER) ran ultimatum games in Indian villages with stakes up to many months' income; rejection rates fell sharply as stakes rose, and at the highest stake only one responder in 24 rejected a positive offer. Carol is the poorest household. The free commitment is weakest for the person the thesis most wants to protect, and weakest when the money matters to her. Krier makes the same point in his own words about the poor farmer who sells cheaply "because they need cash now".

In the field, homeowners facing a developer do extract surplus: land sold into assemblies trades at an 18 per cent premium in Seattle (Cunningham 2013) and 15 to 40 per cent in Los Angeles (Brooks and Lutz 2016), falling with a parcel's share of the site. But that premium comes from being pivotal under unanimity, and developers answer it with straw purchasers who hide the assembly. Spite is not visibly doing the work; pivotality is. A delegated agent keeps pivotality. So in Krier's settings the laboratory comparison overstates what humans currently hold, and the thing they do hold survives delegation.

## 4. The strongest objections

*(a) The household instructs its agent to refuse below a floor, and proves it.* This is the strongest objection, and the thesis as stated does not survive it. Fershtman and Judd, Chlaß and Miettinen, and Liang and Xu's patience lever all say that an observable instruction is a better commitment than temper: it is chosen rather than accidental, it can be set at the right level, and it does not wilt under stakes as human spite does. What the thesis can keep is the qualifier "and proves it". A prompt can be rewritten mid-bargain, and the company's agent knows the principal can override. Temper was credible because it was hard to revise; a setting is credible only if something makes it hard to revise, such as an attested configuration, an escrow, or a bond. That moves the thesis onto ground the essay already holds (escrow as the institution, a hash as no revocation cost), and onto Petel's disagreement with it.

*(b) Krier's market argument.* Krier writes that competition "will be the primary force pushing agents to become powerful and loyal advocates". The objection has force: if floors help users, providers will offer them. The thesis survives in part. A floor helps only when the counterparty believes it, so its value depends on an attestation standard rather than on any one provider's choice; and the cost of a floor, failed deals, is visible to the user while its benefit, offers that would have been lower, is not. Market selection on visible outcomes will tend to select against floors. For Carol, who may be on Krier's voucher agent, the default is set by a procurer, not by her shopping.

*(c) The company's reputation is not untouched.* Correct, and the thesis must concede "untouched". Agents on the other side can read the company's whole record, and Krier's own coalition, declaring a price "non-negotiable", is a cross-street commitment that answers a cross-street reputation. The concession is not fatal: that coalition is a commitment made by delegation, which again shows that delegation can create commitment for the weak side rather than remove it.

*(d) Three floors collide.* This survives as support for the thesis's trade-off clause. Floors set individually under unanimity can sum to more than the company's surplus, so the remedy is not available to the households acting separately. But it is the essay's existing unanimity arithmetic and Snow's point that a resident bluffing "like a billionaire" makes the deal collapse, so it adds little that is new. Note also that Chlaß and Miettinen found near-complete efficiency in late rounds when responders could pre-commit: a single observed floor met by a proposer who can see it does not make deals fail. Failure needs unobserved floors, simultaneous commitment, or several floors under unanimity.

*The test.* Run the street with LLM agents: one company agent making take-it-or-leave-it offers, three household agents with Carol's harm largest. Cross two factors: household agent instructed to maximise payoff versus given a floor; floor visible and locked to the company agent versus private or declared revisable by the principal. Measure Carol's payment and the deal rate across a few hundred runs and two providers. The narrowed thesis predicts Carol does worst under the private payoff-maximiser, best under the visible locked floor, and that only the third household's floor, not the first two, drives failure. This is Fershtman and Gneezy's design, on the street.

## 5. Novelty

The mechanism is in print. Fershtman and Gneezy (2001) is the nearest statement: unobserved responder delegates are seen as more willing to accept tough offers, so the responder's share falls. The domain-specific version is partly in print too. Domingos and colleagues' 2022 review summarises work in which people design negotiating agents closer to selfish game-theoretic play than they themselves behave, against de Melo and colleagues' finding that people program machines to be fairer. Eidenmüller's "Game Over" essay argues that AI will turn negotiation into endgames that large businesses win with "cool logic and precision", without the commitment mechanism. Among Krier's critics, Snow's "leverage, not strategy" passage is near on the trade-off, and Ngo's remark that commitment and disagreement points decide bargaining is near on the frame. I found nothing that links Krier's "debias or quirks" question to the division of surplus via commitment, or that names attestation of agent instructions as the variable deciding whether delegation helps the weak side. That link is the essay's possible contribution, and it should cite Fershtman and Gneezy as the mechanism.
