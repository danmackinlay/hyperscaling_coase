# Krier through the theory of incomplete contracts

## 1. What Krier is claiming, in this theory's terms

Krier's essay runs two pictures together, and they pull in opposite directions. The first is a picture of complete contracts. Transaction costs block bargains because "we can't cheaply find each other, state exact terms, and lock in a deal", and the remedy he names is "hyper-granular contracting": the agent knows you "tolerate loud music on a Saturday, but not on a Sunday" and writes that in. Enforcement, he hopes, becomes "a near-instantaneous computational verification." Taken together, these passages say that agents can write down every contingency and have it checked by machine. That is the claim that contracts become complete.

The second picture is a spot market. Under "continuous self-calibration", rules "flex in real time", prices "slide automatically as new bids and conditions roll in", and governance moves "from statute to thermostat." A thermostat is no long-term contract. It is perpetual recontracting at current valuations, and in our theory that is exactly the regime in which hold-up does its damage. A complete contract would never need repricing, because the repricing would already be written into it.

Krier hedges in three places. He concedes that "constant renegotiation of property rights would destroy investment incentives." He keeps a court for the case where the truck's agent "agrees to the 'clean air fee' but the company refuses to pay." And he says that agents make the baseline entitlements "explicit, contestable, and cheap to renegotiate." The first two hedges admit that incompleteness remains. The third, as I argue below, makes the problem worse.

## 2. Which causes of incompleteness cheap agents remove, leave, or worsen

*The cost of thinking through and writing contingencies.* Here cheap cognition genuinely helps, and Krier is right about it. The foundations literature, however, put this cause aside some time ago, and the reason matters. Segal's "Complexity and Renegotiation" (1999) and Hart and Moore's "Foundations of Incomplete Contracts" (1999) show that when the range of possible trades is large and the relevant one is identified only after the event, the best contract can be no contract at all, *even when writing is free*. What drives the result is that the parties cannot commit not to renegotiate. The cost of writing has nothing to do with it. Making writing cheap therefore removes the cause that did no work in the theory.

*Unforeseen contingencies.* Maskin and Tirole (1999) argue that unforeseen contingencies are irrelevant if the parties can foresee their *payoffs*: a message game played after the event can implement whatever a complete contract would have implemented. On its face, agents that model their principals well bring us closer to Maskin and Tirole's world. Their result, though, needs two things that Krier's architecture does not supply. It needs a third party to enforce the outcome of the message game. It also needs the parties to commit not to renegotiate that outcome, and this is where Hart and Moore and Segal place the whole difficulty. The two sides of the foundations debate therefore converge on one point for present purposes. Whichever side is right about foresight, renegotiation is the binding constraint. Aghion, Fudenberg, Holden, Kunimoto and Tercieux (QJE 2012) add that those mechanisms break under slightly imperfect information.

*Non-verifiability.* Agents leave this almost untouched, and may widen it. Krier's "computational verification" checks that the payment was made. It does not check that Carol slept badly. A noise sensor makes decibels at the window verifiable, but harm is a valuation, and only Carol's own agent reports it. Cheap agents make a great deal *observable* to both sides, since each agent models the other, while making little more *verifiable* to a court. Observable but not verifiable is precisely the condition under which Grossman and Hart's and Hart and Moore's models apply.

*Private information.* Agents shrink it by estimation, and Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983) still bounds what any voluntary, budget-balanced mechanism can achieve. Snow has already made the leverage point, so I leave it with her.

*Inability to commit not to renegotiate.* This cause gets worse. The cost of reopening a deal was itself a commitment device, a crude one paid in lawyers' time. Lower that cost and the commitment it bought goes with it. Agents with escrow make it cheaper to commit to *performance*: the money is held, and the van pays or does not run. They make it no cheaper to commit against *renegotiation*. Renegotiation happens by mutual consent and improves matters for both parties after the event, so no escrow will refuse to release funds that both parties ask it to release.

## 3. Who holds residual control in Krier's architecture

In Grossman and Hart (1986) and Hart and Moore (1990), whoever holds the residual rights of control decides whatever the contract left unsaid. That right determines bargaining positions after the event, and through them, who invests beforehand. Segal and Whinston's handbook treatment says the same thing more generally: property rights are an allocation of bargaining positions. Applied to Krier's three nested layers, the theory finds residual control in places he does not look.

The state holds the default entitlement (whether the street has a right to quiet), and Krier is explicit that this is a "constitutional choice." But the essential gap-filling happens one level down. The platform or consortium that writes the protocol decides which variables can be contracted on at all: whether a preference can say "either, but not both" (Dan's sketch 3), and who makes the final offer (sketch 1). That is control over the space of contracts, prior even to Grossman and Hart's residual rights. Aghion, Dewatripont and Rey (Econometrica 1994) show that an ex ante allocation of bargaining power can restore efficient investment despite renegotiation. In Krier's world that allocation is made by whoever writes the protocol, and no party to the bargain makes it. The model developer fills the gaps in instruction, deciding what the agent concedes when its principal said nothing.

Aghion and Tirole's (1997) distinction between formal and real authority fits closely. The user holds formal authority over her agent. Real authority belongs to whoever has the information and the attention, and that is the agent and its provider: Snow's resident who approves forty-seven deals in a batch. The theory predicts that the unspecified surplus goes to these holders of residual control, who invest in their own positions (protocols, defaults, data across a thousand streets), while everyone else underinvests in anything specific to the relationship.

## 4. Hold-up, and Krier's proposal to price stability

Krier's remedy is to "explicitly price the value of stability versus flexibility, letting some rules ossify by mutual agreement." The trouble is that an agreement not to renegotiate is itself a contract, and the same parties can renegotiate it by the same mutual agreement. Contracting on one's own ability to renegotiate is exactly what the literature that began with Hart and Moore (1988) and Dewatripont (1989) found cannot be done from inside the relationship. The commitment has to come from something the parties cannot undo together: a court that will not enforce the modification, a third party with a stake (who can then be bribed into the renegotiation), or a physical irreversibility.

Krier's analogy with options markets fails for the same reason. An option prices volatility in an underlying asset that its holders do not control. Here the volatility *is* the holders' own future choices. His fallback, that agents "would likely converge on stable equilibria … simply to reduce computational overhead", grounds stability in the very cost of renegotiation that the essay's premise says is going away.

Look also at what he would ossify and what he would leave open. "Basic property rights, contract enforcement" ossify, and those are supplied by the outer doll, the state; they are not bargained. "Noise ordinances, parking rules" stay "perpetually negotiable", and those are the domains where households make small, specific investments: which room to sleep in, whether to double-glaze, whether to buy on this street at all. Che and Hausch (1999) add the sharpest version of the point. When an investment benefits the *other* party (a cooperative investment), the possibility of renegotiation can make contracting entirely worthless, and parties do no better than with no contract at all.

## 5. Testing the candidate thesis

The thesis is nearly right, but two sentences overreach and should be narrowed.

"A rule is worth something because it cannot be renegotiated" is too broad. In this theory, non-renegotiability is worth something only when there is a non-contractible investment specific to the relationship that needs protecting. Saturday music involves none, and for it Krier's flexibility is a pure gain. The thesis should therefore be confined to domains where someone must sink a specific investment before the price is settled.

"Harder to promise not to reopen a deal" is right, provided it is kept apart from the promise to *perform*. Here lies the strongest objection, and the thesis has to meet it. Its advocate says that agents with escrow, bonds and automated enforcement make commitment cheaper. They also have the theory on their side in one important case. Nöldeke and Schmidt (1995) and Edlin and Reichelstein (1996) show that when an investment benefits mainly the investor, a simple fixed contract enforced by specific performance yields efficient investment *even with renegotiation*, because the investor can veto any reopening. Add Maskin and Tirole, and the advocate concludes that agents with foresight plus escrow sign such contracts routinely.

The thesis survives this objection only in narrowed form. First, the escrow remedy works by switching the thermostat off for the term of the contract, so it concedes that flexibility and investment trade off; it does not abolish the trade-off. Second, it fails when trade is not verifiable, and when the investment is cooperative (Che and Hausch). Third, and most important on a street, it binds only today's parties. A deal between agents does not bind the household that buys Bob's house next year, and it does not bind the council or the platform that changes the consent rule. Commitment that runs to successors is what property law supplies through covenants that run with the land. That is a function of the outer doll, and Krier proposes to make it "cheap to renegotiate."

On who collects, I would put it more precisely. The party who collects is the one that has not yet sunk its investment and can commit by reputation across many bargains: the company working a thousand streets can refuse to be held up on any of them. A single household, which sinks its investment first and bargains only once, cannot.

Is this already in print? Ngo makes the general point that commitments and disagreement points decide bargaining outcomes, and Petel treats commitment as a friction that agents remove; neither applies hold-up or residual control to Krier's architecture. Holden and Malani (NBER WP 25833, 2019) ask whether blockchain contracts can solve hold-up and, as I read them, locate the answer in whether code can stop the parties from renegotiating; the author should check their exact claims before citing. I know of no published critique of Krier along these lines.

A narrowed statement that I would defend: *Cheap agents make contracts cheaper to write and to perform, but no harder to reopen, and they make it cheaper to reopen the rules beneath the contracts; so wherever someone must sink an investment specific to the street before the price is fixed, Krier's thermostat must be switched off, and the power to keep it off belongs to whoever writes the rules, not to the bargainers.*

## 6. One example to carry it

Carol's double glazing works, but only if its game is stated precisely. Carol invests. The glazing is specific: on its own it saves her exactly what it costs, so its whole extra return comes from the night run. Under Krier's thermostat, Carol's agent streams her current harm. Once the glazing is in, her harm from the night run falls, her price falls with it, and the company's agent pays her the new, lower figure. Carol bears the cost and the company collects the saving. She foresees this and does not glaze, and the night run never happens. That is hold-up in its textbook form.

As it stands, though, the example invites the Edlin–Reichelstein reply: sign Carol to a five-year fixed contract for the night run, enforced by escrow, and she glazes. So extend it by one move. In year two Bob sells his house to Dave, whose agent signed nothing. Under unanimity, Dave's veto reopens the night slot; under the seventy-five percent rule (sketch 2), the protocol's owner can reallocate the night slot over Carol's objection. Either way the deal is reopened by someone who was never a party to it, and Carol's glazing is already sunk. Carol could have been protected only by a commitment that binds successors and the people who write the rules, which is a property right held by the state. A cheaper agent would not have helped her.

One check remains for the model. The glazing must be a selfish investment, one that lowers Carol's own harm, so that the fixed contract would have worked without Dave. That is what makes the successor do the decisive work.

## What I would tell the author in three sentences

Krier sells a thermostat, which means cheap continual repricing, and the theory of incomplete contracts says repricing after someone has sunk an investment is hold-up, whatever agents do to the cost of writing contracts. Escrow and bonds answer the objection for a single household that signs a fixed contract, but only by switching the thermostat off, and they cannot bind successors or the people who write the protocol, so the commitment that protects investment has to come from property law, which Krier makes cheap to renegotiate. Tell it through Carol's glazing, sunk under a five-year deal and stranded when Dave buys Bob's house.
