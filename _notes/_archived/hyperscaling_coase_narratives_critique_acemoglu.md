# A referee's reading of "Coasean street stories"

Dear Dan,

Thank you for the sketches. Before the sketch-by-sketch comments I want to state my main reservation, because it affects nearly all of them. Krier cites my 2003 paper on the political Coase theorem and answers it in one paragraph: agent transparency makes expropriation politically costly, and automated auditing hardens the institutions that constrain power. That answer is the weakest joint in his essay, and your sketches never test it. Instead, almost every sketch closes on a precondition of the form "a rule set by someone without a stake", "enforced from outside", "someone accountable". The argument of the 2003 paper is that no such someone exists. Whoever sets the consent rule, runs the lottery, keeps the identity registry or audits agent loyalty holds power. The people who benefit from that power cannot credibly promise not to use it, and it will be contested by those with the de facto power to contest it. So your sketches show convincingly that bargaining needs institutions. Krier half-concedes that already: his state is a "framework guarantor". What the sketches do not yet show is why those institutions would be any more fragile in an agent world than they are now, and that is the claim the essay makes.

## 1. The honeymoon

This is the best of the ten as a story about institutions, and that is because of the first paragraph, not the model. The protocol is written at a consultation attended by the people who will use it every day. That is de facto power turning itself into de jure rules, and the sketch shows it happening without anyone acting badly. The mechanism that follows is familiar under other names. Whoever makes a take-it-or-leave-it offer takes the surplus. A principal who learns the agent's type extracts the information rent. And the dynamic version of that, the ratchet effect (Freixas, Guesnerie and Tirole 1985), is exactly the "honeymoon then squeeze" curve. Students of legislatures will also recognise the lottery: it is random recognition of the proposer in Baron and Ferejohn (1989). You should say so. It makes the remedy look less odd and more like something institutions have actually used.

The sketch's best line is "none of the agents would have proposed it". Then follow the thought through. Who would adopt the lottery, given that the consultation which wrote the first protocol is still the body that writes protocols? The town that "does something most people find odd" did it through politics, and the sketch has to say what in that town's politics differed. Otherwise the remedy is a deus ex machina.

## 2. Seventy-five percent

This one is strong and well grounded. Paying a decisive subset just enough to sign while the excluded party absorbs the externality is the divide-and-conquer result in the contracting-with-externalities literature: Rasmusen, Ramseyer and Wiley (1991) on naked exclusion, and Segal (1999) more generally. Your point that the pact collapses with no one behaving badly is the right emphasis, and the closing sentence, "the institution needs a leash", is the political Coase problem in four words. The developers funding the reform is also the right detail. When a coalition that gains from a change in de jure rules pays for it, that is how persistence works (Acemoglu and Robinson 2008, on de facto power offsetting de jure change). I would object to one thing. You set the reform against an ideal of unanimity, but Krier's comparison is with today's planning boards, which also outvote Carol and are captured too. A sketch that compares only against a frictionless fair deal falls into what Demsetz (1969) called the nirvana fallacy. It needs a sentence saying why the agent version is worse than the planning-committee version, not just worse than the ideal.

## 3. The form that cannot say "but not both"

This is a sound point about expressiveness, but as political economy it is the weakest of the ten. Combinatorial preferences that additive bids cannot express are a known problem in auction design, with known partial answers, and the sketch half-concedes the optimists' case, since the full table and the glazing deal both work. The disability-assessment analogy is good, and it points towards the sketch you should have written. Forms are written by whoever has the resources to sit on the standards consortium, and they encode those parties' interests as neutral defaults. As it stands, though, the story attributes the failure to computational limits ("the table doubles with each slot") more than to power. Keep the glazing example, which is a nice instance of agents creating surplus. Then either sharpen the consortium's interest or fold this sketch into the agenda-setting frame.

## 4. Everybody's child has asthma

The economics here is correct. It is Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983), information rents, and the regressive incidence of a uniform price, and the sketch dramatises it well. The verdict that Carol, who is worst off, gains nothing is a real indictment of the club. The sharpest move is the turn against Hayek: tailoring payments requires an assessor who sees inside households, which is exactly what Krier says a planner cannot do. I would push this further than you do. An assessor with access to medical records is not only a bureaucracy, it is a site of power, and who controls it will be fought over. Remember too that this is a problem of information, not of power, and Krier could reasonably reply that existing regulation does no better at telling Carol's harm from Alice's. That reply is correct, and you should meet it.

## 5. Two towns, one depot

The mechanism is credible and the examples (HQ2, Foxconn) are well chosen. Mobile capital extracting the surplus of immobile communities is one of the oldest stories about who captures the gains from new technology. I think the conclusion overreaches. A regional compact or state-aid rules are not "a planner of the kind the Coasean programme was meant to retire". They are framework rules of exactly the kind Krier's guarantor state is supposed to provide. To be fair to him, the claim should be narrower and harder to answer. The rule against bidding wars has to be imposed on communities by a level of government that the mobile firm also lobbies, and firms that operate across a thousand streets are better organised at that level than any street is. That is the Olson point and the Power and Progress point together, and it is stronger than the planner jibe.

## 6. The boundary road

This is among the strongest. It goes directly at Krier's claim that agents give the "unconscious blob" a voice, by asking who decides which agents are called. Defining the parties to a bargain is a question about the franchise. In Acemoglu and Robinson (2000) the franchise is extended as a commitment device, because elites cannot otherwise promise future redistribution. Your sketch shows the converse: parties to a bargain have every incentive to keep the franchise narrow. The renters on Krier's own developer street are an excellent detail. The precondition, though, again resolves into "someone without a stake", and the court you invoke is staffed, funded and jurisdictionally bounded by the political process. Say whose interest it is to give the next street standing, and the sketch becomes an argument instead of a wish.

## 7. Branch stacking

The mechanism is right, and Australian readers will know the term, but I find this the least persuasive as a criticism of Krier. Sybil resistance is a solved-enough problem in contexts where the state already issues identity, and Krier could reasonably answer that the guarantor state does this. The interesting part is the last sentence: whether a renter, a child or a trust counts as a party is a political choice. That belongs with sketch 6 as a single story about who holds standing. On its own, sketch 7 reads as a technical exploit with a technical fix.

## 8. On commission

Strong, and well documented. Levitt and Syverson (2008) on real estate agents and the Hayne royal commission are the right evidence, and the point that a client cannot observe the counterfactual deal is the credence-good problem that defeats Krier's "parasitic agents get abandoned". From my standpoint the political economy is the voucher. The publicly funded agent is procured, and procurement is where vendors lobby. Who writes the voucher's eligibility rules, and what the vendor's commission structure is permitted to be, are contested regulatory choices. One extra sentence on that would connect this sketch to the others.

## 9. Repeat players

From my standpoint this is the strongest sketch, because it is the only one in which power accumulates. Galanter (1974) is the right ancestor, and reputations for toughness in repeated play (Kreps and Wilson 1982) supply the mechanism. Scoring households for refusals and tying groceries to vans are the ways that position in many bargains becomes leverage in each one. The sketch also unwittingly answers Krier's response to my 2003 paper. His defence is transparency: "it's harder to steal what is clearly priced and publicly recorded". Your public record of the company never raising an offer, and your platform scores of household refusals, are transparency working for the repeat player. Legibility favours whoever can process it at scale. Say that explicitly, because it turns Krier's one answer to the political Coase theorem around. The weakness is the ending: "legislatures write them" is true, but legislatures face lobbying from the party that is in a thousand bargains, too.

## 10. The poor street

Krier concedes most of the economics, so wealth effects and the initial allocation are not news to him, and the Summers memo carries much of the rhetoric. The last paragraph, though, is the best paragraph in the set. Krier hands the allocation of rights to democratic institutions he himself calls "ossified and captured", which agents are supposed to repair. That circle is the political Coase theorem. The sketch misses the dynamics. Each round of cheap bargaining shifts wealth toward the company and away from Carol's street. Wealth is a source of de facto political power, so the next allocation of rights is set by a polity in which Carol's street weighs less. A static wealth effect becomes self-reinforcing. That loop is what my work would emphasise, and no sketch shows it.

## Overall

The strongest sketches are 9, 6 and 1, because each locates the failure in who sets the terms rather than in what agents can compute. The weakest are 7 and 3, which describe exploits and expressiveness limits with fixes Krier could plausibly accept without giving up his thesis. Sketches 5 and 10 are sound but should be more careful about what Krier has already conceded.

Two stories are missing. The first is the political Coase theorem itself. Streets strike a decade of agent-mediated bargains, investing and organising around them. Then the party that guarantees enforcement, whether council, platform or state, changes the terms after the fact because it can, and nothing in the bargaining layer can stop it. Krier's answer, transparency and automated auditing, should be tested inside that story: who runs the auditor, and what happens when its owner and the reneging party are the same coalition? The second is the feedback loop from sketch 10: the gains from cheap bargaining go to the repeat players, they convert those gains into political influence, and they use the influence to rewrite the protocol. This is the general claim of Power and Progress, that the gains from a technology follow power unless countervailing institutions are built and defended, and it is the essay's strongest available thesis. Your "Not yet used" note asks whether the consultation is the frame. In my view it is, and the frame should be extended in time so that each consultation is held by people whom the previous round of bargaining has made stronger.

Does the set convince? It convinces me that agent bargaining cannot stand on its own and needs rules it cannot write for itself. It does not yet show that the preconditions are more fragile than Krier implies, because each sketch hands the remedy to a disinterested outside party whose existence is assumed, which is the same move Krier makes. The essay needs to argue that the agent world weakens the countervailing powers that would supply those rules, rather than only showing that the rules are needed.

## Three changes I would ask for

First, add a sketch in which the enforcer reneges or is captured, and use it to test Krier's transparency-and-auditing reply to the political Coase theorem directly. Second, replace each "someone without a stake" precondition with an account of which coalition would have the de facto power and the interest to build and sustain that institution, and whether agent bargaining strengthens or weakens that coalition. Third, compare each sketch's outcome against today's captured regulatory process as well as against the frictionless fair deal, so that readers who share Krier's low opinion of the status quo cannot dismiss the set as a nirvana comparison.
