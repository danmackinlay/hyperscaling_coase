# A referee's letter on "Coasean street stories"

Dear colleague,

I read Krier first and then your ten sketches, and I come away persuaded of something narrower than you intend. You show, often well, that the "framework guarantor" Krier wants is a thicker thing than his word "leaner" suggests. You do not show that the bargained outcomes you describe are unjust, and only rarely do you show that they are worse than the regulatory status quo Krier is arguing against. Two habits produce most of the gap. The first is scoring outcomes by a Nash product of gains, so that "Carol gains nothing" reads as a failure even when Carol was paid her full harm and consented. That is a patterned end-state criterion, and a reader of Nozick (1974) will ask why the division of surplus between willing parties is a question of justice at all, as opposed to the question of whether anyone's rights were crossed on the way. The second habit is calling every rule-of-law institution a "planner". A court, a fiduciary duty, a register of titles and a constitutional rule chosen unanimously are not the Washington planner of Hayek (1945); they are the institutions Hayek thought made dispersed knowledge usable. Where your precondition turns out to be one of those, you have restated Krier's concession rather than refuted him.

That said, the sketches are vivid, and several land real blows. Taking them in turn:

## 1. The honeymoon

The mechanism is credible and well known: the party that makes a take-it-or-leave-it offer takes the surplus (the ultimatum game; Rubinstein, 1982, for the alternating-offers version where the first mover's advantage shrinks as patience grows). The learning curve, where the neighbours do well while the company is ignorant and lose the margin as it learns, is the best piece of storytelling in the set. But it is unfair to Krier on one point. In his own asthma example it is the household's agent that broadcasts a price and the truck's agent that accepts or refuses. You have inverted his protocol and then shown the inversion is bad. Your consultation explains why the inversion happens, which is a story about capture of a rule-making process, the very vice Krier charges to planning. Make that explicit and the sketch becomes stronger. Your remedy, a random-recognition rule, is also well known (Baron and Ferejohn, 1989), and I dispute that "none of the agents would have proposed it". Buchanan's distinction between constitutional and post-constitutional choice predicts exactly that parties uncertain of their future position would agree to it unanimously. That makes the lottery a constitutional contract, not something imposed.

## 2. Seventy-five percent

This is the strongest sketch from where I stand, and I would put it first. A supermajority that can force consent on Carol is a taking, and the collapse of the neighbours' pact is the empty core of a majority game, a result as old as cooperative game theory. Buchanan and Tullock (1962) built *The Calculus of Consent* on exactly this trade-off: any rule short of unanimity lowers decision costs by letting the deciding coalition impose external costs on the rest, and they recommend that the rule itself be chosen at the constitutional stage, where Wicksellian unanimity is feasible. The sketch would gain from naming that lineage, and from noting the design literature it bypasses, such as Heller and Hills (2008) on land assembly districts, which tries to cure holdout without licensing pairwise bribery. One fairness caution: Krier does not propose a supermajority. His remedies are the club and the Harberger-style option fee. The sketch is an argument against a reform his critics might reach for, which is worth having, but say so.

## 3. The form that cannot say "but not both"

This is the most Hayekian sketch, and I enjoyed the irony: the tacit, non-additive knowledge Krier praises is precisely what a consortium's schema cannot carry, so the bargaining protocol becomes the crude category of Scott's state. The combinatorial-auction literature has wrestled with this for decades, and you should cite it rather than present the bundling dilemma as new. But notice what happens to Carol: she refuses and nothing runs. Her veto works and her rights are intact. The failure is lost gains, not injustice, and under a planning regime the same additive form would simply be imposed on her without a veto. The precondition, a language "written by someone answerable to the people whose harms are hard to describe", does not follow uniquely; competing schemas with households free to choose among them would serve as well, and that is the market answer you leave out.

## 4. Everybody's child has asthma

The economics is right. You are retelling the information-rent results of mechanism design, including Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983), and the critique of Krier's uniform-price club is fair and sharp: a single price paid to all overpays the least harmed. That much is a genuine correction to his "single, fair number". But the normative conclusion overreaches. Carol is paid her harm and agrees; by her own lights she is no worse off, and nobody took anything of hers. The rents Alice and Bob collect are the price of not letting anyone look inside households, which is a price a liberal should be willing to pay. And the "way out" is not a planner. Nuisance law has always required a claimant to show damage, and a tribunal adjudicating a particular claim under general rules is the rule of law, not central knowledge of the kind Hayek denied the planner. The dilemma you pose, rents or an assessor, is real; the label you put on the second horn is not.

## 5. Two towns, one depot

This is the weakest sketch from my standpoint. The ring road is the company's own legitimate alternative; when a party acquires a better outside option, its share rises, and calling the result unfair just means preferring the old threat point. Competition among jurisdictions for mobile capital is Tiebout (1956), and Brennan and Buchanan (1980) treat it as one of the few working restraints on Leviathan. Your examples cut the other way: HQ2 and Foxconn were bidding wars among governments spending taxpayers' money, which is exactly what a libertarian condemns, and condemns as state action, not private bargaining. Residents voluntarily selling quiet for less than they hoped is a different thing. EU state-aid rules are a treaty constraint on governments, not a planner of private deals. If you keep this sketch, restrict it to cases where the "mobile firm" extracts public money.

## 6. The boundary road

Strong, and fair to Krier where it matters: his developer example buys every homeowner's consent and says nothing of renters or of the next street. But you should call the mechanism by its name. Coase (1960) never claimed that a bargain among a subset of affected parties was efficient; dumping harm on non-parties is a tort, and the remedy is the old one of Calabresi and Melamed (1972), liability for harm to people who did not consent. Krier's state already enforces contracts and keeps courts. The sketch shows that his court must also hear nuisance claims from third parties and decide who counts as affected, which is more than "leaner" implies, but it is not a planner. I would cut "which is to say a court" from the precondition, as though that were a concession wrung from libertarians; it is the first thing a libertarian would supply.

## 7. Branch stacking

Credible, briefly. Sybil attacks are real, and the analogy to stacked meetings is apt. The proposal that resolves it, though, is cheaper than you imply. Consent in a nuisance bargain attaches to holdings, to title in the affected land, not to heads; Bob's household holds one parcel. Counting agents was a protocol error. Your residual question, whether a renter or a trust is a party, is a real one, but it is the question of who holds which entitlement, and you treat it again in 6 and 10. Merge it.

## 8. On commission

The second strongest. Krier's faith that switching costs discipline parasitic agents meets the problem that the client cannot observe the counterfactual; these are credence goods in Darby and Karni's (1973) sense, and your Levitt and Syverson evidence fits. A libertarian has no difficulty with your precondition: fiduciary duty is common-law contract and agency doctrine, older than any regulatory state, and disclosure of how an agent is paid is fraud prevention. One point to sharpen: the voucher is what makes this bad. A state-funded agent chosen from a low-bid list is the public-defender model with its familiar weaknesses, and that is a criticism of the state-provision half of Krier's proposal, not of bargaining.

## 9. Repeat players

Galanter (1974) is the right ancestor, and the reputation result is solid. But the sketch bundles four mechanisms, and they need different verdicts. Commitment by reputation is how markets work. Scoring households for refusing is more interesting, and Nozick's own essay on coercion (1969) supplies the test you need: whether a conditional is a threat or an offer depends on the morally expected baseline. Penalising someone in unrelated dealings for exercising a right they hold looks like a threat by that test, and the grocery tie-in looks the same way. That is a stronger argument than "it is the ordinary advantage of being in many bargains", because it identifies a rights violation rather than an unequal division. Develop that and drop the others.

## 10. The poor street

Here I part company most sharply. Carol selling quiet cheaply because she needs cash is a voluntary exchange that, by her judgment, makes her better off; forbidding it or repricing it in "wellbeing units" overrides her judgment with yours, and it needs exactly the interpersonal comparison of utility that Robbins (1932) taught economists to distrust. The Summers memo is offensive in tone, but its logic is the one you are arguing against without answering. The libertarian ground for objecting lies elsewhere, and you leave it unused: how did Carol come to be poor, and how did the company come to hold whatever it holds? Nozick's principle of rectification and the Lockean proviso, and Gauthier's (1986) insistence that the bargaining baseline must itself be free of prior predation, give you a principled reason to question the threat point that does not depend on a patterned criterion. Gauthier is your natural ally here, since he too rejects the raw Nash solution, but for its baseline, not its division. Your closing jab, that Krier hands the hardest choices to institutions he calls captured, is fair and it lands.

## Overall

The strongest sketches are 2, 8 and 6, in that order; each identifies a place where either a right is crossed (forced consent, harm to non-parties) or consent is hollow (an agent paid by the other side), and each is answerable within a liberal order, which makes them persuasive against Krier's claim that the state can become "a lot leaner". The weakest are 5 and 10, which treat a loss of surplus under a changed threat point as an injustice; 7 is credible but slight.

The story I most want and do not find is the counterfactual. Put Carol's street through the planning committee, the diesel ban, the low-traffic order Krier actually lived under. Who turned up to that consultation? What was Carol paid? Probably nothing, and the boundary road got the traffic anyway. Without that sketch every comparison runs against an ideal Nash bargain, and Krier's readers will reply, rightly, that the ideal was never on offer. I also want one story about how an entitlement arose, since the justice of a holding is settled by its history, not by its shape. Your "Not yet used" note half-sees this: the consultation that recurs in 1, 2, 3, 7 and 9 is Buchanan's constitutional stage, and the essay's real claim is that Krier is thin on how the rules of bargaining get chosen. Make that the frame. It is a criticism I share, and it does not require the end-state scoring.

As it stands the set convinces me that the institutions under Krier's market are thicker and more contested than he admits. It does not convince me that bargaining produces worse or less just outcomes than the regulation it would replace, because the sketches rarely test that comparison.

## Three changes I would ask for

Score each sketch first by whether anyone's rights were crossed or their consent was hollow, and keep the Nash-product shares as a secondary measure, so that "paid her harm and gained nothing" is no longer reported as a failure. Add one sketch that runs the same street through the regulatory status quo, and judge every precondition against that alternative rather than against an ideal bargain. Separate institutions of the rule of law (courts, fiduciary duty, title, constitutional rules chosen unanimously) from planning in the strict sense, and reserve the word "planner" for the second.
