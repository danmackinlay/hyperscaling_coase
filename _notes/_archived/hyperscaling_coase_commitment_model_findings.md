# What the red-team found in the commitment model, in plain words

Written 2026-10-21 for Dan, the essay's author, from six independent reviews of `_plans/hyperscaling_coase_commitment_model.md` (called "the model" below) and from the primary texts the model cites, which I read directly. "I" and "the collator" both mean the session that ran the reviews, read the primaries, checked the reviewers' numbers and wrote this; nothing here was written by a reviewer. The document is for a reader who has read the model and nothing else. Every paragraph under "The findings" is meant to stand alone: it quotes the sentence of the model it is about, under the model's own heading, says what is wrong in plain words, names the reviewers who reached it, runs the point on the river's numbers where the point is numerical, defines each technical term where it is used, and ends with what the model should do. It tries never to send the reader to another paragraph; where two paragraphs need the same fact, the fact is stated in both. Provenance sits in HTML comments, which can be ignored.

The six reviewers each read the model and the one section of the essay that describes the river, and none of the other planning notes for the essay; none saw another's work. They are: **an economist** in bargaining, contest and commitment theory; **a cooperative game theorist**; **a behavioural and experimental economist**, who also surveyed the studies of negotiating software agents; **an advocate for Krier**, who read Krier's essay itself; **a cold reader**, who was given no brief at all beyond one sentence of purpose; and **a repeated-games theorist**, in equilibrium selection and learning in games, added after Dan objected that the model presumes a unique equilibrium. When this document says "the essay's river section", it means that section of the essay, which the model's first section summarises but does not reproduce; it is quoted nowhere below, only described. A finding reached by five or six readers who had no contact with one another is about as solid as this method gets. Where a number below is a reviewer's, I re-ran it on the river; two reviewer numbers that failed that check are not repeated here.

The model's own numbers, repeated so that nothing needs looking up: a factory earns a profit $\pi$ from polluting; three residents suffer harms $h=(2,3,9)$, which sum to $14$; at $\pi=20$ polluting and paying the harms leaves a surplus of $6$, which the model calls the pie; at $\pi=10$ the clean river is the right outcome; under the rule $k=3$ the factory needs all three residents' consent, under $k=1$ it needs one. $S$ is the residents' share of the pie, between $0$ and $1$. To commit, in the model, is to publish a position and a revocation cost $r$, the money you lose if you back down. The model's four cells are: A, nobody can commit; B, only the factory can; C, only the residents can, as one; D, both can. The model's compute budget is $c$, in operations, and its estimation error is $\sigma/\sqrt{c}$. Four things the model never states but this document needs. An *equilibrium* is a pair of choices, one per side, from which neither side gains by changing only its own. The essay's river has two information settings that the model's first section does not restate, one in which every harm and the profit are known to all, and one in which each resident alone knows their own harm. Instructions that *can read each other* are published instructions that may condition what they demand on what the other side's published instructions demand; instructions that *cannot* are fixed demands. And a *principal* is the person an agent acts for.

## The verdict in one paragraph

The model's four cells are right about two things and wrong about the two that carry its thesis. Whoever alone can commit takes the pie: that is cells B and C, and it holds for bargainers who accept a penny over nothing, which software agents do and people often do not, provided they answer one at a time rather than voting at once. But cell A's "three and three" is not derived and is wrong on the river, where four parties each hold a veto and every symmetric solution gives $1.5$ each, giving the residents three quarters of the pie, not half, though even that is one choice among the many equilibria of a game of named shares. And cell D, the standoff, is asserted rather than derived: the paper the model cites for it, Ellingsen and Miettinen 2008, now read in full, shows that when both sides can make perfectly reliable cheap commitments three outcomes survive, two of which are one side taking everything, the model's own B and C, and the third a gamble in which each side randomises between demanding everything and waiting, which usually ends in a standoff; the standoff is the unique outcome only when commitments are made at the same moment, cannot read each other, are cheap relative to the pie, are made once and are slightly unreliable, and then it happens with probability $q^2$, where $q$ is the chance a commitment sticks. All six reviewers found this independently, and all six found, by different routes, that the model's own ingredients point to B rather than D: published commitments arrive in an order and the second mover gives way; three residents commit more slowly and more expensively than one factory; and agents that play closer to equilibrium lack the one commitment humans make without any device, the refusal of an insulting offer. The model's compute story then collapses: in D better estimation does not make the standoff more reliable, since what moves the standoff's odds is how cheap and how reliable commitment is, which compute changes only when spent on committing, and otherwise compute there at most locks in whichever outcome convention has already picked; in B it lowers the residents' share of each deal, as the model says, but more slowly than it says and to a floor if part of the harms cannot be measured, and it raises the chance of a deal, which the model omits, so that the residents' expected surplus, counting failed deals as zero, peaks at a middling budget; and once the parties are patient enough to care about next year's deal, at $\pi=20$ no cell has a single outcome unless one side's standing instruction, a published rule its agent follows in every future dealing, makes it the leader of the repeated game, the side whose strategy is fixed first and to which the other adjusts. What survives is narrower and, I think, more interesting: unless the instructions can read each other, cheap commitment hands the pie to whoever commits first and most cheaply, which a single claimant is better placed to do than three; the residents' remedy, once residents can commit, is a rule fixing their internal split before any of them commits, since residents who commit separately collide with one another, plus a rule that bars the factory from committing; and compute converges play on a cell's own outcome only where the cell has one, B and C always and D only under the standoff's five conditions, while everywhere else it converges, if at all, on whichever equilibrium the agents' errors and history select and otherwise enters as asymmetries, which choose the cell (who commits first, who commits most cheaply, who stays fixed while the other adapts) or act within it (whose agent is better, which convention, a way of playing each side expects the other to follow, formed first).

## A proposed thesis, for Dan's decision

*Proposed, not adopted. Every clause below is carried by a paragraph under the next heading; nothing in it is new.*

Cheap commitment for everyone does not by itself produce a standoff. When both sides' commitments are perfectly reliable, a standoff is one of three outcomes, the other two being one side taking everything; it is the only outcome when commitments are made at the same moment, cannot read each other, are cheap relative to the pie, are made once, and sometimes fail to stick, and then it comes with probability $q^2$, where $q$ is the chance a commitment sticks. If the instructions can read each other, the outcome is instead a coordination problem that a focal rule settles. Otherwise cheap commitment produces a race to commit first, won by whoever commits first and most cheaply: the factory, one claimant, against three residents who, if they commit separately, collide with one another and reach no deal, and, if they wait to agree, publish later. Agents lack the one commitment humans make without any device, the refusal of an insulting offer, so the take-it-or-leave-it cell becomes exact when the residents answer in turn or as one bloc: the factory pays $2$, $3$ and $9$ plus a margin that shrinks as it learns the harms, with deals failing while the margin is large, and keeps the rest of the six. When nobody can commit the residents need no internal rule, and bargaining as one would cost them, three quarters of the pie under any symmetric solution falling to one half. Once residents can commit, their remedy is two rules: one fixing how three veto-holders divide their six before any of them commits, where unanimity itself is the inefficiency, by the theorem of Miettinen and Vanberg that minimum-share declarations which become binding with some probability are inefficient under unanimity and efficient under every other voting rule; and one barring the factory from committing, which is what labour law did to take-it-or-leave-it employers. Compute converges play on a cell's own outcome only where the cell has one: the two cells where one side alone can commit, and the standoff under its five conditions, where what sharpens it is cheaper and more reliable commitment rather than estimation; haggling and the cell in which both sides can commit otherwise have no single outcome, and there compute selects among equilibria according to what the agents' errors are about. Elsewhere compute enters as asymmetries. It chooses the cell by who commits first, who commits most cheaply, and who stays fixed while the other adapts; within a cell it tilts the split toward the better agent and locks in whatever convention, a way of playing that each side expects the other to follow, formed first. Institutions choose the cell in the weak sense that a rule giving one side the power to commit fixes the division; Krier says so himself. The disagreement with him is about scale and price: his club, which pays every member one clearing price, needs at least $9$ a head to bring in resident 3, so $27$ against a profit of $20$, and his union names a price he leaves open.

## The findings, in the model's order

### Under "1. The river, in one paragraph"

**The yardstick sentence is wrong in three ways.** The model says: "The essay's own yardstick, "is anyone worse off than under no deal", turns out not to tell the cells apart, because unanimity protects everyone's no-deal position in all of them. That yardstick is too weak for the question. The share is the right one." The cooperative game theorist, the advocate and the cold reader found three faults. First, unanimity protects the no-deal position only when committing is free and nobody backs down. The paper the model relies on for the standoff, Ellingsen and Miettinen 2008, selects the standoff only under conditions that include a small positive cost of committing, and with that cost both sides in the standoff end below their no-deal payoff of $0$, so the old yardstick sees the standoff perfectly well. Krier's reimbursing veto, which the model files under cell B, also breaks the floor: if the factory commits to paying $(1,2,8)$ and a resident who vetoes must reimburse the factory's lost profit, accepting costs each resident $1$ while vetoing costs a third of $20$ if the three share the reimbursement equally, so all three accept and end at $-1$. Second, the essay also scored equity by whether the median resident ends better off, and that is not the same test as whether anyone ends worse off: under the model's own split, three slices shared equally by the residents, they get $(1,1,1)$ in A, a penny each in B, $(2,2,2)$ in C and $(0,0,0)$ in D before any cost of committing is paid, and with the four parties, the factory and three residents each holding a veto, sharing equally, A gives $(1.5,1.5,1.5)$ instead; either way the median test separates A and C, and B by a penny, from D. Third, the share is defined in this section as a count, "of the $6$ slices, how many go to the residents", and used everywhere as a fraction; it is $0/0$ in D, where there is no pie, and it merges the resident with harm $2$ and the resident with harm $9$, so it cannot see who among the residents got what, which is the question the essay calls equity. *Action:* rewrite the sentence. Keep three scores: whether the deal happens, the residents' share of the pie as a fraction, undefined when there is no deal, and whether anyone ends below their no-deal payoff; and say that the share is blind within the residents' side.

The advocate, reading Krier's text, found that the developer passage does say the developer must find a price every homeowner accepts, so it is a unanimity case. The beach passage says the agent of the man who would close the beach must make an auditable offer to every person deprived of access; its point is accountability, not consent, and nobody in it holds a veto. Krier's third large example, a factory and ten thousand residents, states no consent rule at all. *Action:* cite the developer alone for $k=3$, or say that Krier's large examples are mostly silent on the rule and that the essay is supplying it.

### Under "2. What "commit" means, and its unit"

**A published instruction and its hash prove what the instructions say, not that they cannot be replaced.** The model says: ""Cheap commitment" means that setting up such a promise costs almost nothing and the other side can check it, which is what an agent that publishes its instructions and a hash of them provides". The economist, the behavioural economist, the cold reader and the advocate found the gap. By the model's own unit, commitment is a revocation cost, the money you lose by backing down. A hash proves which instructions were running when the hash was made; it forfeits nothing when the principal, the person the agent acts for, re-instructs the agent or replaces it, so the published position has a revocation cost of about $0$, which is cell A, not cell D. On the river: the factory's agent publishes an instruction to pay exactly $2$, $3$ and $9$ and not a cent more, with a hash; the residents' agents hold out; the factory's owner reissues the instructions at $3$, $4$, $10$; nothing was lost by backing down, so nothing was committed. To make the cost real you need a bond that is forfeited, an escrow that will not release, or a court that will enforce; each of those is an institution, so the model's claim in its fourth section that "without them cheap commitment chooses D" refutes itself, because without an institution there is no revocation cost to choose anything. The economist adds the standard result (Katz 1991) that a contract delegating bargaining to an agent carries no commitment value if the other side cannot observe it or the principal can renegotiate it. What an agent's published instructions do provide depends on how often the principal overrides them: never, and the hash is as good as a bond; at will, and it is no commitment, as above; sometimes, and it is a commitment that is observable but imperfectly binding, which is the case in which Ellingsen and Miettinen 2008 make the standoff the unique outcome, provided the commitments are also simultaneous, unable to read each other, cheap relative to the pie and made once, with probability $q^2$ where $q$ is the chance the commitment sticks; the driver there is imperfect binding, not proof. *Action:* rewrite the definition. Cheap commitment for agents means a published, checkable position backed by a bond or escrow; say where the bond lives, since that is the institution.
<!-- provenance: cold reader, economist (Katz 1991), behavioural economist, advocate (human override as Krier's gap). Collation finding 10. -->

**The revocation cost is not Crawford's formulation.** The model says: "This is Crawford's 1982 formulation and it is the standard one." The cold reader, the cooperative game theorist, the economist and the behavioural economist each doubted the attribution from the abstract; I read Crawford's paper in full to settle it. In it a bargainer who attempts commitment announces a demand and receives "a draw from a probability distribution, whose realization is the cost ... that must be borne if the bargainer in question later decides to accept anything less than his demand", and "each bargainer learns his own, but not his opponent's, cost of backing down" only after the demands are made; "bargainers are perfectly informed about everything except their costs of backing down." His revocation cost is therefore random, private and learned after the attempt, and he writes on page 611 that this uncertainty is "essential", because with known costs and simultaneous demands the game is Nash's demand game, the game in which each side names the share it insists on and gets it if the two shares fit within the pie and nothing otherwise, which has, in his words, "great strategic uncertainty, in the form of multiple Nash equilibria." (The same game is meant wherever this document says demand game.) The model's object, a cost that is chosen, published and verified before bargaining, is Schelling's device: in Schelling's 1960 account a bargainer commits by posting a bond with a third party or burning a bridge, so that retreat costs more than it gains. On the river the difference is the whole standoff: with each side's cost known to be larger than the pie of $6$, Crawford's model offers no impasse theorem, only the demand game's many equilibria. *Action:* attribute the chosen, published revocation cost to Schelling, and cite Crawford only for the case of uncertain costs.
<!-- provenance: collator, from the JSTOR text Dan supplied, pp. 611-617; the cold reader, cooperative game theorist, economist and behavioural economist each flagged the attribution as doubtful from the abstract. Collation hygiene. -->

**A position with a revocation cost is a commitment for one deal; a published standing instruction is a commitment to a whole strategy, and that changes the game.** The model says: "So each side, before bargaining, may publish a **position** (the share of the pie it insists on) and a revocation cost $r$." The repeated-games theorist, asked to consider the river negotiation recurring, found that the model's revocation cost is per deal while an agent's published instructions govern every future deal, and that the two are different objects. If the factory and the residents set terms every year at $k=3$ and $\pi=20$, the folk theorem applies: in a game that repeats, any division that gives every party at least what they could guarantee alone, here $0$ each, with the four payoffs summing to at most $6$, can be sustained as an equilibrium when the parties are patient enough, because a deviation today is punished by the loss of future deals. Patient enough is measured by the discount factor, the weight placed on next year's payoff relative to this year's. For the residents bargaining as a bloc he derived a threshold on that discount factor of one half: below it the factory's take-it-or-leave-it division is the unique outcome of repeated cell B, and at or above it every share from $0$ up to $1$ is an equilibrium of repeated B, and likewise of repeated C, A and D. The derivation is his and I have not checked it; the folk theorem itself is standard. So for renewed consents between patient parties the four cells' sets of efficient outcomes coincide and the table determines nothing but the threat points, the payoffs each side gets if no deal is ever reached, which the entitlement, the rule saying who holds the right to start with, fixes. A one-sided standing instruction makes that side the leader of the repeated game, the side whose strategy is fixed first and to which the other adjusts, so B or C survives repetition, which is the model's best defence. Two-sided standing instructions that can read each other are conditional commitments, and under those every division that leaves each side at least its no-deal payoff is an equilibrium even of a single deal. At $\pi=10$ and $k=3$ repetition changes nothing, because the clean river is the only outcome that leaves nobody worse off. *Action:* state the model's scope as one-shot sales of permanent rights between parties who do not deal again, with commitments that cannot read each other; say that renewed consents and standing instructions put the river in the folk theorem's world, where the entitlement sets the threat points and nothing else.

### Under "3. The four cells"

**Cell A's split is imported, not derived, and on the river it is wrong.** The model says: "The standard answer, which is what happens in experiments too, is that they split it: three and three." and, in the same cell, "This is ordinary haggling over six slices between two parties who each need the other." The cold reader, the cooperative game theorist, the behavioural economist, the economist and the repeated-games theorist found the same error independently. In cell A nobody can commit, so the residents cannot bind themselves into a bloc; the model's own cell C says a bloc needs a pact. Without the bloc, the game at $k=3$ and $\pi=20$ has four parties each holding a veto: a veto player is one without whose consent no group is worth anything, and here any group short of all four is worth $0$ while all four together are worth $6$. Every symmetric way of solving such a game gives each veto player the same amount. The Shapley value, which pays each player their average contribution over all the orders in which players could join, gives $1.5$ each; the four-person Nash bargaining solution, which picks the division maximising the product of everyone's gains over no deal, gives $1.5$ each; bargaining in which the parties take turns to make offers gives $1.5$ each in the limit of patient parties. The payments are then $3.5$, $4.5$ and $10.5$, the factory keeps $1.5$, and the residents' share is $4.5/6=\tfrac34$, not $\tfrac12$. The model's "three and three" is what you get by first treating the residents as one player, which lowers their share from three quarters to one half; so under unanimity fragmentation favours the residents, and it is the factory that should want them to bargain as one. The cold reader also found the experiments misused: the equal-split experiments are between two people; experiments with several veto holders show delay and failed deals; and experiments on the ultimatum game, in which one party proposes a division and the other can only accept or refuse, show responders refusing offers that leave them nothing, which is exactly cell B's offer. *Action:* rewrite cell A for four players, giving the residents three quarters under any symmetric solution, and say that even that is one choice among many equilibria, since in a game of named shares every split that fits within the pie is an equilibrium.
<!-- provenance: cold reader, cooperative game theorist (Owen value), behavioural economist, economist, repeated-games theorist (payments 3.5, 4.5, 10.5); advocate in the weaker form. Collation finding 7. -->

**Cell B's "a penny more makes them accept" is a tie-break that humans refuse and that three simultaneous voters need not make.** The model says: "They are indifferent, so a penny more makes them accept." Two things are wrong, found by the behavioural economist and the repeated-games theorist respectively. First, the cell is the ultimatum game played three times: in the ultimatum game one party proposes a division and the other can only accept or refuse, and humans refuse offers that leave them nothing or nearly nothing about half the time, which is a punishment, since the refusals collapse when refusing no longer hurts the proposer. If each resident refuses a zero-surplus offer with probability one half, all three accept with probability one in eight, so cell B with humans mostly fails to close; a payment framed as compensation for harm may be read as fair, which softens this. Second, even with fully rational residents, if the three vote at once then all three refusing is itself an equilibrium of the voting stage, because when the other two are refusing no single resident's vote changes anything, so nobody is pivotal, that is, nobody's single choice decides the outcome. Cell B has a single outcome only when the residents respond one after another or as a bloc, and only after the indifferent resident is assumed to accept. The behavioural economist's survey of negotiating software agents finds that this refusal is exactly what agents built to maximise payoff give up: the AI systems whose play is closest to equilibrium accept almost anything, one accepting a share of $0.23$ per cent. So the penny is right for such agents and wrong for people, and the difference is the human commitment the model says humans lack. *Action:* keep cell B, state that it is the ultimatum game with three responders, and say in words that its outcome needs the residents to break indifference in the factory's favour and to answer in sequence.

**The residents are not a side; cell C assumes the hard part.** The model says: "Notice what it needs: the three residents must commit *as one*, which means they first settle among themselves how to split their six, and the factory must be unable to offer resident 3 alone a better deal." All six reviewers found that the first clause is the whole problem and the second does no work. Settling how to split six among three veto holders, three parties each of whose consent is needed for anything, is itself a bargaining game with every split as an equilibrium; and if commitment is, in the model's words, "cheap for whoever has an agent", each resident can commit against the other two. The cooperative game theorist ran it: if resident 3 alone commits to demanding $15$, the factory pays resident 3 $15$ and the others exactly their harms $2$ and $3$, and all six slices go to one resident; if the three commit at once to incompatible internal demands, the residents' side is its own standoff. A single committed resident already takes the whole pie under unanimity, so "as one" adds nothing against the factory; its work is internal, fixing the split so that the residents neither collide nor fight over it. Meanwhile a side offer to one resident cannot help the factory at $k=3$, because the other two keep their vetoes: pay resident 3 $13$, and residents 1 and 2 can still demand $3$ and $4$, so the factory pays $20$ as before. There is a theorem behind this. Miettinen and Vanberg 2025, which I read in the publisher's abstract, study bargaining among several parties in which each can declare a minimum share that becomes binding with some probability, and find that "unanimity rule is uniquely inefficient. All other rules, including all-but-one, are fully efficient." Unanimity is the river's $k=3$; the inefficiency lives among the residents, not between the sides. *Action:* relabel the right-hand column "an internal allocation rule exists", say that cell C needs three conditions (the residents can commit, the factory cannot, and the split is fixed before anyone commits), and move the side-deal sentence to a $k=1$ paragraph, if one is kept, where one signature suffices and a side deal does kill a pact.

**The sentence in which resident 3 breaks first names the wrong resident and the wrong rule.** The model says: "Under unanimity the factory needs all three anyway, but the pact still has to be enforced among the residents, or resident 3, who has the most to lose from no deal, breaks first." All six reviewers rejected the sentence. At $k=3$, no deal means a clean river, which the model's own first section says "is worth $0$ to everyone", so resident 3 loses nothing more from no deal than residents 1 and 2 do; what each loses is their slice of the six under whatever split the pact fixed, and under a split in proportion to harm resident 3 has the most to lose and is therefore the most loyal member, not the first to defect. Resident 3 is the costliest to buy, needing $9$ before seeing any surplus, which is a different thing. Nor is there anything to break under: in cell C the factory cannot commit, so it cannot credibly refuse, and a lone resident has nothing to sell, since the factory still needs the other two. The sentence imports the logic of $k=1$ at $\pi=10$, where the factory needs one signature and the resident worth most to it is the one with the smallest harm: the factory and resident 1 together can split $10-2=8$, the factory and resident 2 can split $7$, the factory and resident 3 only $1$. So at $k=1$ resident 1 breaks first, then 2, and resident 3 last. The repeated-games theorist's thresholds agree: if the three face the same choice every year, each resident keeps refusing only if the weight they put on next year's payoff is at least $(10-h_i)/10$, which is $0.8$, $0.7$ and $0.1$, so resident 1 is the weak link. *Action:* delete the sentence. If an example of defection is wanted, give the $k=1$ one with resident 1.

**The standoff is not what the cited papers prove.** The model says: "The result that this is what happens when committing is free and provable is Ellingsen and Miettinen 2008, and with a small cost of committing the standoff still happens with positive probability; Crawford 1982 has the same conclusion with revocation costs." I read both papers in full; the economist and the behavioural economist read Ellingsen and Miettinen's text, the cooperative game theorist quoted it, and the cold reader, the advocate and the repeated-games theorist reached the same correction from the abstract, so all six did. Ellingsen and Miettinen study two bargainers who each either commit to a demand at a small positive cost or wait, with commitments observable and made once. Their solution method is to discard, repeatedly, any choice that does worse than some other choice whatever the opponent does. With commitments that never fail, which is the model's "provable", three outcomes survive: one side demands everything and the other waits, which is the model's cell B; the reverse, which is cell C; and an outcome in which each side randomises between demanding everything and waiting, with "a high probability of conflict". On the river, taking the two sides to split the pie $3$ and $3$ when both wait, which is the model's own two-sided cell A rather than the four-player value and is used here because Ellingsen and Miettinen's game has two players, with a posting cost of $\kappa$ slices each side waits with probability $\kappa/3$ in that randomised outcome, so both demand everything, and there is no deal, with probability $(1-\kappa/3)^2$. The game that remains after the discarding is, in their words, Chicken: a game in which each side would rather yield than crash but would rather the other yield. With the residents as three separate committers rather than one side, and the four-player values of $1.5$ each in place of $3$ and $3$, the numbers change but the conclusion does not; that case is Miettinen and Vanberg's, in which binding minimum demands under unanimity are inefficient. The standoff is the unique outcome only when the commitments are simultaneous, cannot read each other, are made once, stick with probability $q<1$, and cost less than $3q(1-q)$ slices to post, a bound that shrinks as reliability rises, again with $3$ as a waiter's share, which is the cost below which demanding everything beats waiting even against an opponent who also demands everything; conflict then occurs with probability $q^2$: $0.81$ at $q=0.9$, $0.98$ at $q=0.99$. The cost direction in the model's sentence is reversed. Crawford had noted that with free, reliable commitment there is also an efficient compromise, both sides committing to exactly half; Ellingsen and Miettinen show that a small positive cost is what removes that compromise, and that at zero cost it is removed only under a stronger discarding rule, one that also drops a choice which is never better than another and sometimes worse. Crawford's impasse is different again: his revocation costs are random draws, learned by each side privately and only after the demands are made, and he calls that uncertainty essential; impasse in his model happens with positive probability, never with certainty; and under known costs his model is the demand game, the game of named shares, with many equilibria. The cost direction matters because the model's sentence has the standoff at zero cost and only "positive probability" with a small cost, whereas in the paper it is the small positive cost that makes the standoff unique and zero cost that leaves the compromise alive. The two papers also contradict the model's story that cheaper commitment means more standoffs: Crawford's fifth section shows that making commitment harder "need not always lower the probability of impasse", and the open avenues Ellingsen and Miettinen name are precisely the sequential and delayed commitments the model's agents would make. *Action:* rewrite cell D as "both sides can commit", with the three surviving outcomes, and state the standoff as a theorem under its real conditions: cheap relative to the pie, almost but not perfectly reliable, simultaneous, unable to read the other side's commitment, made once; probability $q^2$.

**The model's own premise defeats the standoff twice: published positions arrive in an order, and published instructions can be conditional.** The model says: "The factory publishes "I keep all six" and the residents publish "we take all six", each with their hands tied." All six reviewers found that nothing in the model makes the two publications simultaneous or unconditional, and that either relaxation removes the standoff. In order: with instant messaging, one side publishes first; the other sees a commitment to six and, by the model's own tie-break in cell B, a penny makes it accept; so the game is a race to publish first, and the first mover takes the six. ### Under "4. What cheap agents do to the table"

**Humans in large cases do not sit near cell A.** The model says: "Humans sit near cell A by default, because for a human a believable commitment is costly to make." **Institutions are assigned to cells by fiat, and the standoff has nine exits besides the two rules the model names.** The model says: "That is the second claim: **the institutions are what choose the cell, and without them cheap commitment chooses D.**" and, just above it, "A liability rule, "the factory may pollute but must pay each resident a set amount", is cell B written into law: the factory's position is fixed by the statute and the residents have nothing to commit to." A union rule makes the residents one player, which costs them when nobody can commit and is needed, as a rule fixing their internal split, only once they can; either way it neither gives them commitment nor takes it from the factory, so with the factory's agent present it yields the standoff or a race unless a second rule bars the factory from committing, which labour law in fact supplies. ### Under "5. What "as budgets rise" means, cell by cell"

**There is no "exact outcome of the cell" to converge to in the two cells the model's thesis rests on, A as the human baseline and D as the agents' default.** The model says: "As $c$ grows the errors vanish and play converges to the exact outcome of the cell." Every reviewer found this ill-posed, and the repeated-games theorist, assigned the question, took it apart cell by cell. Only cells B and C have a single outcome under perfectly reliable commitment, and only once equilibria resting on implausible choices, such as an indifferent resident refusing, are set aside; D has one only under slightly unreliable commitment, as below. Cell A, as a game in which each side names a share and gets it if the shares fit within the pie, has every split as an equilibrium and also every pair of incompatible demands. Cell D, under perfectly reliable commitment, contains B's and C's outcomes as well as the standoff, by Ellingsen and Miettinen's own result. Where the set of equilibria is not a single point, the model's rule that an agent "plays its cell's strategy using its estimates" is not a strategy; it needs a rule for choosing among equilibria, and if each side picks its favourite the demands are incompatible, so the standoff comes from the selection rule, not from compute. What vanishing noise does in such games is select an equilibrium, not reproduce the set, and which one it selects depends on what the noise is about. Noise about the size of the pie gives the equal split, because that is Nash's own 1953 way of smoothing the demand game, making it slightly uncertain whether two demands fit; under it demanding everything is never an equilibrium, and in the repeated-games theorist's computation for two sides sharing six, the demands rise from about $2.6$ each toward $3$ each and the chance of agreement from $78$ per cent to $99.9$ per cent as the noise falls from one slice to a hundredth; with four parties the same logic lands on $1.5$ each. Commitments that fail with probability $1-q$, made at the same moment, unable to read each other, cheap relative to the pie and made once, give the standoff with probability $q^2$. Durable commitments that lapse at random give delay and then agreement. Which errors agents make is a design fact, not a budget fact. And in models where players learn by imitation with rare mistakes, the time to leave a convention, a way of playing that each side expects the other to follow, grows without bound as mistakes vanish, so under the model's own mapping, errors vanishing as $c\to\infty$, selection takes forever and history decides. *Action:* delete the sentence. Replace it with: "where the cell has one outcome, B and C, more precise agents approach it; in A and D the limit is whichever equilibrium the agents' remaining errors, their design and their history select, and better agents hold that selection longer."

**Compute enters the model elsewhere, as commitment and as speed, so a side's commitment power and its computing budget are not separate dials.** The model says: "(This is the only place "compute" enters, and its unit is operations.)" The repeated-games theorist found a literature in which bounded computation is itself a commitment device, and it runs against the model's sign. A player who can only follow a simple fixed rule cannot be talked out of it, while a player who can work out a best reply to anything will give one; so the side that adapts concedes and the side that is fixed, by limitation or by design, commits. The standard results, in plain terms: a fixed rule that threatens to punish extracts surplus from an opponent who keeps adjusting toward whatever did best (Press and Dyson 2012); a player facing an opponent who learns in that way gets at least what a first mover would get (Deng, Schneider and Sivan 2019); a player with unlimited computation facing one who can only run a small fixed program ends up accommodating it (Gilboa and Samet 1989). On the river, if the residents' agents learn against a fixed factory offer, the residents' share goes to $0$; if the factory's agent learns against a fixed residents' demand, it goes to $1$. Raw compute makes a side a better best-replier, which makes it the follower; compute buys commitment power only when it is spent on shaping the other side's learning or on verifying conditional commitments, instructions that read the other side's instructions before acting, which is spending on the revocation cost $r$, not on the estimation error. ### Under "6. The one place the ratio of compute matters: the race at $k=1$"

### Under "7. What this says about Krier, in his own terms"

**The digital union is cell C by capability and cell B by outcome, and what holds it together is contract and the club's own channel, which Krier keeps, not a statute.** The model says: "His digital union of residents' agents is cell C, and it works exactly if the pact among the residents is enforced against side deals, which is a rule, not an agent." The advocate, reading Krier's text, found three problems. **Krier's developer has no commitment power, so Krier's developer is not cell B, whatever real developers do.** The model says: "His developer who offers every homeowner a price is cell B, and the better the developer's agent knows each homeowner's floor, the less it pays." The advocate found no textual basis. **The option fee is a tax on the residents' demands; the reimbursing veto is Calabresi and Melamed's fourth rule, a liability rule that protects the polluter, not the one the model means, and it can put residents below no deal.** The model says: "His option fee and his reimbursing veto are liability rules, cell B written into law with the payment fixed by a formula rather than by the factory's estimate, which is why they work and why they are regulation." The advocate found half of this right and half wrong. Calabresi and Melamed 1972 set out four rules for a nuisance like the river's: the residents may stop the pollution outright; the factory may pollute but must pay damages the court sets; the factory may pollute freely; or the residents may stop the pollution only by compensating the factory. The second and fourth are liability rules, rules under which a right can be taken at a set price; "cell B written into law" is the second. The option fee, a self-assessed value on which the resident pays a fee and at which the resident must sell, does make declared values approach true values and does give a cell-B-like outcome; but it is a device on the residents' side, a cost on naming a high demand, and in the model's own terms it attacks the standoff directly by making the residents' aggressive position expensive, rather than legislating cell B; Krier offers it as a club rule that can be voluntary, so "written into law" is the model's phrase. The reimbursing veto, under which any coalition that vetoes must reimburse the quantified loss, is the fourth rule, not the second. Run on the river with the factory committed to paying $(2,3,9)$ and the three residents sharing any reimbursement equally: each resident compares accepting, net $0$, with vetoing and paying a third of the factory's $20$, so all accept and the residents' share is $0$. Run with the factory committed to $(1,2,8)$: accepting costs each resident $1$ while vetoing costs each a third of $20$, so all accept and end at $-1$, below no deal, which breaks the floor the first section says unanimity guarantees. At $\pi=10$, where the clean river is right, no group of residents finds a veto worth its share of the factory's forgone $10$: resident 3 alone would pay $10$ to avoid a harm of $9$; residents 2 and 3 together would pay $5$ each to avoid $3$ and $9$, and resident 2 declines; all three would pay $10/3$ each and residents 1 and 2 decline; so the factory pollutes, the wrong outcome. Reimbursing the net loss instead, profit minus harms, requires someone to quantify the harms, which is the public authority's problem back again, the very thing bargaining was to replace. *Action:* keep the option fee, relabelled as a residents'-side device that prices aggressive commitment; move the reimbursing veto out of cell B and into the list of exits from the standoff, as a cost on holding out, and say what it does to the floor.

**Krier has no free-bargaining club, and the club he has fails on arithmetic rather than on commitment.** The model says: "His bargaining club, in which agents negotiate freely and all can commit, is cell D, and the better the agents, the more reliably it fails to close." The advocate found one club in Krier's text, and it is not this one: homeowners pool their agents, each privately enters the minimum they would accept, the software aggregates these into a single take-it-or-leave-it offer to the developer, and if it clears everyone is paid at a common clearing price, so padding your number, entering a minimum above your true one, does not raise your cheque. The attack the model missed is on the river's numbers: a common clearing price must be at least $9$ to bring in resident 3, so three residents cost $27$ against a profit of $20$, and the club kills an efficient deal; if instead each is paid their own minimum, padding pays whenever the deal still clears, and the incentive claim fails. **The small-stakes exemption holds, and the cited theory supplies the mechanism the model lacked.** The model says: "The long tail of his small examples, the thermostat and the parking, is cell A with a custom already pinning the split, where none of this bites." The cold reader objected that nothing in the model keeps small cases out of the standoff if committing costs "almost nothing" at any stake. The economist and I found the answer in Ellingsen and Miettinen's own text: "it is the ratio of the commitment cost to the size of the surplus that matters for the likelihood of conflict ... our model is consistent with the notion that some issues are too small to be fighting about. Conversely, any sufficiently big issue invites conflict." A commitment cost fixed in money is large relative to a thermostat's surplus and small relative to a factory's: a posting cost of $0.3$ is one twentieth of the river's pie of $6$ and three fifths of a thermostat dispute worth half a slice. The advocate adds that Krier's small cases are repeated games among a fixed small group, where reputation enforces, and that "custom" is standing in for repetition. *Action:* keep the sentence and give it this mechanism: the cost of committing relative to the pie, with repetition doing the rest.

**The closing sentence stakes the essay on the least-supported cell.** The model says: "It is that in the large cases his program is sold on, what the agents cut costs *of* is commitment, and cheap commitment for everyone is a standoff." ### Under "8. What would make this wrong"

**The first falsifier points the wrong way, and the other two cannot fire.** The model says: "If agents in practice do not commit, because they are built to be agreeable, then the world stays in cell A and the claim about D is idle." Then: "If the cost of a standoff is large enough that one side always backs down in D, then D collapses into B or C according to who backs down". Then: "If at $k=1$ the residents' pact assembles far faster than the factory's single offer, which is Krier's "flash coalition in a nanosecond", the race in section 6 goes to the residents whatever the ratio of compute, and the one-shot empty core stops mattering." A falsifier here is one of the three things the model says would make it wrong. On the first, the behavioural economist found that agreeableness is one-sided in practice: firms' agents are limited in what they may concede and sometimes publicly committed while counterparties' assistants accommodate, accepting the first proposal far more often than the best one and ignoring instructions to be aggressive, so one-sided agreeableness does not keep the world in cell A, it hands the outcome to cell B; the standoff cell is probably idle at consumer stakes, in their judgment about $80$ to $90$ per cent likely over the next few years, but idle in B's favour. On the second, the cold reader found that it cannot fire from inside the model: the cell is defined by revocation costs above the pie, and the only cost of a standoff the model allows is the lost pie of $6$, which is less than the revocation cost by construction, so a side that does back down had a small revocation cost and was never in the cell; a rule that imposes a larger cost on holding out, such as Krier's reimbursing veto, can make backing down pay, but that is an institution, not the model's own mechanism; and the repeated-games theorist found that when durable commitments can lapse the result is a war of attrition, a contest in which each side waits for the other to give in and the pie shrinks with delay, which ends in agreement, not a collapse into B or C. On the third, the cooperative game theorist found that speed cannot repair stability: the $k=1$ game's core is empty, meaning there is no division from which no group of parties would walk away, so once a pact exists residents 1 and 2 each gain by leaving it, up to $8$ and $7$ against $0$, however fast it was formed; and the repeated-games theorist found it fails at every discount factor, the weight a party puts on next year's payoff, if the factory can offer a defector a long-term contract. *Action:* replace the three falsifiers with the ones the findings imply: that agents in practice publish in sequence rather than at the same sealed moment; that their instructions can read each other; and how often principals, the people the agents act for, override published agents, which fixes $1-q$, the chance a commitment fails to stick, the quantity, with the cost of committing relative to the pie, on which Ellingsen and Miettinen's result that cheap simultaneous commitments end in conflict with probability $q^2$ turns.

## What survives, in one list

- Whoever alone can commit takes the pie. Cell B holds as the ultimatum game, in which one party proposes and the other can only accept or refuse, with three responders; cell C as its mirror, with the factory responding; both for bargainers who accept a penny over nothing, which software agents do and people often do not, provided the responders answer one at a time or as one bloc; C also needs the residents' split fixed before any of them commits. They are the only cells with a single outcome under perfectly reliable commitment.
- Unless the instructions can read each other, cheap commitment on both sides is a race to commit first, won by whoever commits first and most cheaply; on the river that is the factory, one claimant against three residents who collide if they commit separately and are slower if they wait to agree.
- Ellingsen and Miettinen's theorem, correctly stated: once-off, simultaneous, observable commitments that cannot read each other, are cheap relative to the pie and stick with probability $q<1$ give a unique outcome in which both sides demand everything and conflict occurs with probability $q^2$. Near-perfect but imperfect commitment is the standoff's home, and at a fixed commitment cost "any sufficiently big issue invites conflict".
- Miettinen and Vanberg's theorem: when each party can declare a minimum share that becomes binding with some probability, unanimity is uniquely inefficient and every other voting rule is efficient. The standoff with a theorem that fits the river's own structure is holdout, refusal in order to be paid more, among the residents under $k=3$; Ellingsen and Miettinen's is the theorem for two sides.
- Institutions choose the cell, in the weak form in which a rule assigning commitment to one side fixes the division. Calabresi and Melamed said it in 1972 and Krier says it now.
- In cell B, factory compute raises the deal rate and lowers the residents' share of each deal, more slowly than the model says and down to a floor set by whatever part of the harms cannot be measured, if any; the residents' expected surplus, counting failed deals as zero, peaks at a middling budget because at low budgets most deals fail.
- Compute chooses the cell by how and by whom it is spent: on committing first, on committing most cheaply, or on staying fixed while the other side adapts and concedes; on the river the first two favour the factory.
- In the cells with no single outcome, A and D under perfectly reliable commitment, compute acts as asymmetries rather than as convergence: the better agent takes more even in plain haggling, unnoticed by principals; and whatever convention, a way of playing each side expects the other to follow, forms first, better agents hold longer.
- The one-shot framing survives for permanent rights sold once between parties who do not deal again; for consents renewed each year between patient parties the table determines nothing.
