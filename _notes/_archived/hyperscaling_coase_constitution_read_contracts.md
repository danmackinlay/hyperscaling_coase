# Choosing the street's constitution

Terms. The *constitution* is the set of rules under which the street's agents bargain, including the rule for changing the rules. *Ex ante* means before a party knows its own position (its harms and gains); *interim* means once it knows its own position but not the others'; *ex post* means once everything is known. The *veil* is the condition of choosing rules ex ante.

## Answer to A

Bargaining over the constitution is different in kind from bargaining under it, and the literature finds differences in both directions. It is easier when the rule covers many future decisions and nobody can tell which side of them they will be on: interests then coincide, and the impossibility results for bargaining with private information do not bite (proved in models: Aghion and Bolton; d'Aspremont and Gérard-Varet). It is harder because a rule lasts: the longer an agreement will run, the harder each side holds out (Fearon), a deal that becomes tomorrow's default makes parties vote by fixed interest (Dziuda and Loeper), and forward-looking parties block improvements that would shift future rule-making power (Acemoglu, Egorov and Sonin). On capture, Aghion, Alesina and Trebbi show that rule-writers who know they will hold power choose rules that suit themselves. Unknown: I found no source comparing what is extracted or wasted per unit of effort in a contest over rules with a contest inside deals. Finding 4's gap stands.

## Answer to B

No source proves that an untiltable procedure exists in general. The literature instead lists what each known route needs. Ignorance of future position, or legal substitutes for it: rules that are prospective, general, durable and delayed (Vermeule). Participation that is either compulsory before positions are known, or voluntary from a starting entitlement that somebody has set correctly (Cramton, Gibbons and Klemperer; Segal and Whinston). An enforcer outside the bargain, or enough repetition to do without one (Acemoglu; Maggi and Morelli). And an amendment rule that survives its own vote, which in general must be separate from and stricter than the rule for ordinary business (Barberà and Jackson). Every item is supplied from outside the bargain it protects. Whether a first constitution can be chosen fairly with no prior rule is unresolved; Ostrom notes that any analysis has to hold some deeper layer of rules fixed.

## Answer to C

The conjecture is in print in its general form and has been since 1962. Buchanan and Tullock rest constitutional agreement on the chooser being "unable to predict the role that he will be playing", and they list predictability of issues and outcomes as the conditions under which he backs partisan rules. Aghion, Alesina and Trebbi model it; Vermeule catalogues the legal devices that depend on it; Hirshleifer proves the insurance version; AI-governance writers (MacAskill and Moorhouse, Mahoney, O'Keefe) say the window for fair agreement closes as winners become identifiable. What I did not find is the specific step: that cheap forecasting by personal agents, as distinct from events revealing winners, thins the veil for everyday rule-making. On a search of about thirty-five sources, that application appears to be unpublished. It is supported, subject to the results listed under "What cuts against the thesis". Restated in the terms above, the conjecture says that forecasting moves rule choice from the ex ante stage to the interim stage, where each party can refuse; that restatement is mine and is not proved anywhere I read.

## Constitutions as incomplete contracts

Aghion and Bolton (2003) put a two-period model behind the veil: citizens first choose the share of votes needed to pass a reform, then learn who gains and who loses. If the founding contract could specify every contingency, unanimity would be the best rule. A lower threshold becomes optimal only because compensating losers has a deadweight cost; the optimal threshold falls with that cost and rises with the scope for a majority to expropriate a minority. The rule for amending the threshold is left as future work. Aghion, Alesina and Trebbi (2004) choose instead the share of votes needed to block a leader. Behind the veil everyone prefers the same threshold. Once people know their wealth they want different ones; under unanimity the wealthy veto, and the authors conclude that bargaining over the constitution makes its content depend on the bargaining rules and the distribution of wealth. A minority that knows it will appoint the leader chooses more insulation than the veil would.

*For the street.* If agents make compensation nearly free and deals nearly complete, Aghion and Bolton's own model moves the consent share toward unanimity, which is Krier's direction. If the delivery company's agent or the platform drafts the rules knowing it will always be the proposer, Aghion, Alesina and Trebbi predict rules that insulate the proposer.

## Rules that choose themselves

Barberà and Jackson (2004) call a voting rule *self-stable* if no alternative rule beats it under its own threshold. Each voter is described by a known probability of favouring change. If all voters share that probability, simple majority is the unique self-stable rule (for an odd number of voters). If they differ, a constitution that uses one rule for both ordinary business and amendment can fail to be self-stable. A pair of rules repairs this: majority for business with unanimity to amend is always self-stable, as is the median voter's preferred rule with any amendment threshold of at least a majority. Messner and Polborn (2004) let overlapping generations vote on the threshold for future reforms. Each voter foresees growing older and more exposed to reform costs, so the median voter picks a supermajority (a threshold above half), fitted to his average future self and too conservative for anyone newly arrived. Acemoglu, Egorov and Sonin (2012) show that patient, forward-looking players refuse any change of rules that would open the way to a further change they dislike; rules that everyone would prefer to replace can be stable for that reason.

*For the street.* Alice, Bob and Carol with identical prospects agree on majority; with known different prospects they need a stricter amendment rule to hold any rule in place. Dave, who buys Bob's house, inherits a threshold chosen for Bob's future self. The neighbours may refuse to admit the company as a voting party, even when that helps everyone today, because of what it could then amend.

## Bargaining when the rule itself can change

Acemoglu (2003): efficient political bargains fail because whoever holds power cannot promise how they will use it, and nobody outside enforces promises made by the rule-makers. Fearon (1998): a longer expected life for an agreement makes it easier to enforce and harder to reach, since the prize for holding out grows with duration. Dziuda and Loeper (2016): when the last agreement is the next default, parties disagree more often than their preferences do, and patient parties can reach complete gridlock; the authors favour sunset clauses. Maggi and Morelli (2006): with no outside enforcer, majority rule is sustainable only if members are patient or decisions frequent; otherwise unanimity is the best self-enforcing rule. Diermeier and Fong (2011) cut the other way: when a single proposer can reopen any decision, the other voters protect one another's shares, and the proposer's power shrinks.

*For the street.* Fixing a five-year rule is a harder bargain than pricing tonight's slot. Sunset clauses ease it, and they remove the commitment that the earlier read found Carol's double glazing needs.

## The veil

Vermeule (2001) shows how law manufactures a veil when real choosers know who they are: prospectivity, generality, durability and delayed effect each make the incidence of a rule harder to predict. All four assume that prediction is harder than hindsight. The insurance theory of judicial review, as surveyed by Vanberg (2015), has the same shape: parties accept constraint when they may lose office, and a party sure of winning has no bet to hedge. In the laboratory, Durante, Putterman and van der Weele find that a subject's own payoff drives the chosen tax rate more once position is known; Engelmann and co-authors find that subjects behind the veil choose thresholds with no systematic bias and capture about 63 percent of the available surplus. Hirshleifer (1971) shows that in pure exchange, public information released before trade leaves risk-averse parties worse off in expectation, because it removes what they could have insured.

## What ex ante agreement buys and what it needs

Myerson and Satterthwaite's impossibility (original not opened) says that a seller and a buyer who each know only their own value cannot always trade efficiently if each may walk away and no outsider adds money. d'Aspremont and Gérard-Varet (1979) show that an efficient mechanism that is *incentive-compatible* (truth-telling is each party's best reply) and *budget-balanced* (payments sum to zero) exists when private information is independent. Their theorem has no participation condition: the parties are taken to be already bound. Cramton, Gibbons and Klemperer (1987) restore voluntary participation at the interim stage by changing the entitlement: when ownership is shared nearly equally, efficient reallocation is possible, and when one party owns everything it is not. Segal and Whinston (2011) generalize: with independent private information and a convexity condition, participation is assured if the starting entitlement is the expected efficient allocation. Their 2016 paper (abstract only) shows that for wide classes of simple property rights and liability rules efficient bargaining is impossible without a subsidy. Holmström and Myerson (1983) add that a rule which is efficient given the incentive constraints can still be unanimously abandoned once a party's private knowledge shows that everyone prefers something else; they call a rule *durable* if it resists this.

*For the street.* Ignorance is one way to obtain participation. The other is an entitlement set at the expected efficient number of night slots, and somebody outside the bargain must compute and impose it. A rule agreed before Carol knows she is a light sleeper may not survive her finding out.

## The cost of describing contingencies

This section is thin: I read abstracts only for Battigalli and Maggi (2002) and Tirole (2009). Both derive incompleteness from a cost, of writing clauses in the first and of thinking through contingencies in the second, so cheaper agents mean fuller contracts. Al-Najjar, Anderlini and Felli (2006) show that some events are understood by everyone and yet cannot be captured by any finite description; where those are the only risk, the best finite contract is no contract. One reason for brevity survives free description, and it is an inference of mine from Vermeule, not a published result: generality and durability are what produce the veil, so a constitution detailed enough for agents to compute its incidence on each household has lost that property.

## Communities writing their own rules

Ostrom (1990) separates operational rules, the collective-choice rules for changing them, and constitutional-choice rules for changing those. Deeper layers are harder to change, and a small community's constitutional rules sit inside those of larger jurisdictions, which answers the question of whether a newcomer is bound. Strata law is an instance: in New South Wales a by-law change needs a special resolution with no more than a quarter of votes against (snippets only; check the Act).

## What Krier already says about this

Krier does not claim that agents bargain over the constitution by willingness to pay. He says the baseline of entitlements is a normative, collective choice that agents make explicit, contestable and cheap to renegotiate; that centuries of jurisprudence are the starting point; and that changes belong to democratic politics, which agents can improve through continuous delegated voting. He separates an economic mode from a one-person-one-vote mode for community values. He concedes that constant renegotiation of property rights would destroy investment, and proposes that some rules ossify by mutual agreement. He raises Acemoglu's political Coase theorem himself and answers it with transparency and automated auditing. He keeps the state to define and enforce property, contract and constitutional rights, and admits that mechanisms to stop minorities holding majorities hostage are still to be designed. He does not say who assigns a matter to a mode, how the threshold for changing the baseline is chosen, or what forecasting does to that choice.

## What cuts against the thesis

- Aghion and Bolton: with complete contracts and free compensation, unanimity is the optimal rule. Agents push toward both.
- Maggi and Morelli: frequent decisions make majority rule self-enforcing without an outside enforcer. Agents make decisions frequent.
- Segal and Whinston; Cramton, Gibbons and Klemperer: the right entitlement gives efficient, voluntary bargaining at the interim stage. A veil is sufficient and not necessary.
- Fernandez and Rodrik (1991): when individuals cannot tell whether they will gain, a majority can reject a reform that most would support afterwards. Here foresight helps agreement.
- Müller (1998, abstract only): where the underlying problem is a prisoners' dilemma, fully informed players agree on an efficient rule and a veil adds nothing.
- Hirshleifer's own caveat: information has value where it redirects production, which must be set against the lost insurance.
- Diermeier and Fong: the ability to reopen decisions weakens a monopoly proposer.
- Vermeule: durability and delay rebuild the veil so long as distant positions stay unpredictable. I found no evidence on how far ahead agents can forecast a household's position.
