## The three answers

**1. Is bargaining over the constitution different from bargaining under it?**
Yes, but the reads found no result about computation in particular.
It is proved in models that choosing rules before anyone knows their own position (the "ex ante" stage) can avoid the impossibility of efficient bargaining with private information (Aghion and Bolton 2003; d'Aspremont and Gérard-Varet 1979).
It is also proved that durable rules are harder to agree: the prize for holding out grows with the agreement's life (Fearon 1998), and forward-looking parties block changes that would shift later rule-making power (Acemoglu, Egorov and Sonin 2012).
Neither read found a theorem comparing what computation buys in choosing a rule with what it buys in playing under one; the mechanism read's remark that at the street's size neither is hard is its own inference.

**2. Do constitutions exist that provably remove the advantage of the side with more computation?**
Only under narrow assumptions.
It is proved that under a truthful rule (honest reporting is best whatever others do), modelling opponents buys nothing if valuations are private and known at no cost.
Once a neighbour must compute their own valuation, only "separable" rules, in which reports do not interact, such as posted prices, remove the incentive to learn about others (Gleyze and Pernoud 2023).
If the company can infer more about a neighbour's harm than the neighbour knows, generically only constant rules work (Jehiel et al. 2006).
The contracts read found no proof that a procedure for choosing the constitution can be made immune to tilting; every known route needs something from outside the bargain, such as an outside enforcer.
Nobody found a rule that protects a weaker party when the stronger one also chooses the rule; the one model found says the rule-chooser takes nearly everything from a naive learning agent (Braverman et al. 2018).

**3. Do those constitutions still let computation do useful work?**
Mostly not.
Posted prices and ascending clocks remove the advantage most completely and cannot search for better schedules.
VCG (Vickrey–Clarke–Groves, a rule that picks the welfare-maximizing outcome and charges each party for its effect on the others) keeps the gains: each party's return to computing equals the social return, and stronger search can enter as a proposal that raises the total (Nisan and Ronen 2007).
But it is proved that VCG cannot balance its payments, that no efficient auction resists false identities, and that the alignment breaks if optimization is only approximate.
The size of these losses with four parties is unknown, and no experiment varies agent capability across rule types.

## Where the two reads agree, and where they pull apart

They agree that whoever drafts or operates the rules can tilt them, and that protection has to come from outside the bargain.
They pull apart on ex ante agreement: the contracts read treats d'Aspremont and Gérard-Varet as showing that early agreement escapes the impossibility results.
The mechanism read, from memory, notes that this mechanism is truthful only on average given beliefs about others, so computation spent on modelling opponents would pay again.
If so, the contracts read's route around the impossibility does not remove the computational advantage, a question the contracts read never asks.

## Does a thesis come out of this?

One claim both reads would support, and that is specific to delegated agents: if personal agents can forecast each household's position and infer its harms better than the household knows them, they remove both the ignorance that makes ex ante rule choice consensual and the private-values assumption that makes truthful rules work, so the protections that remain must be fixed and run from outside the agents' bargaining.
By the contracts read's own search, the step from cheap forecasting to a thinner veil (the condition of choosing rules ex ante) is unpublished.

What would most easily knock it down is Vermeule's point that durable, delayed and general rules rebuild ignorance so long as distant positions stay unpredictable; neither read found evidence on how far ahead agents can forecast.
Segal and Whinston also show that a correctly set starting entitlement gives voluntary efficient bargaining without any veil.
