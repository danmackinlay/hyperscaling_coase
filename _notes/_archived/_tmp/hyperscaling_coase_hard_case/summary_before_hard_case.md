## Where the existence test stands

The null fails, and the essay exists, but the optimist's advocate changed what its spine is. Argued as hard as it goes, the null survives on three rows under one condition the table had never written down: that the dials are symmetric across the parties. With equal information about past play, equal commitment reliability and nobody systematically first to move, capable agents converge on the equal split of $1.5$ each, which is the Nash-product maximiser, with no rule supplied: by theorem in the two-party models and by extension on the four-party river, in haggling under recurrent adaptive play (row 3), under short-lived commitments attempted in a random order (row 6), and in patient repeated play (row 9). The same theorems say what an asymmetry does: the bigger sampler of past play, the more reliable committer and the earlier mover take more, and the Nash product falls. Four conditions fail under any reading, symmetric or not, over five rows: sealed unconditional commitment, where the pie is lost with probability $q^2$ (row 5); simultaneous binding declarations under unanimity, where delay grows with reliability (row 8); and divide and conquer at $k=1$ or $2$, where every resident ends below no deal whatever is spent, once (rows 10 and 15) or every year (row 13). And nothing in "give everyone an agent" makes the dials symmetric: one principal against three is first to commit when speed sets the order; a repeat player samples more history than one-shot residents; the side the rules let commit is the side that commits; whoever holds the entitlement sets the threat point. So the essay's spine is now two lists rather than one: the symmetry conditions the agenda presupposes without supplying, and the rows where even symmetry fails together with the rules that repair them. The verdict is the same under Dan's criterion as under every candidate the criterion agent ran (the null fails under the Nash product through rows 5 and 10 alone), and under it rows 1 and 2 also count against compute past a middling budget. **The three symmetric rows go to Dan before anything is drafted**, as the brief required. The mission's own paragraph on where the table stands predates this round and lists sequential commitment and repeated play among the failures; updating it is Dan's call.

## The table: conditions against compute's effect

Notation. $x=1$ if the factory pollutes, else $0$; $t_i$ is the signed transfer to resident $i$; payoffs are $u_f=\pi x-\sum t_i$ and $u_i=t_i-h_ix$, written $(f;\,r_1,r_2,r_3)$, so the clean river with no transfers is $(0;\,0,0,0)$ under every rule. **The criterion is Dan's (mission, 2026-10-21): the Nash product of gains over no deal**, computed here as the product of the four parties' expected gains with the rule's no-deal outcome as origin, which at $k=3$ is $0$ for everyone; its maximum on the pie of $6$ is $1.5^4=5.06$; it is $0$ when any party gains nothing and undefined when any party's expected gain is negative, and in those cases **the floor check**, the lowest expected gain among the four, carries the row. Two blind spots of the product, recorded by the criterion agent: a failed deal and a deal that hands one party everything both score $0$, which the efficiency column separates; and gains are measured from the rule's own no-deal point, so at $k=0$ the origin is pollution. "Ex post" values are expectations of the realised minimum, which differ when the outcome is a lottery. $q$ is always the probability that a commitment sticks; $\delta$ is the discount factor between rounds of one negotiation or between years, as the row says; $\sigma$ is the error of an estimate; $s_i$ is the number of past plays a party samples. Confidence marks are the reviewers' own: [sure] read or certain, [likely] remembered, [unsure] guessed. Status vocabulary: *theorem* only where a session read the primary, named, with "two-party" flagged where the river has four; *cited, not read* for a standard result nobody has read; *computed* only where re-run, with the script named (all under `_tmp/hyperscaling_coase_existence_test/`); *argued* with the count of independent reviewers; *empirical* with the study named. "One claimant beats three" means that a single principal commits faster and more cheaply than three who must first agree. "Core deficit" is the amount by which the claims of the factory-plus-one-resident coalitions exceed what all four parties are worth together.
