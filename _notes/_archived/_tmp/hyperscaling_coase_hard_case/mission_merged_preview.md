# hyperscaling_coase — mission

Read before anything else. The stable half of a pair; the state is `hyperscaling_coase_summary.md`. Written 2026-10-21 after a round that answered the wrong question well.

## The question

**Under which conditions does more computation for bargaining agents produce better outcomes, and for whom?** "Better" means better under a welfare criterion in which inequality counts, not merely Pareto-better; see the measures below. The target is the computational-optimist agenda, the claim that cheap, capable negotiating agents let private bargaining replace regulation and reach good outcomes by default. Krier's "Coasean Bargaining at Scale" is the foil, with Shahidi et al. and Petel's sympathetic version beside it; the essay says if, when and whom that agenda favours, and treats the foil fairly. (Dan, 2026-10: "respond to the Coasean Bargaining at scale agenda with a productive analysis of if and when and who this agenda is likely to favour in general"; and: "ubiquitous cheap bargaining agents, as their budget for computation increases, do not necessarily lead to equitable outcomes in adversarial settings".)


## The existence test

There must be an essay to write, or we pack up. The null: give everyone a moderately capable agent and, in every setting, outcomes converge by themselves to something recognisably just under a welfare criterion stronger than Pareto; then Krier is right, utopia arrives by default, and there is nothing to say. The alternative: the just outcomes of Coasean bargaining smuggle in conditions, premises and pre-game negotiation that the agenda does not handle and that are not available by default; there are settings in which compute does not help uniformly or equitably; and there may be settings in which more compute favours incumbents, meaning whoever already holds the entitlement, is already one principal rather than many, already has the better agent, already set the convention, or can already stay rigid while others adapt. If the alternative holds, there is an essay, and its spine is the list of smuggled conditions. (Dan, 2026-10-21.)

The null fails on both members of the family. On the river it fails on sealed commitment, simultaneous declarations and divide and conquer, and survives on haggling, sequential commitment and repeated play only under symmetric dials. On the noise schedule it fails on private sensitivities, the enforcer regress below unanimity, a misspecified preference model and a one-sided outside option, and survives on the remaining rows only under a list of rules somebody outside the bargain supplies. The essay exists; its spine is the two lists, the symmetries the agenda presupposes and the rules that repair the rows symmetry cannot, each rule carrying a distributional choice made before the bargain. The next round tests whether the three nulls stated below add a third list: the conditions under which more compute for one side captures value from the other below the regulated status quo.


## The model, as a family

The model is a family, not one instance. A single principal (the river's factory, the noise schedule's fleet, the humanised documents' company) faces $n$ residents (the humanised documents' neighbours) with public exposures and private sensitivities, under a consent rule $k$ of $n$, with transfers, and with switches that each remove one feature that makes bargaining easy. Its members so far are the river ($n=3$, one shot, one public action, one private number per resident, linear money, an enforcer assumed, a closed system) and the noise schedule ($n=3$, four slots, the switches of `hyperscaling_coase_hard_case_instance.md`). An arm of a round may take $n$ to thirty or three hundred, add repeated play under a stated learning rule for each side's agent, add a formation stage in which residents may organise before the principal's offers arrive, state an information regime (who can observe or verify whose harms, offers and past dealings), or open the system (one principal against a sequence of neighbourhoods; two neighbourhoods competing for one deal), provided it states every parameter and every distribution it uses, in the instance note Dan signs before anything runs, and reports the $n=3$ case alongside for comparison with the river. A protocol is the rule for who proposes a schedule and payments each period and who must consent; the seat is the proposer's position under a protocol; a party's take is its expected gain over no deal per period, in money, or in utility units where wealth effects are on; the seat share is the take the seat earns in the limit with equal compute on both sides, under the protocols the instance note names and defines. The one-model rule becomes the one-family rule: an arm that cannot be stated as parameters of this family is a sequel. Its known unrealistic features stay tracked rather than assumed away: the no-deal origin is fixed by the rule, where an open system lets a party move it; the agents are identified with the people they act for, where a vendor has its own payoff; and the criterion counts parties, which the next section addresses.


## What "more compute" can buy (the dials)

Each is a separate dial, because the round found they pull in different directions. An artefact that says "more compute" must say which.

| Dial | What it changes | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Estimation | the error in a side's estimate of a number it does not know (a harm, the pie, the other's floor) | operations; error $\sigma/\sqrt{c}$ |
| Speed | who publishes a binding position first | time to commit |
| Commitment | how cheaply a side can post and prove a bond, and how reliably it sticks | cost of committing relative to the pie; probability $q$ it sticks |
| Adaptation | how well a side best-replies to the other; its opposite is rigidity | regret per round |


## Outcome measures

"Better" must be inequality-sensitive. **The primary criterion is the geometric mean of the parties' gains over no deal**, the $n{+}1$-th root of the Nash product, in utility units when money has wealth effects. For a fixed number of parties it ranks outcomes exactly as the product does, so nothing computed under the product changes; across different numbers of parties it is a per-party average, and it is reported as a fraction of its maximum at that $n$, which is attained by the equal split at every $n$. The floor check is the worst-off party's gain over no deal, unchanged. Reported alongside, without counting as "better": efficiency under linear utility; the residents' share and the split among residents; the fraction of residents at or below no deal; and expected values at finite budgets counting failed deals as zero.

The criterion ranks voluntary outcomes only: it is zero when any party gains nothing and undefined when any party loses, and that silence is not a judgement. Since the outcomes the essay most needs to call worse are imposed ones (consent below unanimity, a liability rule with assessment error, harm exported to a neighbourhood with no say, the regulated status quo itself), the ordering is completed as follows, which is what the state has done tacitly. **An outcome in which any party ends below no deal ranks below every outcome in which none does. Among outcomes with losers, rank by the worst-off party's gain over no deal, then by the fraction of residents below it. Among outcomes with no losers, rank by the geometric mean of gains.** Every tier uses the bargaining origin, no deal. The comparison with the regulated status quo of the next section is reported beside the ordering, resident by resident, and is not folded into it, because under an ordinance that lets the principal act for free every voluntary deal leaves the principal below the status quo, which is not a harm the essay counts. Four quantities, fixed so no two agents compute them differently: the maximum at $n$ is the largest geometric mean over divisions of the realised pie, the equal split under linear money and the utility-Nash division under wealth effects; where some party has no positive gain, the geometric mean is taken over the parties with positive gains and that number is reported; at finite budgets the criterion is the geometric mean of expected gains, a failed deal counting as $0$ for every party; the fraction below is of residents, the principal's position reported separately. This is Rawlsian at the bottom and Nash at the top; it never trades one party's loss against others' gains, which is the right strength for a thesis that says everyone gains by default. The alternative secondary criterion, which Dan may prefer, named here so the choice is visible. Every criterion above scores a party by her gain, her position after the outcome minus her position at no deal. One could instead score each party by her level, her position after the outcome with payments and harms in (Carol at 2 before, 3.5 after a payment of 1.5), and add the levels up with a rule that weights the poorest more: Atkinson's family, which sums each party's wealth raised to the power $1-\epsilon$ and divides by $1-\epsilon$, so that $\epsilon=0$ is the plain sum, $\epsilon\to\infty$ is the worst-off party alone, and $\epsilon=1$ is the sum of logarithms. A levels criterion ranks every outcome, losers included, because a level is always defined where a gain can be negative; that is its advantage. Its costs are two. It sees endowments, so its maximiser is not the equal split but a transfer toward the poorest, and the optimist's target moves. And its curvature is chosen, not forced: the Nash product has the same logarithmic shape over gains, but there the bargaining axioms force it, whereas over levels nothing picks $\epsilon=1$ over $2$, which is the aversion parameter the mission rejected as one dial too many. [Dan: choose; the draft proceeds under the completion above.]

Three things the criterion assumes, stated so that no round rediscovers them. Who counts as a party: each resident is one party, the principal is one party, and a party that splits into several agents still counts once; the Sybil result (state, row 26) is why. Zeros and losses at scale: in arms with many residents some party will gain nothing or lose, so the lower tiers carry the row, and the geometric mean is reported among the parties with positive gains with the fraction below beside it. The origin: gains are measured from the rule's own no-deal point, which the open-system cases show a party with more than one thing to trade can move; the comparison with the status quo of the next section is the number that sees it. [For Dan: the mission's numbered list 1–4 under the measures and the last sentence of "Why this criterion" are river leftovers; replace the list with the "reported alongside" sentence above, and the alternatives sentence with: the minimum payoff, adopted only as the lower tier of the completed ordering; an Atkinson aggregator over levels, the named alternative above.]

1. Efficiency, as a placeholder only: does the efficient action under linear utility happen (pollute at $\pi=20$, not at $\pi=10$), and how often.
2. The residents' share of each deal, $S$, with the within-residents split reported alongside, since $S$ is blind to it.
3. The worst-off party's payoff, as the inequality-sensitive stand-in: who ends lowest, and how far below their no-deal payoff of $0$.
4. Expected surplus counting failed deals as zero, which is what a resident actually gets.

Why this criterion: it is inequality-averse in the required way, scale-free, standard, and it is what the symmetric bargaining solutions the optimist leans on already maximise, so the null has a clean target, whether agents reach the Nash-product maximiser without added rules, and cannot dispute the yardstick. Alternatives considered and not adopted: the minimum payoff as primary (Rawlsian, under which the optimist loses too easily); an Atkinson-style concave sum with a stated aversion parameter (one dial too many).


## The status quo

"Worse" in the essay means worse than the regulated status quo, not worse than no deal, and the status quo is a stylised regulation, not the regulation where the reader lives. (The hard-case instance note's word "baseline" means its all-switches-off instance, called the base instance from now on; the status quo is this section's.) It is one of two, named by each arm; both are reported where both are computable, and the ordinance is the default because it is what noise and pollution regulation mostly is. (i) **An ordinance**: the council closes the slots a rule names and permits the rest, with no compensation. On the noise schedule the ordinance closes the night slot: the principal runs morning, midday and evening for $13.5$, and the residents bear harms of $1$, $2.5$ and $4$ unpaid, so the status quo leaves every resident below no deal, and "worse than the status quo" for resident 3 means below $-4$, not below $0$. On the river the matching ordinance is no pollution, which is the clean river, so there the status quo and no deal coincide; the difference between the two models is itself a finding. (ii) **A liability rule**: the principal may act on paying damages assessed by the council with the error the assessor's budget allows (state, row 12): efficient on average, someone below $0$ in most draws at any error, the assessor's compute raising the floor. Every arm reports, beside its gains over no deal, each resident's position against the named status quo at the same parameters; the essay's "worse for whom" is made resident by resident with that number, not through the ordering. The readers of the hard case found that under the liability rule the assessor's compute raises the worst-off party's position while under bargaining the principal's compute lowers it; that comparison is the human-legible claim and it is to be computed, not assumed.


## The three nulls for the next round

The existence test is passed on both members of the family (state, "Where the existence test stands"). The next round tests the content of the spine. Each theme is a null to be falsified on a stated arm, with the test written into the instance note. Briefs carry the null verbatim; one agent per null is briefed to argue it as hard as it can be argued, the optimist's role of earlier rounds.

1. **Compute asymmetry between two sides.** Null: a compute advantage for one side cannot move the division beyond what the protocol's seats already allow, so the finite-budget finding that the seat extracts and the budget does not (state, row 22) survives repeated play. Test: the residents' agents run a stated learning rule from a stated class (a mean-based no-regret learner, which rarely plays an action whose cumulative payoff lags the best action's by much, multiplicative weights being the standard instance; a no-swap-regret learner, which regrets no fixed remapping of its own actions; adaptive play with memory $m$, which best-responds to a sample of $m$ past plays); the principal's agent computes a best response to that rule over a stated horizon; the seat share is the share the protocol gives that seat in the limit with equal compute on both sides, a quarter under a proposer drawn by party, a half under one drawn by side, everything under take-it-or-leave-it, so the test runs under the proposer drawn by party, where the null can fail; the gap in compute is, for this null, the adaptation dial: the principal's planning horizon over the residents' stated learning rule and its knowledge of that rule's class; the classes are ordered by what they guarantee against a strategic opponent, a fixed threshold, then adaptive play with memory $m$, then a mean-based learner, then a no-swap-regret learner, each costlier to run than the last; the null is refuted if the principal's take exceeds its seat share and grows with the horizon, and narrowed if a stronger class caps it. Primaries: Braverman, Mao, Schneider and Weinberg 2018; Deng, Schneider and Sivan 2019; Mansour et al. 2022; Ben-Porath 1993; Press and Dyson 2012. The status-quo comparison is reported alongside.
2. **Multi-party adversarial play and the formation stage.** Null: after the principal announces its offers, the residents' agents can form a pact that resists divide and conquer, at a cost that does not grow with their number, with no standing organisation. A pact is a vector of minimum gains its signatories commit to refuse below; a winning coalition is any set of residents whose consent suffices under $k$; a pact fails when the principal can profitably buy a winning coalition out of it; a standing organisation is a body that holds its members' mandate before the dispute and can bind them without further messages; the cost in question is the message cost of forming the pact and the organisation's fixed cost. Test: a formation stage before the offers at $n=3$ and $n=30$, with a stated communication cost per message and private sensitivities, with and without a standing organisation that holds a mandate before the dispute; the principal's agent computes the order and schedule of its offers; the null is refuted if the pact holds at $n=3$ and fails at $n=30$ without the organisation, or if the per-resident message cost needed to hold it rises with $n$, and it holds with the organisation; the organisation's cost is reported against the gain it protects. Arms in the open system: the chain store (one principal against $M$ successive neighbourhoods with its record public and a stated prior on a tough type, a principal that never raises an offer after a refusal) and two neighbourhoods competing for one deal. Primaries: Segal 1999 and 2003; Genicot and Ray 2006; Winter 2004; Halac, Kremer and Winter 2020; Chipty and Snyder 1999; Stole and Zwiebel 1996. The pinned sentence this tests: you cannot join a club you do not yet know you need, and the party that knows first is the one that chose the dispute.
3. **Information and privacy.** Null: the agenda's outcome does not depend on what parties can observe or verify about one another beyond their own harms, and information available to everyone weakly improves the outcome under the criterion. Test: an information regime as a parameter of the instance (who can observe whose harm, whose offers, whose past dealings; what a court can verify), run on the pact arm (state, row 20) and on null 1's repeated two-side arm; the null is refuted if a regime in which a party's harm is observable or verifiable by the other side lowers the worst-off party's gain, or lowers that party's own gain below no deal, or if public information lowers the deal rate or the floor against private information; the hard case expects the low types to lose rents under verification, which is not a refutation. Primaries to be proposed by the theme's reader, with Hirshleifer 1971 and Milgrom and Stokey 1982 as the first two; this theme's ledger is due first because its regime is an input to the other two arms.


## Rules of use: mission and state

Two files, two jobs. This file is the **mission**: the question, the test of whether there is an essay, the dials, the measures, and these rules. It changes only when the question or the measures change, and only Dan changes it. `hyperscaling_coase_summary.md` is the **state**: the table of conditions against compute's effect, the open rows, the foil, the records. It changes every round, and one writer changes it.

1. **Read order, for every agent and every session.** This file in full; then the state table; then only the artefacts the task names. Agents do not edit either file.
2. **Every brief carries the question verbatim and names the rows it targets.** It asks the agent to end with two things, before any verdict on anyone's claims: its own answer to the question on the river's numbers, and a proposed delta to the table, rows changed, added or retired, each with a status and a source.
3. **Every artefact opens with a delta block:** `Delta to state, <date>: rows changed …; rows added …; rows retired …; mission touched: no | yes, because …`. An artefact with no delta is not progress and is not kept.
4. **One writer applies deltas.** The collating session applies them to the state table by named pathspec; a row's status rises only on a primary read or a re-run, never on a reviewer's say-so; Dan commits.
5. **Reports to Dan are written from the state, not from the artefact:** the one-sentence answer first, then the rows that moved, then where the existence test stands. Scaffolding is introduced in two sentences or not at all, by a low-context writer who has read only the mission and the state.
6. **Budget.** Three reviewers unless they fail to converge; one readability pass on agent-facing documents; no verification loop on anything that has not first stated its delta.
7. **The argument map** (`notebook/hyperscaling_coase.argdown`, under the argdown skill) is the third artefact when built: one node per row, the question as root, findings attached to nodes; it is read with the state, never instead of the mission.
8. **Arms are a closed list.** The instance note Dan signs enumerates every arm with its parameters, its distributions and the null it attacks. A row agent reports results only at those parameters; a wish for another parameter goes in a proposed-open-rows field, never in the delta. Only the model critic proposes arms, as open rows.
9. **Every arm reports the $n=3$ case** beside its own, so that no arm becomes a model of its own.
10. **The humanised layer is the admission test.** Three layers are kept: the state (every number), the plain summary (the arguments in words), and the humanised document (one running example, named actors, each finding as a story with a one-number grounding and a mapping back to the state). An arm earns its row if it changes a sentence in the humanised document; an arm that only adds a number is retired at collation.
Rule 2 amended: an agent's answer is given on the family's numbers, with the $n=3$ noise-schedule case alongside, no longer "on the river's numbers".
11. **Reports to Dan come in the humanised form first**, from a writer who has read only the plain summary, with the plain summary and the state behind it.
