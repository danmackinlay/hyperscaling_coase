# Optimist's advocate: the null, argued as hard as it goes

Read: mission in full; state table and open rows; the river section. Nothing else under `_plans/` or `_tmp/`. Primaries fetched this session are marked [sure]; [likely] is a statement I derived from a primary's model or took from a secondary quotation; [unsure] is memory or a snippet I could not open.

Criterion used: the Nash product of gains over no-deal across the four parties, applied ex post. On the river at $\pi=20$, $k=3$ the game in characteristic-function form is $v(N)=6$ and $v(S)=0$ for every other coalition, since all four are veto players; the Nash product, the Shapley value, the nucleolus and the Rawlsian floor on gains then all pick the same point, $1.5$ each: transfers $(3.5, 4.5, 10.5)$, the factory keeps $20-18.5=1.5$, residents' share $S=4.5/6=3/4$. The two-sides reading (factory $3$, residents $3$ split $1$ each) has Nash product $3$ against $1.5^4\approx5.06$ and a floor of $1$ against $1.5$, so the four-party criterion rejects it; that gap is the Sybil open row and I flag it rather than settle it. At $\pi=10$ the just outcome is the clean river, $0$ each.


## (a) Answer

The null survives on three rows, haggling with everything known (row 3), many residents under unanimity with binding declarations (row 8) and repeated play with patient parties (row 9), and probably on a fourth, one side adapts (row 11), under one condition that the null itself states and the table never writes down: that the agents are symmetric in the dial the row turns. On the river at $\pi=20$, $k=3$, symmetric capable agents reach $1.5$ each by theorem and with no added rule: adaptive play with equal information selects the Nash bargaining split (Young 1993, Sáez-Martí and Weibull 1999) and, under logit noise, the core point that minimises the richest player's take (Sawa 2019), which on this river is $1.5$ each; sequential short-lived commitment attempts under unanimity give a unique, efficient, immediate agreement whose patient-limit split is proportional to the probability of committing first, $1.5$ each when reliability is equal (Hang and Wen 2026); patient repeated play with cheap commitment has symmetric Markov equilibria in which both commit to the fair demand in almost every period (Pei 2025). The same theorems say what happens when the dial is unequal: the bigger sampler takes $s_i/\sum s$ [likely]; the more reliable committer moves from $1.5$ to at most $2.81$ of $6$ in the sequential protocol and wins the war of attrition in expectation under durable commitments (Ellingsen and Miettinen 2014); the slower adapter beats the faster one (Hilbe, Nowak and Sigmund 2013). The null fails outright on sealed unconditional commitment (row 5, Ellingsen and Miettinen 2008), on $k=1$ at $\pi=10$ (row 10, the core is empty and the program-equilibrium pact is not coalition-proof), and on every row where one side holds a dial the other lacks (rows 1, 2, 6 under durable commitments). So the essay exists, but its spine is one condition rather than a list of rules: the agenda assumes the dials are symmetric across parties, and nothing in "give everyone an agent" delivers that. "Better for whom": under symmetry, for everyone equally; under asymmetry in any dial, for the party with more of that dial, with the single exception of adaptation, where more is worse.

## (c) Working, row by row

### Rows 1 and 2: take-it-or-leave-it by one side

Premise rejected: "who can commit: factory only" (and its mirror). The null gives both sides an agent that can post a bond, and an ultimatum is not available against a party that can pre-commit to refuse. That is Schelling's point and the design of Chlaß and Miettinen 2026 [sure, abstract]: a sequential ultimatum game with pre-commitment; responders who can unilaterally pre-commit face the proposer's incentives yet commit less aggressively than proposers propose; in a simultaneous-commitment variant proposers commit even when it cannot help; the payoff differences do not match the commitment theory; in late rounds pre-commitment yields almost 100% efficiency while the plain ultimatum game stays inefficient. The result is experimental, with human subjects, and the authors attribute the gap to reciprocity and equality of opportunity. It attacks the rows' efficiency claim (deal rate rising with the committer's estimation) by changing the game: with two-sided commitment the deal closes without the estimation dial doing any work.

What the rows become: unchanged in content, with the status note that they assume one-sided commitment and so are not tests of the null. Conditions: both sides able to bond; the river supplies no rule against that. Concession: the null says nothing about why the residents would in fact have a bonding agent when the factory has a legal department; the essay must argue that asymmetry is the default, and if it can, these rows stand as the essay's.

### Row 3: haggling, all known

Results. Nash 1953 [sure]: in the demand game, smoothing the feasible set by a small uncertainty and letting it vanish selects the Nash bargaining solution out of the continuum. Young 1993 and Sáez-Martí and Weibull 1999 [sure, abstract]: in adaptive play, two populations sampling recent history and best-replying with rare errors, the Nash split is the unique long-run outcome when all individuals are equally informed about past play, and "cleverness", best-replying to the opponent's best reply, "does have an advantage in some cases" but does not change that outcome under equal information. Sawa 2019 [sure, abstract]: in $n$-person coalitional bargaining with logit noise, stochastically stable allocations are the core allocations that minimise the wealth of the richest player; on the river the core is the whole simplex of divisions of $6$, so the selected point is $1.5$ each. Agastya 1997 [sure, abstract]: self-perpetuating allocations in a multiplayer demand game are core allocations, and under a sufficient condition the society learns a core allocation from any history. Agastya 1999 [likely, snippet]: under uniform errors the stochastically stable allocations are a subset of the core and need not be unique with four or more players. Young's unequal-sample result [likely]: in his model the resistance to pushing a population down is proportional to its own sample size, so the stable split is $s_1/(s_1+s_2)$ to population 1, and the bigger sampler takes more; Sawa 2021's text confirms that unequal samples give an asymmetric Nash solution but I could not read the direction in Young's own words.

Attack. The row's equity cell, "the better agent takes more, unnoticed", conflates two dials. Reasoning depth does not tilt the split under equal information; sample size, the information or memory dial, does. The null, "everyone has a moderately capable agent", is the equal-information case, where three independent dynamics select $1.5$ each with no rule supplied. Project Deal is one empirical study of unequal LLM agents and does not say which dial differed.

What the row becomes: status "theorem under equal information; the tilt is a theorem for the sample dial and not for the reasoning dial"; equity "$1.5$ each under equal information; $s_i$-weighted under unequal samples". Conditions and the river: every one of these results needs recurrent play with bounded memory; the river is one-shot by construction, but the mission makes repetition non-negotiable, and nothing here is a rule anyone must supply. Concession: the selection depends on the noise model (Naidu, Hwang and Bowles 2010 [sure, read]: uniform noise selects Kalai–Smorodinsky in contract games and intentional noise selects Nash; in the Nash demand game both select Nash), and under uniform noise Agastya's multiplicity at $n\ge4$ may leave a symmetric set of asymmetric allocations rather than the single point, which is just ex ante and not ex post [unsure]. The river's symmetry (equal gains over no-deal for all four, since transfers compensate the harms exactly) is what makes every symmetric selection land on $1.5$ each; that symmetry is a feature of the model, not of rivers.

### Row 4: haggling, private harms, fast offers

Gul, Sonnenschein and Wilson 1986 [sure]: an uninformed party making all the offers to an informed one, in the gap case and in stationary equilibrium, loses its power as the interval between offers shrinks; the price goes to the informed side's reservation and trade is immediate. The row already records the residents keeping the information rents. For the null it is not enough: the outcome is a corner, the factory's gain near $0$, floor $0$, Nash product near $0$; the just split needs the factory at $1.5$. And on the river with three sellers whose consents are complements the bilateral theorem does not apply as stated; the essay's own citations (Rob 1989; Mailath and Postlewaite 1990) say the efficient action is reached with a probability that falls toward zero as $n$ grows, which at $n=3$ is a positive number nobody has computed. Concession: the row stands as "applied, not run". One thing for the record against the incumbent reading: here the speed dial hurts the side that holds the offerer's seat, which is the single principal, so this row cuts against "compute favours whoever already holds a position" and for "compute does not help uniformly".

### Row 5: both commit, sealed, unconditional, once

Ellingsen and Miettinen 2008 [sure, abstract]: binding commitments at a low positive cost $c$; among outcomes surviving iterated strict dominance, with commitment that never fails there are three, two giving all the surplus to one player and one with a high probability of conflict; with success probability $q$ the conflict case is as the row says. Nash's smoothing selects the Nash split in the same demand game only at $c=0$ exactly and without the option to stay flexible; the E–M result is the $c\to0^+$ limit, and any bond an agent posts costs something, so theirs is the right perturbation. The only optimist's escape is that capable agents would not choose this protocol when conditional (row 7) or sequential short-lived (row 6a) commitment is available; but choosing the protocol is a pre-game negotiation, which is exactly the essay's claim. Concede. Note that Pei 2025 (row 9) reverses the result in repeated play, so the row's "once" is load-bearing.

### Row 6: both commit, publication in sequence

Hang and Wen 2026 [sure, read in full]: $n$ players, unanimity, each period a commitment stage in which players attempt short-lived commitments one at a time in a random order, each succeeding with probability $\rho_i\in(0,1)$, followed by a random-proposer bargaining stage. Proposition 1: a unique stationary Markov-perfect equilibrium, efficient, with immediate agreement, identical to a random-proposer game without commitment (Eraslan and McLennan 2013) in which player $i$'s recognition weight is the probability of being the first to commit successfully plus the probability of proposing after every attempt fails. In the patient limit the realised split goes to those weights. On the river (four veto players, pie $6$, random order, equal proposer probabilities; script `hang_wen_river.py` beside this file): symmetric reliability, at $0.5$ or at $0.9$, gives $1.5$ each; factory at $0.9$ against residents at $0.5$ gives $2.55$ to the factory and $1.15$ to each resident (residents' share $0.575$); factory at $0.99$ gives $2.79$ and $1.07$; the ceiling as the factory's reliability goes to $1$ is $0.469\times6=2.81$, since a resident is first to attempt three times in four. So "first and cheapest committer takes the pie" is the one-round or impatient limit, and in this protocol the reliability dial moves the factory from $1.5$ to at most $2.81$ of $6$.

Ellingsen and Miettinen 2014 [sure, abstract]: with durable commitments that decay stochastically, the unique Markov-perfect outcome is a war of attrition, incompatible commitments first and agreement when one decays; faster decay makes the terms more equal; the more patient, more committed and less risk-averse side gets more in expectation.

Premise rejected: that a published commitment lasts. If it expires with the round, the row's split vanishes under symmetry; if it persists, the row stands with delay and an expected tilt to the patient and reliable side, and under symmetric agents it is ex ante equal and ex post one-sided. "One claimant beats three" holds only if the three cannot each commit; under Hang–Wen each resident has a one-in-four chance to be first. What the row becomes: 6a and 6b as in the delta, and a new fact in the open row "the three facts that pick the row": durability. Concession: durable bonds are the kind a smart contract posts, so 6b is the more natural reading for deployed agents, and under 6b with any asymmetry in patience or reliability the row is the essay's.

### Row 7: both commit, instructions read each other

Tennenholtz 2004 [sure] and Kalai, Kalai, Lehrer and Samet 2010 [sure, abstract]: every feasible, individually rational payoff of a two-person game is an equilibrium of one universal voluntary commitment game with simple devices. Krier [sure, read]: agents "condition their actions on shared signals and contracts", reaching "a correlated equilibrium", enforced by "automated escrows and reputation systems" and "near-instantaneous computational verification". The row says "a focal rule decides". Rejected word: rule. A focal point is what the parties compute, not what someone supplies, and on this river the computation is overdetermined: the row-3 dynamics and every symmetric cooperative solution land on $1.5$ each because the game is the four-player unanimity game. What the row becomes: status "theorem for the set; selection to $1.5$ each by symmetric dynamics [likely]"; equity "$1.5$ each under symmetric agents; verification capacity enlarges the set and does not move the point". Conditions: mutual readability, which the open row "the three facts" already carries; no rule. Concession: the selection results are about recurrent play and program equilibrium is one-shot; the bridge, adaptive play over programs, is mine and unchecked [unsure]. And the four-parties-versus-two-sides ambiguity ($1.5$ each against $3,1,1,1$) is real; the Sybil row owns it.

### Row 8: many residents under unanimity, binding declarations

Miettinen and Vanberg 2025 [sure, abstract]: distributive multilateral bargaining; before each round players declare a minimum share, binding with an exogenous probability; stationary subgame-perfect equilibria under every $q$-majority rule; unanimity uniquely inefficient, every other rule including all-but-one fully efficient. Their working-paper version says efficient equilibria exist under unanimity "only if the probability that commitments bind" meets a condition I could not read [unsure]. Hang and Wen 2026 [sure, read], above: with sequential rather than simultaneous attempts, unanimity is efficient with immediate agreement. So the row's inefficiency is specific to simultaneous declarations. What the row becomes: "simultaneous: inefficient delay; sequential: efficient and immediate, split as 6a, $1.5$ each when symmetric". Conditions: stationarity, patience, imperfect commitment ($\rho<1$); the river adds no rule, and the sequencing is a fact about deployment, not a rule. This is the cleanest row on which the null survives: symmetric agents, no rule, a unique equilibrium at the just split. Concession: with asymmetric reliability the same equation tilts, bounded at $2.81$ of $6$ for one party against three.

### Row 9: repeated play, patient parties

The folk theorem gives the set [sure]. Selection: Pei 2025, with a 2026 revision [sure, abstract and introduction]: two players, each period a Nash demand game in which each chooses to stay flexible or commit at a cost; incompatible commitments destroy the period's surplus; if both are flexible the status quo, the last no-conflict division, rules. With patient players and small commitment cost there are symmetric Markov-perfect equilibria, asymptotically efficient and renegotiation-proof, in which both commit to the fair demand (one half) in almost all periods; the probability of demanding the fair share goes to one as patience goes to one. These are Markov, so "history decides" is false in them beyond the status quo, and the status quo converges to the fair split. Young 1993 [sure]: the long-run distribution concentrates on the Nash split from any initial history. Ellison 1993 [sure]: the expected time to leave a convention is exponential in one over the error rate, so "better agents lock in the first convention" is a medium-run statement at tiny experimentation rates; an agent that explores deliberately is not locked.

What the row becomes: status as in the delta; equity "symmetric patient agents reach $1.5$ each in the long run; samples tilt it". Conditions and the river: Pei's result is two-player and uses strictly concave stage utility, which makes the fair split the unique efficient one; whether the selection of one half survives linear utility and four parties is unverified [unsure]. Young needs recurrence and incomplete sampling ($s/m\le1/2$). No rule. Concession: the tilt with sample size is a theorem [likely], and more sampling also slows transitions, so the better-informed agent both takes more and holds it longer; that is the essay's row 9 under asymmetry, by the same theorem that gives the null its symmetric case.

### Row 10: divide and conquer, $k=1$, $\pi=10$

The core is empty [sure, the essay's computation: $8+7+1=16$ from a pot of $0$]. The optimist's move: residents' agents submit programs "refuse iff both other residents run this program, else sign at any price". A unilateral deviation by resident 1 to any other program makes residents 2 and 3 sign at $\varepsilon$; the factory pollutes on their consent and resident 1 ends at $-2$ or $\varepsilon-2$, below the pact's $0$. The factory's unilateral deviation meets three refusals and leaves it at $0$. So the pact is a program equilibrium with no imposed rule [reviewer-derived, unchecked]. It is not coalition-proof: the factory committing to pay $9$ to resident 1 alone and ignore cheaper offers gives $(1, 7)$ against $(0, 0)$, so the core deficit returns through a two-party commitment; and that exclusive partnership is itself contestable by residents 2 and 3 underbidding for it, which unravels to the race. Two program equilibria, pact and race, and the selection is open. Concession: the cooperative claim stands and the null does not survive here; "must be imposed" overstates and should read "is a program equilibrium that a factory-plus-one coalition can break".

### Row 11: one side adapts, the other is fixed

Press and Dyson [sure]: an extortioner against an adapting opponent takes the larger share. Hilbe, Nowak and Sigmund 2013 [sure, abstract]: in reasonably large populations extortioners catalyse cooperation and are not the stable outcome; they hold their ground only in very small populations; in co-evolutionary arms races between two distinct populations extortion benefits the population that evolves at the slower rate, the Red King effect. Stewart and Plotkin 2013 [sure, read]: generous zero-determinant strategies dominate in evolving populations and extortion succeeds only when the population is very small.

Premise rejected: "the other is fixed". Under symmetric adaptation the outcome is the cooperative payoff, the deal at the symmetric split [likely, two-player populations mapped to the river by analogy]. Under asymmetric adaptation rates the slower side wins, which means the adaptation dial is the one dial where more compute hurts its holder. What the row becomes: as in the delta. Concession: the Red King result is a point for the essay's alternative hypothesis as the mission states it ("can already stay rigid while others adapt"), even though it is against the reading "more compute favours its holder".

### Row 12: liability rule in statute

Not a test of the null; by construction. No change.

### The invariance result

Coase's invariance [sure]: with zero transaction costs the efficient action obtains under either entitlement; it says nothing about the split and is Pareto-only. It supports the table's efficiency column and nothing in the equity column, which is where the whole question lives. No row moves.
