## Miettinen and Vanberg 2024/2025, "Commitment and Conflict in Unanimity Bargaining", AWI DP 749 (June 2024) = AEJ Micro 17(1). [sure; FULL TEXT read, Heidelberg open copy, saved as `primaries/miettinen_vanberg_2024.md`]

Model (§1): n ≥ 3 symmetric players divide a pie of 1, discrete time, infinite horizon, common discount factor δ. Each period: commitment stage, then Baron–Ferejohn bargaining stage. Commitment attempt x_i ∈ [0,1] is short-lived (lasts at most the current period, re-established each period; footnote 11: this differs from Ellingsen–Miettinen 2014's persistent commitments and is "necessary to make the model tractable for larger n"). Each attempt fails independently with probability 1−ρ ("loophole"); status common knowledge at the bargaining stage. Proposer drawn with probability 1/n; proposes d with Σd_i ≤ 1; a committed player with s_i > d_i rejects automatically; deal implemented if at least q players (proposer included) accept; otherwise next period. Equilibrium concept: stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium (SSPE), no weakly dominated voting.

Results:
- Theorem 1 (§3.1): under any q-majority rule with q < n, an essentially unique SSPE: nobody commits above their continuation value, agreement is immediate, ex ante payoffs 1/n each. Ex post it is Baron–Ferejohn: each included responder is paid δ/n, excluded responders get 0, the proposer keeps the rest. Proposition 1 (n=3, majority): proposer 1−δ/3, one responder δ/3, the other responder 0; "when the discount factor approaches zero ... the proposer receiving the entire pie". Mechanism: "Bertrand-like competition in commitments": an aggressive committer is left out of the winning coalition.
- Theorem 2 (§3.3): under unanimity an inefficient symmetric SSPE always exists: all players attempt the most aggressive commitment (agreement only if n−1 attempts fail); delay increases in ρ and in n; "the final outcome can be more unequal than under majority rule"; as δ → 0 "a single player secures the entire pie". Proposition 2 (n=3): agreement probability per round ξ = (1−ρ)²(1+2ρ); payoff v* = (1/3)·ξ/(1−δ(1−ξ)) < 1/3.
- Theorem 3 (§3.4): under unanimity ALL SSPE are inefficient iff ρ < ((n−1)/(2n−1))^{1/(n−1)}. For n=3 the threshold is √(2/5) ≈ 0.632 (Proposition 3: an efficient symmetric equilibrium, everyone committing to 1/3, exists iff ρ ≥ √(2/5)); for n=4 it is (3/7)^{1/3} ≈ 0.754; as n grows the threshold rises toward 1, so "any efficient equilibrium will eventually be destabilized"; n̂ < 10 already at ρ = 0.9. Footnote 18: the existence condition does not depend on δ.
- Proposition 4 (§3.5): as the period length t → 0 (frequent negotiations), both the efficient and the most aggressive equilibrium exist under unanimity; the aggressive one's payoff tends to 0 (expected waiting time → ∞) and its commitments tend to 1.
- Conclusion: "any less-than-unanimity rule circumvents this problem in the context of our model"; "even a move to an all-but-one rule ... may help dramatically"; caveat: the model assumes delay is the only inefficiency and "could also increase the risk of decisions that impose external costs on non-consenting members, an aspect that is absent from our model" (Buchanan–Tullock decision versus external costs).

Consequences for the state table:
- "Unanimity is uniquely inefficient" means: unanimity is the only rule under which an inefficient equilibrium exists; it is NOT that every unanimity equilibrium is inefficient. With three claimants an efficient equilibrium coexists whenever commitments stick with probability at least 0.632; it is the unique kind of equilibrium only below that. The dial here is commitment reliability ρ (the mission's commitment dial, probability q), and the sign is the opposite of the optimist's: more reliable commitment makes inefficiency more likely (delay increases in ρ; efficient equilibria vanish for ρ below a threshold that rises with n, so for a fixed ρ more claimants destroys efficiency).
- The model's players are symmetric claimants of a dollar; the river's factory is not one of them. Applying it to the river needs either the residents alone dividing their share (n=3) or the four parties as symmetric veto players (n=4 at π=20, pie 6, threshold 0.754). In neither reading is there a party who pays; so the theorem is about who among the residents gets what, as the table says.
- Under q < n the efficient outcome is ex post unequal (an excluded responder gets 0). On the river at k=2 the excluded resident is worse than 0: harmed and unpaid, −h_i. The "efficient under every other rule" half of the row carries that cost, which the river already knows (the pivotal-fraction point) and the paper names as its caveat.
- The commitments are one-period; a standing instruction is not this object. Ellingsen–Miettinen 2014 (persistent commitments, two players) is the nearest model for durable published instructions.

## Young 1993, "An Evolutionary Model of Bargaining", JET 59(1) 145–168; SFI WP 92-02-009 read. [sure; FULL TEXT of the working paper read, saved as `primaries/young_1993_sfi.md`]

Model: two classes of agents A and B (two-player bargaining, one from each class per period), Nash demand game over a pie on a grid of precision δ; each agent samples k of the last m plays (k ≤ m/2) and best-replies; mistakes with probability ε.
- Theorem 1: with ε = 0 and k ≤ m/2 the process converges with probability one to a convention (a fixed division).
- Theorem 2: if all agents in A have utility u and all in B have utility v, the stochastically stable conventions are the discrete Nash bargaining solution(s) (maximisers of u(a)v(b)), and for all sufficiently large k and m the only ones.
- Theorem 3: with heterogeneous classes, the stable convention maximises the lower envelope R*(a) over all pairs (u,v) represented; "the long-run behavior of the process is determined by the person who is most risk averse". With mixing between classes the stable division is fifty-fifty.
- §5: perturbations select among conventions; the process sits at a convention most of the time and leaves it only after "a large accumulation of mistakes", so selection is a long-run statement (the time to leave a convention grows as ε → 0).

Consequences: the result is two-class; with linear utility and symmetric roles it selects the equal split between the two sides. On the river with the residents as one class it gives 3–3 (residents' share 1/2), and with four symmetric veto players the natural extension is 1.5 each, but that extension is not in the paper. The "for whom" content: a class's share is set by its most risk-averse member (Theorem 3), which on the river is a statement about the residents' side being set by its weakest bargainer. Selection needs repeated play across a population with rare mistakes, and history holds until mistakes accumulate.

## Kalai, Kalai, Lehrer and Samet 2010, "A Commitment Folk Theorem", GEB 69(1) 127–137. [sure; FULL TEXT read, Microsoft Research open copy]

Theorem 1: for any finite TWO-PERSON game there is a complete voluntary space of commitment devices: every individually-rational correlated-strategy payoff of G is obtained at some Nash equilibrium of the commitment game. "Voluntary" means each player keeps the option of playing without commitment. The authors say: "the commitment devices we use are to a large extent mathematical constructs"; "This paper is restricted to the simple setting of two-person complete information ... Initial attempts to deal with such extensions required us to choose among different appealing modeling alternatives that lead to drastically different conclusions." On Tennenholtz 2004: "a partial folk theorem using program equilibria: the program equilibrium payoffs of a game G consist of all the individually-rational payoff pairs that can be obtained through independent (not correlated) mixed strategies of G."

Consequences: the state table's "every individually rational split is an equilibrium" under instructions that read each other is a two-person theorem; the river has four parties. The two-sided version (factory versus residents as a bloc) is covered; the four-party river is an extension the authors call unsettled. The selection problem is the whole residue, as last round said.

## Tennenholtz 2004, "Program Equilibrium", GEB 49(2) 363–373. [sure the theorem exists, via Kalai et al. 2010 §5.3 and Oesterheld's annotated bibliography; the text itself was not reached: ScienceDirect only]

Folk theorem: the pure program-equilibrium payoffs are the individually rational payoffs obtainable by independent mixed strategies, by programs of the form "if the opponent's program equals mine, play the agreed action, else punish" (syntactic comparison). Two-player as stated.

## Gul, Sonnenschein and Wilson 1986, "Foundations of Dynamic Monopoly and the Coase Conjecture", JET 39, 155–190. [sure; abstract, introduction and §5 argument read from the Princeton open copy]

Abstract: seller quotes a price each period; zero costs, positive interest rate, continuum of buyers. If buyers' valuations are bounded away from zero ("gap") the equilibrium is unique, buyers' strategies stationary. "Coase's conjecture is verified for stationary strategies: reducing the period length drives all prices to zero or the least valuation." Introduction: "without repeat purchases monopoly rents must depend substantially on a monopolist's ability to commit to prices or quantities offered in the future"; the paper also extends Rubinstein's bilateral bargaining to a seller making repeated offers.

Consequences: the uninformed offerer who cannot commit loses its rents as offers become frequent; the informed side keeps them. On the river the factory buying consent from a resident with private harm is the buyer-side mirror: the factory's offers climb toward the top of the harm distribution and the resident keeps the information rent. That is what the table's row says, and it is still a two-party, one-offerer theorem applied by analogy, not run on three residents under unanimity.
