  _Editor’s note:_ This essay was written in reaction to the excellent piece,
  **“Coasean Bargaining at Scale - Decentralization, coordination, and
  co-existence with AGI,” Cosmos Institute, Sep 26, 2025**, a long read by **Seb
  Krier** (writing in a personal capacity as he leads the Frontier Policy
  Development team at Google DeepMind). Our intent is to elevate the debate and
  complement Seb’s work, not to diminish it.


  **The seduction, and trap, of “just price it”**


  There’s an undeniable elegance to the Coasean promise. If people can cheaply
  find one another, state what they want, and bind themselves to what they
  promise, conflict dissolves into trade. The neighbor buys quiet from the
  guitarist; the fleet pays to enter a sensitive street; the developer
  compensates those who value the skyline as it is (Coase 1960). In this
  telling, what we’ve called “policy” for a century is largely a kludge for high
  transaction costs, Greenwald and Stiglitz wrote as much when they formalized
  how pervasive information problems gum up markets (Greenwald & Stiglitz 1986).
  And long before them, Hayek warned that planners cannot access the “knowledge
  of time and place” that lives in the fingertips of participants (Hayek 1945).
  Ostrom later showed that, when the stakes are local and the rules fit the
  culture, communities can govern common pools just fine without a commissar
  (Ostrom 1990).


  The essay we’re responding to pushes this idea to its ambitious edge: give
  everyone a tireless advocate, an AI agent that can learn preferences, find
  counterparties, clear deals, and verify compliance, and the old justification
  for top-down control starts to wobble. If we can obliterate discovery,
  negotiation, and enforcement frictions, we can move governance from statute to
  thermostat. Add enough agents and we might even exit our Nash-style stalemates
  into something like correlated coordination, shared signals, fewer dumb
  deadlocks (cf. Aumann 1974). Recent work already sketches the “virtual agent
  economy”, a layered ecology of specialized agents that transact faster than
  any city council can deliberate (Tomašev et al. 2025). It is a bracing vision,
  one that deserves stress-tests before we let it rewrite our civic playbook.


  Pricing is a marvelous solvent for many conflicts, but it is not a universal
  acid. Some goods resist monetization without doing violence to what they are
  (Walzer 1983). Some harms cannot be verified by a meter without turning our
  neighborhoods into sensor farms (Scott 1998). Some bargains are “efficient”
  only because wealth, liquidity, and endowments tilt the playing field (the
  Coase-invariance critique; see also Kahneman–Knetsch–Thaler 1990 on endowment
  effects). And some domains are not “priceable externalities” but tail risks
  where the only sane equilibrium is prohibition and state capacity (Acemoglu
  2003; Ord 2020).


  So our wager is simple, and stricter. Agents can and should shrink the Coase
  gap. But to avoid reinventing the central planner in platform clothing, or
  drifting into a market of micro-extortions, we need a constitutional layer
  that says, clearly: **price what you can; protect what you must**. Rights and
  red-lines first; continuous clearing underneath.


  This isn’t an argument against markets. Quite the opposite. We want _more_
  markets where they actually belong, and _fewer_ pseudo-markets where consent
  is coerced by circumstances. The right mental move is subtraction: strip away
  the work that statutes do today only because search is expensive and
  enforcement is clumsy. Agents are good at that. They can surface tacit
  preferences (“I’ll accept loud music on Saturdays, not Sundays”), stand up
  bargaining clubs that elicit honest minima (mechanism design 101), and embed
  escrow so that promises keep themselves. They can even make Kirzner’s
  entrepreneur, the alert discoverer of overlooked gains, native to every
  household (Kirzner 1973). This is the part of the vision we endorse.


  But pricing without guardrails quickly curdles. Harberger-style option taxes
  can discipline sky-high “buy-me-off” claims (Harberger 1962; Posner & Weyl
  2017), yet without strong identity and anti-Sybil defenses, a neighborhood of
  a thousand can turn into a botnet of ten thousand, and “voluntary bargains”
  become organized griefing. Without interoperability and portability, the agent
  runtime becomes a de facto planner with different branding. And without a
  minimally capable state, audits, cyber forensics, online dispute resolution,
  the whole promise slides back into the courts, i.e., back into slowness.


  The right question, then, is not “Can agents replace regulation?” It’s “Which
  pieces of regulation are merely crutches for high transaction costs, and which
  are bones?” Noise caps during newborn naps: probably a crutch. Disability
  dignity, historic heritage, child health exposures: bones. Diesel routing
  around asthma clusters: a market with a pulse, if we can verify routes and
  emissions without strip-mining privacy. Bio-procurement for would-be
  pathogens: not a market.


  Seen this way, the agent layer is governance infrastructure, not a libertarian
  fantasy. It helps us relocate coordination to the lowest effective level,
  households, blocks, fleets, microgrids, while keeping a constitutional layer
  intact. It re-reads James C. Scott with a friendlier ending: legibility
  doesn’t have to be tyranny if the maps are drawn by those who live on the land
  (Scott 1998). And it faces Acemoglu’s “political Coase theorem” head-on:
  agents can raise the cost of expropriation through transparency and audit
  trails, but they can’t bind the sovereign, only institutions can (Acemoglu
  2003).


  What follows is our blueprint for that two-layer world. We’ll start by drawing
  a hard border between what pricing can fix and what only rights can protect.
  Then we’ll specify the minimal governance stack any serious agentic society
  needs: identity without surveillance, contracts with verifiable outcomes,
  privacy-preserving oracles, tiered dispute resolution, interoperability that
  prevents platform capture, and safety rails for catastrophic domains. Finally,
  we’ll propose real pilots, no metaphors, just metrics, to prove where
  bargaining shines and where it fails. If Coase at scale is to be more than
  wishful thinking, it has to survive contact with the street, the curb, the
  meter, and the court.


  **What agents actually fix, and what they don’t**


  Agents are very good at three kinds of work that humans and legacy
  institutions do badly at scale: discovery, commitment, and verification.
  Discovery is the tedious hunt for who is affected, what they want, and when a
  deal would actually be pareto-improving. Commitment is the choreography of
  offers, counters, time limits, escrows, and contingencies that make a bargain
  real rather than aspirational. Verification is the dull, relentless checking
  that what was promised actually happened. When the original Coase sketched his
  thought experiment, he abstracted these frictions away to make a point about
  the structure of rights (Coase 1960). In the real world those frictions are
  the whole story. Software narrows all three. An agent can listen across your
  calendars, geolocation, household constraints, and medical flags, infer your
  reservation values with tolerable error, and broadcast them to exactly the
  counterparties who matter. It can stage a thousand sealed-bid micro-auctions
  in the background and sweep the winners into standardized contracts with
  built-in escrows. It can verify delivery routes from tamper-evident logs or
  zero-knowledge proofs rather than from late-night phone calls and angry
  neighborhood forums. None of this is magical; it is just the deployment of
  cheap cognition against expensive coordination.


  That strength extends to problems that look like policy but are actually
  scheduling. A curb is simultaneously parking, pickup, loading, and bus
  priority. A council resolution can only choose one modality at a time; an
  agentic market can treat the curb as a time-sliced asset and clear it minute
  by minute. The same is true of household electricity. Fixed tariffs are crude
  compromises between volatility and fairness. Agents can express time-of-use
  willingness to pay, match it against localized supply, and produce a pattern
  of small, reversible trades that move load without the whiff of compulsion. In
  both cases, what is changing is not the moral theory but the gradient of
  information: Hayek’s “knowledge of time and place” becomes machine-readable
  and cheaply exchanged (Hayek 1945).


  Agents also raise the ceiling on what Ostrom-style local governance can do.
  Her communities worked because they had clear boundaries, cheap monitoring,
  and low-cost conflict resolution (Ostrom 1990). Those conditions rarely hold
  in cities or online spaces. Agents make boundaries computable (residency
  attestations), monitoring lightweight (privacy-preserving sensors with
  proofs), and conflict resolution routinized (tiered online dispute resolution
  with fast escalation). What looked like a tragic commons under
  paper-and-shoe-leather conditions starts to look governable once the costs of
  seeing and settling fall. If Greenwald and Stiglitz are right that market
  failures are pervasive because information is imperfect and markets
  incomplete, then compressing those imperfections, even a little, should move
  the frontier (Greenwald & Stiglitz 1986).


  But the solvent power of pricing has edges, and agents do not dissolve those
  edges. Some goods are not merely scarce; they are protected by reasons that
  are not well expressed as bids. We do not ask a family to name the price at
  which they will allow lead exposure for their child, or a community to
  monetize the graves in its cemetery. Walzer called these “blocked exchanges,”
  domains in which buying and selling corrupt the meaning of the thing itself
  (Walzer 1983). Agents can map preferences with exquisite resolution; they
  cannot convert certain claims of dignity into commensurable prices without
  loss. Even if they could, the social legitimacy of those outcomes would be
  poor. Habermas’s point about public reason applies: some decisions demand
  justification that others can recognize as fair, not merely a clearing price
  that one can afford (Habermas 1996).


  Even in domains that look priceable, wealth and endowments lean on the scale.
  Coase’s invariance result collapses once we reintroduce liquidity constraints
  and reference dependence. The party with cash in hand can buy more peace than
  the party with preference intensity, and the endowment effect makes
  willingness to accept much larger than willingness to pay (Kahneman, Knetsch &
  Thaler 1990). Harberger-style option taxes blunt ransom dynamics by forcing
  claimants to carry the cost of their stated valuations (Harberger 1962; Posner
  & Weyl 2017). Still, those mechanisms presuppose reliable identity, low
  collusion, and honest oracles. Without proof-of-personhood and anti-Sybil
  defenses, a neighborhood of one thousand can flower into a swarm of ten
  thousand sock-puppet agents. Without anti-collusion tools, a bargaining club
  can become a cartel with better UX.


  Measurement itself is a political act, and agents lean heavily on measurement.
  To verify that a truck respected the premium around an asthma cluster, you
  need route telemetry and emissions data. To enforce a noise contract, you need
  acoustic sensing and tamper-resistant logs. As James C. Scott argued, making
  the world legible invites simplifications that miss the thing people actually
  care about (Scott 1998). Add Goodhart’s Law and you get the familiar failure
  mode: once a metric becomes a target, it ceases to be a good metric (Strathern
  1997). Agents can buffer this with richer signals and with privacy
  technologies, differential privacy for aggregates, zero-knowledge proofs that
  “I complied” without exposing the raw trace (Dwork 2006; Ben-Sasson et al.
  2014). Still, there is a trade: the more we can enforce, the more we must
  sense; the more we sense, the more we must govern how models remember.


  There is also the problem of adversaries. Agents won’t just bargain; they will
  scheme. They will bluff on your behalf, drip micro-nuisances to extract rents,
  poison rivals’ reputations, and assemble flash coalitions to grief an
  unpopular but beneficial project. A naïve market of micro-payments can become
  a racketeering layer where the equilibrium is “pay me or I make your life
  slightly worse.” The countermeasures are conceptual as much as technical:
  anti-extortion norms encoded as bright-line prohibitions, minimum stakes and
  cooling-off periods to add friction where mischief thrives, and reputational
  escrows that decay for accounts exhibiting “pattern-of-nuisance” signatures.
  Those design choices are political; they must be debated as such.


  Finally, there is the boundary case the original essay acknowledges but moves
  past quickly: catastrophic risk. A world of competent agents increases the
  surface area for correlated mistakes. Routing agents can synchronize on the
  same fragile detour. Procurement agents can collectively lower the guardrails
  on sensitive biology because each one is “only” shaving a requirement. These
  are not externalities to be priced; they are domains for prohibition,
  auditing, and state capacity. Acemoglu’s “political Coase theorem” remains the
  hard stop: you cannot contract around a sovereign that changes the rules
  tomorrow, and you cannot bargain with a party intent on non-negotiable harm
  (Acemoglu 2003; Ord 2020). The answer here is not a better clearing engine but
  better institutions, and the willingness to say “no trade.”


  What follows from this is modest but clarifying. Use agents to move
  coordination to the lowest effective level whenever the goods at stake can be
  priced without degrading their meaning, and whenever measurement can be done
  without strip-mining privacy. Keep a constitutional layer for the rest. The
  test is not merely whether a market clears but whether citizens would
  recognize the outcome as legitimate if it were explained in public, with
  reasons as well as numbers. In the next section we spell out that
  constitutional layer, the baseline rights and red-lines that markets must sit
  within, and the minimal governance stack required to keep agents from becoming
  planners with better branding.


  **Rights before prices: the constitutional layer**


  Before agents can bargain, someone has to decide what is up for sale. That
  decision is not an implementation detail; it is the normative spine of the
  system. Political communities do this all the time, usually implicitly. We
  protect some interests as rights, we treat others as commodities, and we mark
  a third class as inalienable, off the market altogether. Calabresi and Melamed
  gave this structure a memorable vocabulary: property rules, liability rules,
  and inalienability (Calabresi & Melamed 1972). A Coasean world with agents
  does not erase that trichotomy; it makes it unavoidable and visible. If we do
  not draw the line, platforms will draw it for us in their defaults.


  The first claim is straightforward. Some domains belong under property or
  liability rules precisely because agents can now do them justice. If a truck’s
  passage imposes a small, measurable harm on a block, we can let the block set
  a price and let the fleet decide whether to pay or reroute, with escrow and
  telemetry to keep both honest. If curb space is scarce, we can time-slice it
  and compensate those displaced. These are the places where the state’s
  traditional proxies for coordination, blanket bans, fixed fees, one-size
  ordinances, were doing the work of high transaction costs. Once discovery,
  commitment, and verification get cheap, the proxy can retire.


  The second claim is harder, and more important. Some interests should not be
  left to willingness-to-pay or willingness-to-accept because doing so corrupts
  the good at stake or produces outcomes that citizens will not recognize as
  legitimate. Walzer’s point about “blocked exchanges” captures the first worry:
  there are goods that change their meaning when bought and sold, from votes to
  child adoption to bodily integrity (Walzer 1983). Habermas’s point about
  public reason captures the second: some decisions require justification that
  can be shared among free and equal citizens, not just a price that someone
  could afford to pay (Habermas 1996). An agentic market that buys consent for
  lead exposure near a school or pulverizes a historic site by compensating
  homeowners may be “efficient” in a narrow sense and still be a civic failure.
  The right remedy here is not to engineer a cleverer auction, but to
  constitutionalize a right: no exposure above a threshold; no demolition of
  protected heritage; no pricing of disability accommodations that should be
  dignitary entitlements.


  Initial entitlements also matter because wealth matters. Coase’s invariance
  result assumes away liquidity constraints and endowments. In reality, who
  starts with the entitlement shifts the distribution and, through standard
  income effects, the allocation itself. A poor farmer may sell away air quality
  to a rich factory not because the trade maximizes welfare, but because cash
  now beats health later. The literature on endowment effects and reference
  dependence explains why “take” and “give” valuations diverge by large factors
  (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1990). We cannot wish that asymmetry away by
  adding agents; we can only choose the starting point with care and then temper
  the resulting market with devices like liability rules or option taxes when
  strategic holdout threatens to derail otherwise beneficial projects (Harberger
  1962; Posner & Weyl 2017).


  The right practical stance is to treat the baseline allocation of rights as a
  constitutional question, updated by democratic means on a slow clock, with
  agents assisting rather than replacing that process. There is good evidence
  that preference learning can compress messy human judgments into interpretable
  principles, work on “inverse constitutions” shows that a small number of
  well-chosen statements can reconstruct many downstream choices (cf.
  constitutional preference learning). That capacity should be a tool for
  deliberation, not a substitute for it. Agents can help citizens articulate
  trade-offs, simulate consequences, and surface stable patterns in what people
  actually value; they should not collapse deliberation into latent embeddings
  that nobody can contest.


  Once the non-negotiables are in place, the legal form of protection still
  matters. Calabresi and Melamed’s framework is again useful. A property rule
  says you cannot take without consent; a liability rule says you can take but
  must pay an assessed amount; inalienability says you cannot take or sell at
  all. Agentic markets naturally reach for liability rules because they are
  legible to software and pair well with escrow. That is a feature, but also a
  temptation. There are domains in which property rules are the better
  discipline on power, because consent must be obtained in fact, not induced by
  a clever price, and domains in which inalienability reflects a moral boundary
  we are unwilling to cross. The system needs the courage to leave some switches
  as hard “no.”


  None of this is an argument for ossification. Rights evolve, and a healthy
  polity revises them as circumstances change. Here, too, agents can help. They
  can give lawmakers and courts high-resolution feedback about how rights
  interact on the ground, where thresholds are biting, where unintended
  consequences are accumulating. They can lower the cost of participation for
  citizens who are usually priced out of rulemaking. James C. Scott’s critique
  of legibility is not a counsel of despair; it is a warning to keep the map
  faithful to the terrain. If agents are the cartographers, the community must
  remain the editor (Scott 1998).


  The final constitutional question is privacy, because measurement is the hinge
  on which enforcement turns. A system that prices externalities through
  telemetry must also protect persons from becoming permanently legible. That
  protection cannot be an afterthought stapled onto a data lake. It must be part
  of the right itself: a claim to have conduct verified without revealing raw
  traces; a claim to have profiles portable and erasable; a claim to contest and
  correct model inferences that affect one’s bargaining power. Differential
  privacy and zero-knowledge proofs are promising techniques, but they require
  institutional guardianship and budget discipline to matter in practice (Dwork
  2006; Ben-Sasson et al. 2014). A constitution for agentic markets must
  therefore bind not only what we may price, but how we may prove, remember, and
  forget.


  If we take these commitments seriously, the shape of the project becomes
  clearer. Use markets energetically where they respect the good at stake.
  Reserve rights, unapologetically, where pricing would degrade meaning or
  legitimacy. Choose initial entitlements with an eye to power and
  participation. Give privacy the status of a right, not a setting. And let
  agents serve the constitution, not supplant it. The next step is to make this
  operational by specifying the minimal governance stack that keeps bargaining
  honest: identity without surveillance, contracts that produce evidence rather
  than promises, monitors that prove without peeking, dispute systems that
  resolve without bankrupting, and interoperability that prevents platforms from
  becoming the new ministries of planning.


  **The minimal governance stack**


  If rights draw the border of what may be traded, a working system still needs
  a spine to hold transactions upright. The promise of agentic bargaining
  collapses without credible identity, contracts that produce evidence,
  monitoring that does not turn into surveillance, venues for settling disputes
  that do not bankrupt participants, and interoperability that prevents any
  single platform from becoming a planner in disguise. None of these pieces is
  exotic. What matters is specifying them plainly, choosing designs that respect
  the constitutional layer, and refusing shortcuts that buy convenience with
  legitimacy.


  The first pillar is identity and personhood. A bargaining regime cannot
  function if one resident can become ten thousand agents at will. Sybil attacks
  are not a paranoid edge case; they are the default in open systems (Douceur
  2002). Yet the cure cannot be a universal passport check welded to every
  interaction. We need proofs of personhood and of relevant attributes,
  residency, ownership, guardianship, without building a permanent dossier.
  Cryptographic attestations and selective disclosure can carry most of this
  weight: a resident can prove “I live within these boundaries” without exposing
  a street address; a business can prove it owns a fleet without doxing every
  truck; a citizen can show single-personhood without yielding biometric
  exhaust. Zero-knowledge techniques make these claims verifiable without
  revealing the underlying data (Ben-Sasson et al. 2014). The constraint here is
  institutional rather than mathematical: credential issuers must be plural and
  contestable to avoid a de facto internal passport, and revocation must be
  governed with due process rather than platform whim. The goal is simple to
  state and hard to implement: one human, one principal, one accountable agent,
  proved just enough to bargain, without converting the city into a checkpoint.


  Contracts and evidence come next. Szabo’s old intuition that “smart contracts”
  are promises with built-in proofs rather than prose with aspirations remains
  sound if we resist the tendency to make everything on-chain and irreversible
  (Szabo 1997). Most agreements in civic life are contingent and contextual;
  they need structured terms, clear triggers, and graceful failure modes, not
  baroque code. An agent-friendly contract language should specify outcomes that
  can be verified by multiple independent oracles, define what constitutes
  admissible evidence, and embed escrow that releases automatically when the
  evidence threshold clears. If a fleet buys passage through a sensitive block,
  the proof might be a cryptographic commitment to a route combined with a
  zero-knowledge statement that the truck did not stray into a prohibited zone.
  If a developer compensates residents for construction noise, the proof might
  be an auditor’s signed attestation that decibel levels stayed within the
  agreed envelope. The contract should name at least two oracles and a fallback
  to human adjudication; it should also specify retention limits for any
  telemetry produced so that “verification” does not quietly become surveillance
  (Kroll et al. 2017).


  Monitoring and audits are where James C. Scott’s warning becomes urgent. To
  verify is to measure, and to measure is to simplify (Scott 1998). Differential
  privacy helps when outcomes can be aggregated, neighborhood air quality,
  average curb occupancy, because it allows learning without remembering
  individuals (Dwork 2006). Zero-knowledge proofs help when outcomes are binary,
  complied or not, because they certify a property of a trace without revealing
  the trace itself (Ben-Sasson et al. 2014). Between those poles lies the messy
  center of civic life, where signals are partial and context matters. Here the
  discipline must be architectural. The system should favor local, minimally
  sufficient sensors; it should push computation to the edge so raw data does
  not travel; it should define strict retention windows and audit them; and it
  should make audit logs legible to the people who are measured. If we cannot
  explain to a resident what was sensed, for how long, by whom, and for what
  remedy, we have already eroded the legitimacy we hope pricing will earn.


  Dispute resolution cannot be an afterthought, because no oracle is perfect and
  no contract anticipates every edge case. The ambition is modest: most
  disagreements should be resolved quickly by online dispute resolution with
  clear evidentiary standards and time limits; stubborn cases should escalate to
  human arbitrators with authority to weigh context and fairness; true hard
  cases should go to courts that can set precedent and discipline the rest of
  the stack (Katsh & Rabinovich-Einy 2017; Susskind 2019). If we want bargaining
  to replace bans, we have to make losing a dispute affordable. That means fee
  caps, loser-pays only when abuse is clear, and public reporting of timelines
  and error rates so that the system can learn. It also means a duty to publish
  de-identified decisions as open data. Ostrom taught that commons management
  depends on cheap, trusted forums for conflict (Ostrom 1990). In software, that
  translates to quick, explainable outcomes that people can live with even when
  they disagree.


  Interoperability is the quiet hinge on which everything else turns. Without
  it, agent providers become chokepoints and their “defaults” become policy.
  Shapiro and Varian explained long ago how switching costs and network effects
  entrench incumbents (Shapiro & Varian 1998), and Klemperer mapped the tools
  firms use to lock customers in (Klemperer 1995). An agentic governance layer
  cannot tolerate those dynamics. Data portability, open messaging and discovery
  protocols, and common contract primitives are not niceties; they are
  constitutional requirements. If a resident wants to switch providers because a
  platform starts steering negotiations to favored counterparties, the move
  should feel like changing a web browser: export your keys and your cognitive
  profile, import them across the street, and carry on. Polycentric governance
  only works if exit is real (Ostrom 2010). Platform studies have shown how
  quickly “infrastructure” inherits regulatory power by default (Gillespie
  2018). The antidote is not a better trust-us pledge; it is requirements,
  verified in code and audited in public.


  Finally, safety rails have to be built in at the protocol level, not stapled
  on after the first scandal. Normal accident theory is a standing warning that
  tightly coupled, complex systems fail in ways no one foresaw (Perrow 1984).
  Leveson’s work on system-theoretic hazard analysis adds a more constructive
  lesson: safety is an emergent property you engineer with constraints, not a
  checklist you apply at the end (Leveson 2011). In practice, that means
  maintaining a registry of no-trade domains with public justification and
  periodic review; rate-limiting automated negotiations in categories where
  correlated mistakes are dangerous; running red-team leagues that attack the
  sensing and oracle layer; and pre-committing to kill-switches governed by due
  process rather than executive fiat. Ord’s account of existential and global
  catastrophic risks is blunt about what belongs here: pathogen access pathways,
  certain classes of cyber-physical control, and other “recipes for ruin” are
  not markets to be cleared (Ord 2020). The state’s role is indispensable at
  this boundary, because only the state can compel, investigate, and punish
  across the whole field.


  Put together, these pieces amount to a minimal governance stack. They are not
  utopian. They require law as well as code, institutions as well as models, and
  patient engineering rather than rhetorical leaps. But they are also not
  exotic. We already know how to write attestations, define evidence, resolve
  disputes online, and force portability when the public interest demands it.
  Lessig’s old claim that “code is law” was never a prediction that software
  would replace statutes; it was a warning that architecture channels behavior
  whether we admit it or not (Lessig 1999).
