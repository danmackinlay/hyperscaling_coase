## 1. The Krier mapping, device by device

Overall verdict: **FAILS.** Two of six assignments have no textual basis (1b, 1e), one is misclassified on an axis the model froze (1d), one is right on capability and wrong on outcome and on enforcement (1a), one holds narrowed (1c), one is harmless (1f).

**1a. Digital union → cell C, "needs the pact enforced, which is a rule, not an agent."** HOLDS WITH NARROWING on capability; FAILS on outcome and enforcement. The passage: agents of 10,000 residents, seeing a factory's proposal, form a coalition in a nanosecond and name one collective price, non-negotiable. A non-negotiable price is a residents' commitment, so C by capability. But: (α) the factory in that passage is agented too, so by the plan's own default-shift rule both sides can commit and the cell is D, not C. The plan lands it in C only by giving the residents the first move, i.e. the sequential reading; under the sequential reading with provable (hence observable) commitments the second mover accommodates and D never occurs. The plan cannot use the sequential reading to file the union and the simultaneous reading to derive the standoff. (β) Outcome: the plan's C has the union demanding 20. Krier never says X is the whole surplus, and his companion device (the club, 1e) elicits each member's *minimum acceptable*, i.e. roughly harms, 14, S≈0. His union by his own design is C-capability with B-outcome, a cell the table lacks. (γ) Enforcement: at k=3 each resident is a veto player, so individual provable floors (harm plus share) do everything the pact does; no collective enforcement is needed beyond summing. The plan's own premise, that an agent can publish and prove an instruction, supplies it. The pact is load-bearing only at k=1, and there it is a coordination equilibrium among standing instructions that provable instructions let each resident verify in the others (publish the membership hashes). Again the plan's own premise. See §2.

**1b. Developer against every homeowner → cell B.** FAILS. The passage: the developer's agent must discover the price at which every homeowner would agree; if residents value the character of the neighbourhood highly that price may be very high; the project proceeds only if the developer values the site more than residents value the status quo. The next paragraph: what if a resident names an absurd price and holds everyone to ransom. The developer here has no commitment power at all: it must *meet* each homeowner's reservation, the homeowners hold the entitlement and can hold out for the surplus. That is the river's k=n holdout regime with the residents holding the leverage, A or C, not B. Nothing in Krier has the developer tie its hands or issue a standard-form take-it-or-leave-it; the plan's "developer's standard-form offer to every homeowner" is the plan's invention. Krier presents the unadorned developer case as the *problem* that motivates his secondary institutions; the plan reads it as his proposal. The plan's gloss ("the better the developer's agent knows each homeowner's floor, the less it pays") describes a game Krier does not describe: in his text the floors are revealed by the homeowners' own club, not discovered by the developer.

**1c. Option fee → liability rule, cell B in law.** HOLDS WITH NARROWING. A Harberger-style self-assessed tax [sure: Posner & Weyl 2017 J. Legal Analysis; *Radical Markets* 2018] with a sell obligation makes declared values approach true values; the project proceeds at roughly declared values; S≈0; B-like outcome. Narrowing: (α) it is a residents'-side device. The resident sets the price under a cost rising in the price. In the plan's own vocabulary it is a tax on d_N, a cost on aggressive commitment: it attacks D directly by making the residents' standoff expensive, rather than legislating B. (β) Without the sell obligation (Krier does not state one) it is a holdout tax; residents still set the price; that is C with costly commitment, and whether demand-proportional commitment costs select moderate demands in an Ellingsen–Miettinen-type game I cannot vouch for [unsure]. (γ) Krier pairs it with a fading vote weight for chronic hyper-bidders, which is a move along k, the river's entitlement axis, which the 2×2 freezes at 3. (δ) Krier offers it as a club rule and says in terms that these secondary institutions can be voluntary; "written into law" is the plan's phrase.

**1d. Reimbursing veto → liability rule, cell B in law.** FAILS. The passage: any coalition that vetoes must reimburse the quantified loss, with agents handling the transfers; stonewalling remains possible but carries a real, rising cost. That is Calabresi–Melamed Rule 4 [sure: C&M 1972; *Spur Industries v. Del E. Webb* 1972], the party who stops the activity compensates the actor, not Rule 2 (actor pays damages), which is what "cell B in law" is. Run it in the river at π=20, k=3 with the factory committed to paying harms (2,3,9): each resident compares accepting (net 0) with vetoing (a share of 20); accepts; S=0, B-like outcome. But with the factory committed to (1,2,8): accepting (−1,−1,−1) beats vetoing (−20/3 each), so residents end *below* their no-deal payoff. The device removes the floor the plan's yardstick claim says unanimity guarantees in every cell; the plan files under B a device that violates its own yardstick claim. At π=10, if the gross loss is reimbursed, the same logic produces pollution, the inefficient outcome; if the net loss is reimbursed, someone must quantify residents' harm, which is the planner's problem back again. So the plan's "which is why they work" is unexamined. Structurally the reimbursing veto is an asymmetric cost on *maintaining* the residents' incompatible commitment, which is exactly the plan's §8 bullet 2 escape from D ("if the cost of a standoff is large enough that one side always backs down"), built by Krier and misfiled by the plan as a statute fixing a price. The same paragraph adds a further D-exit the plan never considers: other residents, aware of the gains, bargain directly with the holdouts, i.e. third-party side payments to the vetoers.

**1e. Bargaining club → cell D ("agents negotiate freely and all can commit").** FAILS. There is one club in Krier's text: homeowners pool their agents; each privately inputs the minimum they would accept; the software aggregates into a single take-it-or-leave-it offer to the developer; if it clears, payout is at the common clearing price; padding your number does not raise your cheque; the group speaks with one voice without surrendering property rights. That is a residents'-side mechanism, same side and same function as the union, C by capability, and the developer is never said to commit. The plan's device, free negotiation with everyone committing, appears nowhere; the nearest thing in the essay is the e/acc free-for-all Krier explicitly rejects as the opposite trap. If the plan wants a Krier D it must construct "union meets a pre-committed developer," which Krier never describes.

The attack the plan missed by pattern-matching to a cell: run his club on the river's numbers. A common clearing price with minimums (2,3,9) must be ≥9 to get resident 3, total 27 > 20: the club kills an efficient deal. Sum-of-minimums with individual payouts: padding pays if the deal still clears, so his incentive-compatibility claim fails. Either reading loses one of his two claims, and the river section's own Myerson–Satterthwaite / Mailath–Postlewaite citations already say the club's four joint properties (voluntary, budget-balanced, honest, efficient) cannot coexist. There is also a tension between his fiduciary agents (loyal to the principal) and a club that needs them to report minimums. Both are sharper than "it is cell D."

**1f. Thermostat and parking → cell A with custom.** HOLDS WITH NARROWING, harmless. Krier's passage is about adoption path (office thermostat, apartment-block resources, chores) and mechanism reuse (the parking-dispute mechanism scaled to urban planning). These are repeated games among a fixed small group where reputation enforces; the thermostat is preference aggregation, not pie division; "custom pinning the split" is a stand-in for repetition. The cell says nothing about his actual claim there (reuse across scale), and the plan concedes none of the model bites.

**Underlying error.** Krier's devices operate on the entitlement axis (who holds the right; property versus liability protection; k) and on the cost of holding out (taxes, reimbursement). The plan's cells vary only commitment *capability* at fixed k=3 under a property rule. Mapping entitlement devices onto commitment cells is a category error, and it is what makes every device look like either a cell or a regulation. **Settle it:** a table with Krier's sentence for each device, the cell's defining condition (who can commit; who moves first; which entitlement), and a tick only where the sentence states the condition. On my reading two of six get a tick.

## 2. Does Krier describe enforcement of the union against members who would deal separately?

Not as a no-side-deals rule. He says the group speaks with one voice without surrendering property rights, which is the opposite: members keep the right to deal. Three things in his text do the job anyway: (i) the club software is the channel, each member's agent routes offers through it, and under the plan's own premise a member's agent provably routing through the club *is* the commitment, made by an agent; (ii) automated escrows and reputation systems, which he offers as the enforcement layer for agent contracts generally; (iii) courts enforcing contracts, which he names as the state's non-negotiable role, and a pact among residents is a contract. **Which cell:** the residents-commit cell, enforced by the same means as every other commitment in the model plus contract law. The plan's "a rule, not an agent" is true only if contract law counts as the regulation Krier denies; he counts it as the baseline. The honest gap in Krier is human override of an agent's configuration, which he never addresses; but it weakens the factory's commitment equally and so does not move the cell.

## 3. The institutions-choose claim

**Verdict: HOLDS WITH NARROWING, and not as a disagreement with Krier.**

Holds: given unconditional-demand commitment with revocation costs, a rule assigning commitment to one side yields B or C, and a liability rule or a union rule does that.

Strongest counter-arguments:
(α) It restates Krier and Coase. Krier: a secondary layer of institutions is needed on top of the agents; the initial allocation is a constitutional choice; institutions matter; the state is framework guarantor; clever mechanisms are needed against digital paralysis and minorities holding majorities hostage; the system is a hybrid, agents for micro-coordination and designed institutions including the state for the rest. Coase 1960 [sure] is about bargaining *given* legal entitlements. The plan presents the Coasean setup as a refutation of Coaseans.
(β) "Nothing else gets out of D" fails on at least six exits, most of them in the plan's own premises or Krier's text: conditional (program) commitment [sure: Tennenholtz 2004, *Games and Economic Behavior*]; observability of provable commitments, which makes the game sequential; priced commitment (Krier's options analogy, §4); asymmetric standoff costs (plan §8 bullet 2; Krier's reimbursing veto); third-party buy-outs of holdouts (same Krier paragraph); and a change of k (the river's own axis; Krier's vote-weight fade). At k=2 in the river the factory buys residents 1 and 2 for a little over (2,3), resident 3 is harmed unpaid, and the deal closes: bad for resident 3, but out of D, and neither a liability rule nor a union rule.
(γ) "Written into law": Krier's union rule is a club bylaw under contract law; his liability rule is nuisance damages from the centuries of jurisprudence he names as the right starting point. The equivocation between "institution" and "regulation" is the whole residual disagreement, and it is semantic.
(δ) The 2×2 is Calabresi–Melamed's four rules re-derived with commitment as mechanism: A ≈ Rule 1 with bargaining, B ≈ Rule 2, C ≈ Rule 1 with full monopoly extraction, D ≈ Rule 1 with the injunction standing. "Institutions choose the cell" is C&M's 1972 thesis, which the river already cites. The plan's novelty is that commitment *technology* selects among them absent law; §8 of this report argues it does not.

**Narrowing that saves it:** "Given unconditional, simultaneous, mutually unobserved commitments, the division is fixed by whichever rule assigns commitment to one side; such rules can be voluntary (club bylaws under contract law) or statutory; other exits (conditional commitment, sequencing, priced commitment, standoff costs, changing k) exist and are institutions in the same broad sense." That is Krier's position.

**Settle it:** compute the pie-of-6 game four ways. (i) Unconditional, simultaneous, unobserved: check Ellingsen & Miettinen 2008 AER [sure it exists]; my recollection is that *costless* commitment gives a continuum of equilibria including efficient ones and it is the *small positive* cost that selects the unique conflict equilibrium [likely], which would reverse the plan's attribution ("this is what happens when committing is free and provable"); Crawford 1982 Econometrica [sure] gets impasse from *uncertain* revocation costs, not known large ones [likely], so D with known large r on both sides is not his case either. (ii) Sequential, observable: first mover takes all, no D. (iii) Program commitments: see §8. (iv) Per-period asymmetric standoff cost: who backs down. Plus a k=2 run.

## 4. Pricing stability against flexibility: does it address D directly?

Strongest case that it does. D is the state in which commitment is free to both sides and so both hold it. An option is a priced commitment: the writer sells flexibility for a premium. If stability versus flexibility is explicitly priced, commitment becomes a scarce traded good; one side buys it, and the premium is the division, B or C with S set by the price of commitment rather than by speed or statute. Two further sentences in the same paragraph are equilibrium-selection arguments for the compatible-commitment equilibrium: rules ossify by mutual agreement (both commit to the same rule, the Pareto-superior equilibrium of the simultaneous commitment game), and agents converge on stable equilibria because constant renegotiation is expensive even for AGI (the plan's own §8 bullet 2, standoff cost makes someone back down). In Ellingsen–Miettinen the conflict probability rises as the commitment cost falls [likely]; pricing commitment pushes the other way. Honest limits: one sentence, an analogy, no mechanism; who writes the option against 10,000 counterparties is unaddressed; options price exogenous volatility, bargaining volatility is strategic [sure, as a standard disanalogy]. Verdict: it addresses D in direction, not in design, and the plan's "nothing else" is obliged to engage it and does not.

FAILS as a reading of his proposal; HOLDS as a critique of his rhetoric. (α) He says it himself: hybrid system, agents for micro-coordination, designed institutions including the state for the rest; so the slogan reports his own division of labour as a concession extracted from him. (β) "Exactly" is false by the plan's own cell-A concession and by his small-case domain (thermostat, truck fee, leaf blower), where it is bargaining and the plan says it works. (δ) The devices the slogan counts as not-bargaining (courts, constitutional allocation, club mechanisms, Harberger) are named by Krier as design, and two of them (courts, allocation) are Coase's own preconditions. What would make the slogan fair: a large case in which Krier claims agents alone suffice. The paragraph after the developer example says the opposite.

## 6. Which claims Krier would accept or deny

- **Standoff:** deny as stated. His grounds: agents "condition their actions on shared signals and contracts" (Krier), which he calls a correlated equilibrium escaping Nash deadlock; agents converge on stable equilibria to save compute; the secondary institutions. He accepts the *risk* under his own names for it: digital NIMBYism at unprecedented scale, digital paralysis, minorities holding majorities hostage.
- **Default-shift:** deny both ends. His baseline for large cases is the planner (zoning, bans, political fights), not haggling; his destination is set by the default position, which he says the machinery runs either way around, and by institutions, not by technology.
- **Institutions-choose:** accept, with two corrections: the institutions can be voluntary, and contract enforcement plus the initial allocation are state roles he explicitly keeps. Deny "nothing else."
- **Convergence:** accept the mathematics, deny the relevance: his agents do not play a fixed cell, they build the secondary layer; and whether agents debias negotiations or represent our quirks is, in his words, an open design question.
- **Race:** accept the structure (the nanosecond coalition is a speed claim) and say the residents win it; deny that the compute ratio is left to the market: agent vouchers, compute credits, baseline agent services like public defenders, a right to an agent, and the remark that a billionaire's phone is no better than yours.
- **Krier mapping:** deny developer→B (no commitment power; faces ransom), deny club→D (a residents' mechanism), deny reimbursing veto→B (a cost on vetoers), accept union→C by capability, accept option fee as liability-like, accept thermostat as small.
- **Yardstick:** accept. He says distribution is fixed by the initial allocation, so S is a quantity he already says agents do not set; his own yardsticks are efficiency plus compensation. He would add the diffuse public (Ganesh's unconscious blob) as a party the river omits.
- **Bridge:** deny. Agreeableness, deference, corrigibility, debiasing are design parameters in his text; reputation systems and escrow make the game repeated, where equilibrium play is the "softened" play.

- **Default-shift.** Human large cases sit under zoning and nuisance law, i.e. the planner, not cell A; the model has no planner cell, and Krier's thesis is comparative against the planner. Zoning that blocks a project is the D *outcome* by statute, so "agents land in D" is "agents reproduce the status quo at worst." And provable means observable; with the river's instant messaging, simultaneity has measure zero, so the default moves to a race for B or C, not to D.
- **Standoff.** HOLDS WITH NARROWING to unconditional, simultaneous, unobserved demands (§8). E–M attribution likely reversed; Crawford needs uncertain revocation costs [both likely].
- **Race.** Contradicts default-shift: if agents commit cheaply, residents' standing instructions pre-form the pact at configuration time and there is no race. Rates proportional to compute is an unsupported stipulation [unsure that any paper models coalition formation that way]. "The only cell in which a resident can end worse off than under no deal" is false given the plan's own filing of the reimbursing veto under B (1d).
- **Convergence in B.** No information setting in the river supports an error of σ/√c on h_i: in the transparent river the factory knows h_i at c=0; in the private river the river's own text says h_i is unlearnable because each resident is the only source and is paid to misreport (Myerson–Satterthwaite 1983; Rob 1989; Mailath–Postlewaite 1990, all cited there). In D, provability makes the standoff certain at every c, so "more compute makes the impasse more reliable" has no content under the plan's own premise.
- **Yardstick.** Fine for the 2×2; violated by a device the plan files in B (1d); and nobody in Krier offered "median better off."
- **Bridge.** In the Nash demand game "better at the game" means better at selecting among a continuum of equilibria, not more aggressive; human coordination on focal equal splits is robust [likely: Nydegger & Owen 1974; Roth & Murnighan 1982]; better agents coordinating faster on a focal point is the opposite of stripping the softening.
- **Cell A internal.** "The residents, bargaining as one" with r_N=0 is a contradiction: bargaining as one is a commitment. Krier's examples mostly carry outside options (other routes, sites, venues), which cap demands and turn would-be standoffs into substitutions. The river is fair for the sited factory, not for the development case the plan maps onto it.

## 8. The single most damaging objection

The plan models commitment as a number plus a penalty, made simultaneously and unobserved: Schelling's 1950s diplomat. Its own premise is agents that publish their *instructions* and a hash of them. Published instructions are programs: observable before the counterpart commits, and conditionable on the counterpart's program, which is exactly the program-equilibrium setting [sure: Tennenholtz 2004], and the hash is exactly its recognition device. In the river's pie of 6, the program "demand 3 if the other side's program is the mirror of this one, otherwise demand 6" yields 3 against itself and 0 against an unconditional grab; the unconditional grab yields 0 against everything. So the plan's cell D is a *weakly dominated* outcome once commitments are programs, not the forced result of cheap commitment. What survives is a coordination problem over *which* compatible split, with no-deal as the miscoordination outcome; a focal point (the equal split, the legal default, a club rule, a custom) resolves it, and under sequential publication the first mover takes nearly all, which is a race for B or C, never D. Krier's text names the resolution in the one sentence the plan never quotes, the one about conditioning on shared signals and contracts, and his whole secondary layer is focal-point provision. The honest residual for the author is the commitment-races problem [sure: Kokotajlo 2019; Critch, Dennis & Russell 2022 on open-source game theory, likely]: early commitment can lock in a bad split. But its known resolution is early commitment to a conditional fair policy, which is Krier's design, not the plan's dilemma of regulation or standoff. Everything downstream (default-shift to D, institutions as the only exit, the slogan, the cell-D filing of a device Krier does not have) inherits the restriction of commitments to unconditional numbers, and that restriction is the author's, not the technology's.
