  ###### _**Nocturne in Black and Gold – The Falling Rocket, James McNeill
  Whistler**_ (1875)


  _You wake to a notification: your agent has negotiated 47 agreements
  overnight. The street outside your flat will be resurfaced next Tuesday
  (you’ll receive £32 in compensation for noise disruption). Your building’s
  heating schedule has been optimised in a deal with your neighbours — you get
  cooler mornings, they get warmer evenings, net savings of £8/month each. A
  local restaurant will reduce its late-night deliveries in exchange for your
  support in their license renewal. Your portion of the neighbourhood’s
  collective negotiation with the new development: approved with design
  modifications, you’re receiving £620 to compensate for construction impacts._


  _You scan the list while your coffee is being made. Everything seems... fine?
  Your flat sits closest to the resurfacing, so the system assigned you a
  slightly higher share of the noise-compensation pot. It correctly predicted
  you’d be happy to support the restaurant — you eat there often. The
  development deal is more complex, but the summary says it balances community
  character with housing need, which sounds good._


  _Then you reach the item about the new bike lane. Your agent opposed it,
  citing your revealed preference for parking convenience. But… you’ve been
  meaning to start cycling. And wasn’t the whole point to reduce car traffic?
  You stare at the screen. Should you override it? But the negotiation is closed
  and reopening it would force the system to renegotiate the whole bundle — your
  agent estimates the effective cost to you at about £120. You approve the
  batch._


  _It is only later, walking past where the bike lane won’t be, that you notice
  how the wind cuts right through the street, meeting no resistance at all._


  In a recent essay, Seb Krier argues that by reducing transaction costs to
  near-zero, AI agents could finally enable the kind of decentralised
  coordination that economists have long theorised but never observed. Instead
  of clumsy regulations imposed from above, millions of agents would negotiate
  continuously on behalf of their users, finding optimal solutions that make
  everyone better off.


  The vision draws on Ronald Coase, who argued that if bargaining were cheap
  enough, people could negotiate their own solutions to conflicts like pollution
  without needing one-size-fits-all regulation, and Elinor Ostrom, who
  documented how communities successfully govern shared resources through
  intricate local rules rather than top-down control. The result would be a kind
  of liquid governance that can morph according to the shifting complexities of
  human preference rather than the dictum of the bureaucrat.


  Explicitly pricing externalities - the hidden cost arising from the way our
  actions impact others - is the core of the argument. But Krier’s essay is not
  a simple libertarian fantasy about how markets can solve everything. He
  acknowledges real limits such as catastrophic risks which cannot be priced and
  the role of the state as a guarantor of the system. There is consideration of
  how public services would be reformed to ensure universal access. In addition,
  he is explicit that some boundaries will always require democratic
  enforcement.


  All this sounds much better than a single omniscient central planner. But it
  has some deep flaws from a social democratic perspective. If we just price
  people’s selfishness correctly we can solve so many public ills. And yet, this
  leaves off the table the transformation of selfishness through institutions,
  public norms and technology.


  We are a better society for everyone having the ability to read. Can we not
  envision a better demos with capable AI agents?


  Recall the bike lane. The issue is not that the agent did not understand the
  preference well enough. It’s that the agent is focused on price discovery when
  your preference is embryonic. You never deliberated about whether cycling
  infrastructure or parking convenience mattered more to you. You never weighed
  your personal convenience against broader community needs. You never even
  thought about it until the decision was already made.


  For questions requiring small-p political judgements there are a number of
  factors to weigh. You need to:


  *   Consider facts you don’t yet know (would bike lanes actually reduce
  traffic? who benefits?)


  *   Weigh competing values you hold (personal convenience vs. collective
  benefit)


  *   Think about obligations to others (current residents vs. future residents,
  drivers vs. cyclists)


  *   Imagine possibilities you haven’t considered (what if you started
  cycling?)


  *   Reason about what’s fair, not just what benefits you


  This is about forming a judgment through a process of reasoning. And
  crucially, this reasoning often happens with and through others. You hear your
  neighbours perspective on the housing shortage and it changes how you think
  about development. You discuss climate change with a friend and it shifts how
  you value cycling infrastructure. Someone points out accessibility issues
  you’d never considered. Through these exchanges you figure out what you think
  you actually want.


  Certain relationships require direct engagement to function properly. You
  can’t outsource the deliberation to a representative because the deliberation
  is what creates the relationship. The same is true for political communities.
  Citizens who never reason together about shared problems are just individuals
  who happen to live near each other and whose agents negotiate on their behalf.


  What is unsettling about Krier’s vision is that it forecloses a certain kind
  of political development. If agents always act on revealed preferences before
  citizens form judgments, then citizens never develop the capacity to form
  political judgments at all. The muscle atrophies from disuse.


  The preference problem doesn’t stop at the individual level. Krier recognises
  that neighbourhood politics is often derailed by holdouts, so he proposes
  “bargaining clubs” as a solution. Instead of negotiating individually,
  residents pool their agents and input secret minimums, which the system
  aggregates into a single take-it-or-leave-it offer. In theory, this eliminates
  strategic exaggeration and allows the community to speak with one voice.


  But mechanism design only functions when everyone subject to the collective
  outcome joins it. Real communities contain ideological objectors and
  mistrustful residents who will simply refuse to opt in. If even one necessary
  party refuses to join the club, they retain their veto power, and the
  mechanism recreates the very holdout problem it was meant to solve.


  Still, even if we grant that bargaining clubs could weaken holdouts, a deeper
  problem remains. Krier’s broader vision relies on agents that continuously
  learn and infer your preferences from behavioural data. Once the agent steps
  in to propose your “minimum acceptable price,” the nature of the bargain
  changes entirely. Your minimum is no longer something you consciously judge or
  commit to; it becomes a statistical artefact generated by a model’s internal
  representation of you. A system may infer that you will accept less because
  you are poor, or conflict-averse, or historically willing to acquiesce.


  Consider two residents asking for compensation to endure construction noise:
  the model knows the wealthy resident can afford to wait indefinitely, forcing
  a high offer, while it detects the struggling resident’s need for cash,
  triggering a calculated lowball. Inequality, temperament, and circumstance
  become encoded directly into the clearing price.


  Krier’s answer to this is that the market will solve it - you can simply hire
  a tougher agent. But the opposing model, training on socioeconomic data and
  public records will likely calculate your actual capacity to walk away. If a
  vulnerable resident’s agent bluffs like a billionaire, the deal simply
  collapses, and they lose a necessary payout. You can change the strategy of
  the agent, but you cannot change the leverage of the user.


  Once agents are inferring your minimum, the negotiation no longer occurs
  between citizens expressing judgments but between models exchanging
  predictions. The final “price” that clears is whatever emerges from the model
  architecture: how it weighs past behaviour, what features it uses to
  approximate utility, how its training data encodes socioeconomic patterns, and
  what objective function it optimises. In other words, the bargain is shaped
  not by voluntary assent but by the design choices, priors, and incentives
  embedded in the systems negotiating on your behalf. At that point the Coasean
  ideal of voluntary exchange collapses; what looks like a negotiated agreement
  is in fact a technocratic simulation, determined more by the structure of the
  models than by the people they claim to represent.


  Politics is a process of joint reasoning and identity formation. This is what
  is missing from Krier’s proposals; it’s why Coasean schemes have never
  governed nations and it is why Ostrom’s most successful communities do not
  rely on clever incentive design alone. They work because norms, trust, and
  shared understandings grow through repeated interaction.


  Even if these mechanisms worked perfectly, they would still govern trivial,
  reversible parts of collective life. As Krier acknowledges, the questions that
  matter most – AI governance, genetic engineering, long-term climate risk —
  simply cannot be resolved like this. They require shared reasoning,
  legitimacy, and moral imagination. What is needed is a better process of
  deliberation that helps citizens develop political judgment rather than
  outsources it.


  What would that look like? Here is a rough sketch:


  1.   **Agents as guides for individual reasoning.**  

  Before a negotiation ever occurs, an agent could help a citizen understand the
  stakes of an issue, surface relevant information, and explore the trade-offs
  they have not yet considered. Not to decide for them, but to help them form a
  view. Chatbots already do fragments of this in consumer and health contexts;
  the civic analogue is obvious.


  1.   **Agents as scaffolds for collective deliberation.**  

  If agents can mediate millions of micro-transactions, they can also mediate
  millions of micro-conversations. AI could help structure town-hall processes,
  ensure under-represented voices are heard, summarise points of agreement, map
  disagreements, translate jargon, and make the interaction of hundreds or
  thousands of citizens tractable. The point is not to “optimise the outcome”
  but to make large-scale public reasoning actually possible. Even without AI,
  successful attempts at something like this have been made in Taiwan (you can
  read more on this here).


  1.   **Agents as executors of democratically chosen aims.**  

  Once the human community has argued, weighed, and settled the goals or
  constraints—what is permissible, what values matter, what futures are
  off-limits—agents can handle the fine-grained negotiation, scheduling,
  compensation, and enforcement. Price systems handle the coordination; the
  judgment about what we are coordinating toward remains with us.


  This is the inverse of Krier’s architecture. His system begins with
  agent-to-agent negotiation and assumes that preferences exist a priori. A
  deliberative system begins with citizens, with agents serving to expand their
  capacities for reasoning, coordination, and imagination.
