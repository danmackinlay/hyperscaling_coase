## Convergence

Weighted by independence: a finding counts when two or more attackers reached it without being pointed at it, or when the cold reader reached it alone. The briefed attackers were pointed at the model's own section 8, so restatements of its three falsifiers count for less. Counts are out of six.

**Findings all six reached independently.**

1. **The standoff is asserted, not derived, and the game in which both sides can commit contains the model's cells B and C.** The cold reader reached it from Nash's demand game alone: with free, certain, checkable commitment every pair of positions summing to 6 is an equilibrium, (6,6) only weakly, and nothing in the model picks (6,6). The five briefed readers reached it from Ellingsen and Miettinen's text, which the collator has verified: with perfectly reliable commitment and any positive posting cost the game reduces to Chicken, whose pure equilibria are one side demanding everything while the other waits, and whose mixed equilibrium is the standoff with probability $(1-c/3)^2$ in river units; the standoff is the unique outcome only when commitment is cheap relative to the pie and sticks with probability $q<1$, and then it occurs with probability $q^2$. All six also reached the two ways the model's own premise defeats it: published instructions arrive in an order, and the second mover accepts a committed demand it can see, a race for B or C; and published instructions are programs that can condition on the other side's, under which efficient divisions are equilibria (Tennenholtz 2004; Kalai, Kalai, Lehrer and Samet 2010; the collator notes that this makes the standoff avoidable, not avoided, since the program-equilibrium set is every individually rational division). The economist adds that risk dominance in the river's Chicken selects whichever side commits more cheaply, exactly when $c_N>c_f$, and one factory commits more cheaply than three residents who must first agree. The cold reader and the repeated-games theorist add, independently, that the model's own estimation noise applied to D is Nash's 1953 smoothing of the demand game, under which $(6,6)$ is never an equilibrium and play converges to 3–3 as the noise vanishes (agreement 0.78 at a noise of 1 rising to 0.999 at 0.01, in the repeated-games theorist's computation). *Verdict:* the standoff claim fails as stated; it narrows to one-shot, simultaneous, unconditional, nearly free commitments that stick with probability $q<1$, standoff probability $q^2$. The default-shift claim fails with it.

2. **"Resident 3, who has the most to lose from no deal, breaks first" is wrong.** At $k=3$ no deal is a clean river worth 0 to everyone, so no resident loses anything from it; resident 3 is the costliest to buy, needing 9 before any surplus; under a harm-proportional pact resident 3 has the most to lose and is the most loyal member; a lone resident has nothing to sell under unanimity, so breaking a joint demand gains nothing; undercutting and a side offer to one resident are $k=1$ reasoning, where the first to break is resident 1 (bilateral surplus 8, holdout threshold $\delta\ge0.8$), then 2 (7, 0.7), and resident 3 (1, 0.1) is last. Six of six. *Verdict:* delete the sentence; the anti-side-deal rule belongs at $k<n$.

3. **The model's compute channel does no work in the standoff cell, and section 5's D bullet contradicts section 3's definition of D.** With revocation costs above the pie, backing down never pays whatever a side believes about the other, so "misjudge whether the other has really committed, and back down" cannot happen; a provable commitment is checked by a hash at negligible cost; if compute lets a side see a commitment already made, it yields, so compute removes standoffs; under noisy best response the limit is the mixed equilibrium, certain only at zero cost; under noise about the pie the limit is 3–3. Six of six. *Verdict:* "more compute makes the impasse more reliable" fails; the second decision gate is triggered for cell D.

4. **"The only place in the model where the ratio of the two sides' compute decides anything" is false.** At $k=3$ publishing commitments is also a race to commit first (cold reader, cooperative, advocate, repeated-games); risk dominance compares the two sides' commitment costs (economist); capability gaps move the split in cell A, with Opus agents in Project Deal selling for $2.68 more and buying for $2.45 less than Haiku agents against a $12 median, unnoticed by their principals (behavioural); in adaptive play the selected division's weights rise with each side's information and a group's weight is set by its least-informed member (repeated-games, [likely]). Six of six. *Verdict:* the race claim's uniqueness fails; where the ratio matters its sign favours the single principal and the better-informed side, the opposite of the model's.

5. **The residents are not a side.** The right-hand column presupposes an internal allocation rule among three veto players, which is the river's own holdout problem; where commitment is cheap for whoever has an agent, each resident can commit to the whole 6 against the other two, and a single committed resident already takes the pie under unanimity (cooperative); the internal division is a three-player demand problem with a continuum of equilibria, and with three simultaneous votes "all reject" is itself an equilibrium of cell B's acceptance stage, since no single resident is pivotal (repeated-games); cell B quietly treats the residents as three people (cold reader); the table omits the cell the river itself describes, residents committing separately (behavioural); "bargaining as one" with $r_N=0$ is a contradiction (advocate); cell C's internal split is Miettinen and Vanberg's inefficient unanimity case (economist). Miettinen and Vanberg 2025, verified by the collator from the publisher's abstract, make exactly this the theorem: with strategic precommitment, unanimity is the uniquely inefficient voting rule and every other rule, including all-but-one, is fully efficient. Six of six. *Verdict:* cell C needs three conditions, not one, and the third is the institution the model silently assumes; the best-grounded standoff in the whole model is holdout among the residents under $k=n$, a property of the voting rule, not of agents.

6. **Institutions are assigned to cells by fiat, and "nothing else gets out of D" is false.** A liability rule removes the veto, a change of entitlement (Calabresi and Melamed, whom the river cites), and S is whatever the statutory price makes it, harms plus 1.5 each giving ¾ (repeated-games); a union rule makes the residents one player but neither gives them commitment nor denies it to the factory, so with the factory's agent present it yields D or a race unless a second rule bars the factory, which labour law supplies (cooperative, economist). Exits the model omits: sequencing; conditional commitment; priced commitment (Krier's options analogy); asymmetric standoff costs, which is Krier's reimbursing veto and the model's own second falsifier; third-party buy-outs of holdouts; a change of $k$ (at $k=2$ the factory buys residents 1 and 2 for a little over 2 and 3, resident 3 is harmed unpaid, and the deal closes); a coalition-feasibility restriction, which changes pivotality rather than commitment; commitment to a procedure, arbitration or a coin; randomising who commits; safe Pareto improvements; repetition. Cold reader, advocate, cooperative, economist, repeated-games; the behavioural reader's Miettinen–Vanberg point makes the change of $k$ the exit with a theorem behind it. Six of six. *Verdict:* the institutions-choose claim holds in the weak form that is Calabresi–Melamed's and Krier's own position, and fails as "nothing else".

**Findings four or five reached independently.**

7. **Cell A's split is not derived, and on the river it is wrong.** With nobody able to commit the residents are three veto players, not a bloc, so the $k=3$, $\pi=20$ game has four symmetric veto players; the Shapley value, the symmetric four-person Nash solution, the stationary unanimity-bargaining limit and Harsanyi–Selten's Nash-product selection each give 1.5 to every party, payments $(3.5, 4.5, 10.5)$, the factory keeping 1.5, $S=\tfrac34$, not $\tfrac12$ (cold reader, cooperative, behavioural, economist, repeated-games; the advocate reached the weaker form, that "bargaining as one" with $r_N=0$ is a contradiction and 3–3 a stipulation). The 3/3 split is the Owen value with the residents as an a priori union (cooperative), which presupposes the bloc that cell C says needs commitment; so under unanimity fragmentation favours the residents and it is the factory that wants them to bargain as one (behavioural). Multilateral holdout bargaining with non-contingent sequential offers also has delay and failure equilibria (Cai 2000 [likely]), and in four-party unanimity bargaining every individually rational allocation is a subgame-perfect equilibrium for patient players (Shaked, via Chatterjee and Sabourian 2000 [sure]), so "efficient" in A is protocol-dependent. Five of six. *Verdict:* cell A as written fails; the table needs a four-player left column.

8. **Humans in large cases do not sit near cell A.** Repeat players commit through reputation, posted prices, delegation with limited authority and strike votes; the model's own B example, the developer's standard-form offer, is humans in B today; a developer buying from one-shot homeowners across many projects gets at least its commitment payoff by reputation (Fudenberg and Levine 1989), so it is in B with or without agents; human large cases sit under zoning and nuisance law, the planner, which the table has no cell for; Ellingsen and Miettinen open by rejecting the premise, writing that negotiators "often have access to quite efficient commitment technologies"; take-it-or-leave-it bargaining by a single committed firm is what labour law outlawed as Boulwarism (NLRB v. General Electric, 1969). Economist, behavioural, advocate, cold reader, repeated-games. Five of six. *Verdict:* the default-shift claim's first half fails independently of its second.

9. **The bridge claim points to B, not D.** Where agents have been measured, playing closer to equilibrium strips the responder's refusal, which is the human commitment in the ultimatum game, and sends the surplus to the first mover or the committed side (behavioural, with Araujo and Uhlig's 0.23% acceptance threshold and Li and Shirado's reasoning models); "better at the game" in the Nash demand game means better at selecting among a continuum, and human coordination on equal splits is robust (advocate); conventions in the demand game settle near the Nash split (cold reader, via Young 1993); risk dominance and learning dynamics select the cheaper committer (economist); where the human outcome rests on a tie-break at indifference in one-shot play, more precise agents remove it, and elsewhere they converge to some equilibrium, often the norm, and hold it longer; learning in the ultimatum game converges to equilibria in which low offers are rejected, and it is the residents' precision, not the factory's estimation, that drives B to $S=0$ (repeated-games, Gale, Binmore and Samuelson 1995). The behavioural reader's survey finds current agents satisficing and accommodating (first-proposal bias at 10–30× returns to speed in Magentic Marketplace; Project Deal's inert aggression prompt), no study offering agents a commitment stage, cooperation set by the developer rather than capability (1.5% to 71.5% across 25 models), and the one human lab test of two-sided commitment, Chlaß and Miettinen 2026 (verified by the collator from the abstract), reaching near-full efficiency in late rounds with proposers committing even when it cannot pay. Five of six. *Verdict:* the bridge claim holds in the narrow form "payoff-maximising agents play closer to the subgame-perfect equilibrium of one-shot fully specified games, which strips the responder's refusal", and that form moves the river to B; as the route to D it is contradicted, so the fourth gate is triggered.

10. **A published instruction and its hash prove what the instructions say, not that they cannot be replaced; a revocation cost needs a bond, an escrow or a court, which is an institution.** So "without them cheap commitment chooses D" refutes itself: without an institution there is no $r$ (economist, with Katz 1991 on renegotiable delegation; cold reader; behavioural; the advocate names human override as the honest gap in Krier). The realistic agent case is Ellingsen and Miettinen's observable but imperfectly binding commitment, $q<1$, which is the only case in which the standoff is unique, and its driver is imperfect binding, not proof. Four of six. *Verdict:* section 2's definition of cheap commitment fails; the narrowing is the one in finding 1.

11. **No information regime in the river supports an estimation error of $\sigma/\sqrt{c}$ on the harms.** In the transparent river there is nothing to estimate; in the private river each resident is the only source of their harm and is paid to misreport it, which the river's own sentence says sampling cannot shrink; the model needs a third regime, objective harms measured with noise, that the river lacks (cold reader, economist, advocate, repeated-games, who adds Hart and Mas-Colell 2003: the obstacle to convergence under uncoupled dynamics is information, not computation). Four of six. *Verdict:* the convergence claim's B and C cases survive only in that third regime, which the essay must state.

**Findings two or three reached independently.**

12. **In the factory-commits cell the margin is not $\sigma/\sqrt{c}$ and the residents' share is not monotone in the factory's budget.** Gaussian errors are unbounded, so no margin avoids refusal; literal plug-in play, offering the estimate itself, is accepted by each resident with probability ½ and closes the deal with probability ⅛ at every budget (economist, repeated-games); the profit-maximising margin is the error times a factor growing like $\sqrt{2\ln(2.4/s)}$, about 2.5 at $s=0.1$, 3.3 at $0.01$ and 3.9 at $0.001$ (cold reader on the form; economist and repeated-games on the formula, independently and in agreement); three independent acceptances need a per-resident margin growing with $n$ (cooperative). The economist's Monte Carlo, re-run by the collator, shows the deal closing 34%, 74%, 92%, 98% and 99.85% of the time as the factory's error per harm falls from 1.5 to 0.7, 0.3, 0.1 and 0.01 slices, while the residents' expected surplus rises from 1.07 to 2.08 and then falls to 1.61, 0.73 and 0.10: more factory compute raises efficiency throughout and cuts the residents' share only past a peak near 0.7 of a slice; an irreducible private component of harm stops the fall at a floor. Three of six on the rate; the hump is the economist's, confirmed by the collator. *Verdict:* the model's "from a price toward a quote, with a direction and a rate" survives as the second half of a hump; the rate is wrong and the first half is missing.

13. **The yardstick claim is wrong in both directions.** "Nobody ends worse off than under no deal in any cell" holds only at the corners $r\in\{0,\infty\}$ with zero commitment cost: with the positive cost that Ellingsen and Miettinen need to select the standoff, both parties in D end at $-c$, below no deal, so the model uses $c>0$ to get D and $c=0$ to say the yardstick cannot see it (cooperative); Krier's reimbursing veto, which the model files under B, puts residents below no deal when the factory commits to paying $(1,2,8)$ (advocate); the chain-restricted $k=1$ core puts the non-gatekeepers below no deal (cooperative). And "median better off" is not "anyone worse off": under an equal internal split the residents get $(1,1,1)$ in A, $(\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$ in B, $(2,2,2)$ in C and $(0,0,0)$ in D, so the median yardstick does separate A and C from D (cooperative). S is 0/0 in D and merges harms 2 and 9 (cooperative, cold reader), and the model never says which S is equitable (cold reader). Three of six. *Verdict:* the yardstick claim fails; the honest statement is that it takes efficiency and S together, and S is blind within the residents' side.

14. **The race at $k=1$ is a Tullock contest decided by stakes, not by compute, and the pact cannot form.** With chosen spending the win probability is the ratio of stakes, 14 against 10 (cold reader; economist, who adds that total equilibrium spending is $140/24\approx5.8$, more than the 4 the clean river saves, so cheap compute changes the form of the waste, not its size). The cooperative theorist adds two further failures reached alone: the factory needs the first of $n$ residents and the pact the last, so at equal per-agent rates the pact wins with probability 1/6 at $n=2$, 1/20 at $n=3$, 1/250 at $n=5$ (collator confirmed $n\le3$); and the pact cannot form at any speed, because residents 1 and 2 are worth 8 and 7 outside it and 0 inside, the enforcement it needs summing to the river's own core deficit of 16. The repeated-games theorist reaches the same impossibility by repetition: with spot bribes the pact needs $\delta\ge0.8$ (or $8/15$ with transfers from resident 3), and if the factory can offer a defector a long-term contract it needs $14\ge30$ and fails at every discount factor. The advocate adds that standing instructions pre-form the pact at configuration time, so the race contradicts the default-shift claim. *Verdict:* the race claim fails; the uniform-cheapening invariance survives trivially because nothing in the race is compute.

15. **Repetition dissolves the table.** With consents renewed each period at $k=3$, $\pi=20$, every party's minmax is 0 and every payoff vector with $\Sigma u\le6$ is a subgame-perfect payoff for patient players in all four cells (Fudenberg and Maskin 1986); for the residents as a bloc, repeated B has $S=0$ unique below $\delta=\tfrac12$ and every $S$ in $[0,1)$ above it, so at an annual discount factor of ½ the four cells' sets of efficient equilibria coincide; at $\pi=10$ repetition changes nothing; a standing published instruction is a commitment to a whole repeated-game strategy, which one-sided makes that side the leader and two-sided brings the folk theorem into the one-shot river (repeated-games, [derived]). Learning agents in repeated settings reach punishment-backed cooperation rather than one-shot equilibrium, with the level set by design (behavioural, repeated-games, Calvano et al. 2020; Fish, Gonczarowski and Shorrer; Assad et al. 2024); reputation and escrow make Krier's game repeated (advocate). Three of six, one of them assigned. *Verdict:* the one-shot framing holds with narrowing: the table describes one-shot sales of permanent rights between parties who do not deal again and whose commitments are unconditional; for renewed consents it determines nothing once $\delta\ge\tfrac12$; where one side is a repeat player its reputation already assigns the cell, and agents change the margin, not the cell.

**Single-attacker findings worth keeping.**

- **The Krier mapping** (advocate, the only reader given his text): developer → B has no textual basis, since the developer must meet every homeowner's reservation and faces ransom, the $k=n$ holdout regime with residents holding the leverage; club → D has none, the club is a residents'-side mechanism and the developer never commits; the reimbursing veto is Calabresi–Melamed's Rule 4, the party who stops the activity compensates the actor, not Rule 2, and on the river it can put residents below no deal; the option fee holds narrowed as a residents'-side tax on aggressive demands, which attacks D directly rather than legislating B; the digital union is C by capability with a B outcome, since Krier's club elicits minimums; of the two examples the model cites for $k=n$ only the developer passage supports it. The sharper attack on the club that the model missed: a common clearing price with minimums $(2,3,9)$ must be at least 9 to get resident 3, total $27>20$, so the club kills an efficient deal, and sum-of-minimums with individual payouts makes padding pay. The slogan "works at scale exactly to the extent that it stops being bargaining" fails as a reading of his proposal, since he states the hybrid himself, and holds only as a critique of his rhetoric. Partial independent confirmations: the behavioural reader finds "the better the agents, the more reliably [the club] fails to close" contradicted by Project Deal's only head-to-head comparison (Opus users closed 2.07 more deals each); the cooperative theorist finds the club landing on "the faster agent takes the pie"; the repeated-games theorist finds that whether a club fails depends on its voting rule.
- **Bounded compute is itself a commitment device, so the model's two budgets are not independent** (repeated-games): an unbounded player facing a finite automaton best-responds and can be exploited (Gilboa and Samet 1989); a fixed extortionate strategy extracts surplus from an adaptive opponent (Press and Dyson 2012); an optimiser facing a no-regret learner gets at least its Stackelberg value (Deng, Schneider and Sivan 2019); complexity costs do not narrow unanimity bargaining, and with noise only the stationary allocation, $S=\tfrac34$, survives (Chatterjee and Sabourian 2000). So the side that adapts concedes and the side that is fixed commits; raw compute makes a side a better best-responder, hence the follower; compute buys commitment power only when spent on shaping the other side's learning or verifying conditional commitments. On the river, resident agents that are no-regret learners against a fixed factory offer give $S\to0$.
- **Lock-in** (repeated-games): in stochastic-stability models the time to leave a non-selected convention grows like $\varepsilon^{-R}$, so under the model's own mapping, errors vanishing as $c\to\infty$, selection takes forever and history decides; better agents make the first convention stickier, so the rule in force when they arrive matters more; compute's plausible role is to lock in whichever convention forms first and tilt it toward the better-informed side.
- **Abreu and Gul** (economist): with commitment types on both sides the protocol-free limit is a war of attrition decided by ratios of prior credibility and patience, perpetual disagreement has probability $z_1z_2$ and the rest of the loss is delay; with verifiable code the credibility prior is 0 or 1 and the result has no bite. Ellingsen and Miettinen 2014 [sure, abstract]: with decaying commitments and re-commitment, the unique Markov perfect equilibrium is a war of attrition in which the pie is lost to delay, not outright, which also answers the model's second falsifier (repeated-games).
- **Cell C is not B's mirror image** (economist): C needs one estimate and B three acceptances; at $s=0.1$ the factory's share in C is 0.042 against the residents' 0.124 in B.
- **The chain restriction** (cooperative): a nonempty $k=1$ core, but by changing who is pivotal, not by commitment; a third kind of institution the table cannot express; efficient, with the non-gatekeepers paying a bribe below their no-deal payoff.
- **No-regret learning can settle into a standoff rate of one third** (repeated-games): probability ⅓ on each of $(6,0)$, $(0,6)$ and $(6,6)$ is a correlated equilibrium of the bloc demand game, each side expecting 2 (collator: checks).
- **Crawford's comparative statics** (collator, from the text; cooperative from the abstract): making commitment harder, or enlarging the contract zone, "need not always lower the probability of impasse"; the model's monotone "cheaper commitment, more standoffs" is the conventional wisdom Crawford shows cannot be supported a priori.
- **The cleanest experiment** (behavioural): Ellingsen and Miettinen's game embedded in the river, varying cost, binding probability, sealed versus asynchronous posting and conditionability, across providers, tiers and thinking budgets; not run with agents as far as found.

**What survives.**

- Whoever alone can commit takes the surplus: the ultimatum game, true with or without agents (all six). Cells B and C stand, as outcomes of one-sided commitment, and they are the only two cells with a unique refined one-shot equilibrium.
- Ellingsen and Miettinen's theorem, correctly stated: one-shot, simultaneous, observable, unconditional commitments that are cheap relative to the pie and stick with probability $q<1$ produce a unique outcome in which both sides demand everything, and conflict with probability $q^2$. Near-perfect but imperfect commitment is the standoff's home. Their scale remark, that at a fixed commitment cost "any sufficiently big issue invites conflict", supplies the mechanism the model's section 7 lacked for why the thermostat and the parking dispute stay out of it (economist; collator).
- Miettinen and Vanberg: under strategic precommitment unanimity is uniquely inefficient and every other rule is efficient. This is the strongest published support near the thesis, and it locates the standoff in the voting rule $k=n$, among the residents, not in agents on both sides.
- Institutions choose the cell, in the weak form: a rule that assigns commitment to one side fixes the division. This is Calabresi and Melamed's 1972 thesis and Krier's stated position, with his rules voluntary as often as statutory.
- In the factory-commits cell, factory compute raises the deal rate and, past a peak, lowers the residents' share: the hump (economist; collator).
- Capability asymmetry moves the split even in haggling, unnoticed by principals (behavioural, Project Deal), and the side that adapts concedes to the side that is fixed (repeated-games).
- The one-shot framing, for permanent rights sold once between parties who do not deal again (repeated-games).

**What this does to the thesis.** The four gates in the handoff:

- *Standoff.* Fails as stated. The thesis loses its headline. What the attackers converge on instead, six of six in their own words, is that cheap commitment for everyone produces a race to commit first, or a Chicken game whose risk-dominant convention is the cheaper committer, and that the cheaper and faster committer is one principal rather than three: cell B, efficient, on the factory's terms. "Cheap commitment for everyone is a standoff" becomes "cheap commitment for everyone hands the pie to whoever is already one principal". That is a distributional claim, and it is the essay's old intuition in a different cell. The standoff that does have a theorem behind it is holdout among the residents under unanimity.
- *Convergence.* Fails as stated. Compute does no work in the standoff cell; in the one-sided cells it works only in an information regime the river lacks, and there it first raises efficiency and then lowers the weaker side's share. Under repetition no cell has a limit at all. The essay's compute content is the hump in cell B, the capability gap in cell A, the leader-versus-learner asymmetry, and lock-in, all of which make the ratio of compute matter, which is the opposite of the model's section 6.
- *Krier mapping.* Fails on the reimbursing veto (Rule 4, not a liability rule in the model's sense) and on the developer and the club; holds narrowed on the option fee. Section 7 of the model goes. The disagreement with Krier narrows to scale: his club cannot clear harms of 2, 3 and 9 against a profit of 20 at one common price, and his union is C by capability with a B outcome by his own design.
- *Bridge.* Contradicted as the route to D. In its surviving form it is a route to B. The thesis as written is a bet against the current evidence, and the evidence points at B.

**Hygiene, to fix regardless.** The model's section 3 sentence on Ellingsen and Miettinen and Crawford is wrong on both. Ellingsen and Miettinen 2008 (collator, full text): their commitment cost is small and positive; with never-failing commitment three outcomes survive iterated strict dominance, two of them one side taking all, the third the mixed equilibrium with conflict probability $(1-c/b_1)(1-c/b_2)$; the standoff is unique only for $q<1$ and cheap commitment, with probability $q^2$; at $c=0$ the same needs iterated weak dominance; commitments are made "in a single period only" and they name sequential or delayed commitments as the open avenue. Crawford 1982 (collator, full text): revocation costs are random draws learned privately after the attempt, "bargainers are perfectly informed about everything except their costs of backing down"; impasse has positive probability $(1-F^i(d^i))(1-F^j(d^j))$; under known costs his p. 611 says the game is Nash's demand game with "great strategic uncertainty, in the form of multiple Nash equilibria"; his leading special case is a Prisoner's Dilemma with impasse probability $p_1p_2$; his section 5 shows that making commitment harder need not lower the probability of impasse. "This is Crawford's 1982 formulation and it is the standard one" is wrong on the object. Miettinen and Vanberg 2025 (collator, abstract): "unanimity rule is uniquely inefficient. All other rules, including all-but-one, are fully efficient." Abreu and Gul 2000 (collator, abstract): "the bargaining outcome is independent of the details of the bargaining protocol if players can make offers frequently"; delay and inefficiency vanish as the probability of irrationality goes to zero. Chlaß and Miettinen 2026 (collator, abstract): "In a simultaneous commitment game, proposers who cannot benefit from committing are nevertheless observed to commit" and "in late rounds, allowing for pre-commitment yields almost 100 % efficiency while the standard ultimatum game continues to feature significant inefficiencies." Smaller items: S is defined as a count and used as a fraction; $\sigma$ is never defined; section 6 calls $k=1$ a "cell"; "the two who did not sign" should be all three residents, since the signer's token is below their harm; of the two Krier examples cited for $k=n$ in section 1, the beach passage requires an auditable offer to everyone deprived, not everyone's consent; the "revocation cost" definition of commitment should be attributed to Schelling's bond or third-party contract, not Crawford. The economist's report has one arithmetic slip, a standoff probability of 98% at a posting cost of 0.06 where the formula gives 96%. The repeated-games theorist's Nash-smoothing table and folk-theorem thresholds are his own derivations, not re-run by the collator.
