## Ellingsen & Miettinen 2008, AER 98(4) 1629-35. [sure; FULL TEXT read from the open LSE copy, sticerd.lse.ac.uk/seminarpapers/et04122008.pdf]
Model (Section I): two players, surplus 1, common knowledge. Stage 1, simultaneous: commit to a demand s_i in [0,1] at cost c >= 0, or wait (w). Stage 2: two uncommitted players split b_1 + b_2 = 1 ("a reduced form of an (unmodelled) ensuing bargaining game"); a committed player cannot revoke; an uncommitted opponent OBSERVES the commitment and best responds with 1 - s_i; compatible commitments: each gets at least s_i - c; incompatible: "there is disagreement and both players get utility -c". "Here, we focus exclusively on the case of observable commitments." Commitments are made "in a single period only".
Certain commitment (q = 1):
- Lemma 1 (c > 0): only the greedy demand 1 and waiting w survive iterated strict dominance. The reduced 2x2 game is CHICKEN: neither (1,1) nor (w,w) is an equilibrium.
- Proposition 1 (0 < c < b_2): a unique pair of pure asymmetric equilibria (one player commits to 1, the other waits: one side takes all) and one mixed equilibrium with p_i(1) = (b_i - c)/b_i, p_i(w) = c/b_i. In the mixed equilibrium conflict occurs when both commit, probability p_1(1) p_2(1), which tends to 1 as c -> 0. This is the abstract's "third outcome ... a high probability of conflict".
- Proposition 2 (c = 0, iterated weak dominance): the same three outcomes: (i) both commit to 1 [conflict]; (ii)/(iii) one commits to 1 and the other waits. "As noted already by Crawford (1982), there is a plethora of efficient equilibria when c = 0" under plain Nash equilibrium.
Uncertain commitment (q < 1, Section II): Crawford's case with c = 0: if q < 1/2 the unique equilibrium has both commit to 1, conflict with probability q^2; if q > 1/2 "there is always an efficient Nash equilibrium in which s_1 = s_2 = 1/2".
- Proposition 3: (i) if 0 < c < q(1-q)(1-b_i), only s_i = 1 is iteratively strictly undominated: unique outcome, both demand everything, conflict with probability q^2 "even as q is arbitrarily close to 1". (ii) if q(1-q)(1-b_i) <= c <= q(1-b_i), only 1 and w survive (Chicken again). "with uncertain commitment and low cost of attempting, it becomes a Prisoner's Dilemma: attempting commitment is a strictly dominant strategy."
- Proposition 4 (c = 0, q < 1, iterated weak dominance): only s_i = 1 survives.
- Scale: "it is the ratio of the commitment cost to the size of the surplus that matters for the likelihood of conflict ... our model is consistent with the notion that some issues are too small to be fighting about. Conversely, any sufficiently big issue invites conflict."
- Limits they name: single commitment period; no delays in communication: "Two natural avenues ... allowing more time periods, and allowing (random) delays in the communication of commitments." Also: Muthoo 1996 "obtains an efficient compromise solution as the unique outcome" in a related model where revocation cost is not prohibitive.
Consequences for the model file:
- Cell D as "both commit to everything, certain standoff" is ONE of three outcomes under perfectly reliable (q = 1) cheap commitment; the other two are the model's own cells B and C (one side takes all). The standoff is uniquely selected only when commitment is cheap relative to the pie AND slightly unreliable (q < 1), and then it occurs with probability q^2, not certainty. The model's words "free and provable" should read "cheap relative to the surplus and almost certain to stick".
- The cost direction in the model's sentence is inverted: a small positive cost is what removes the efficient compromise; at c = 0 the same needs iterated weak dominance.
- Sequential or delayed commitments are outside E-M by their own statement; three attackers' objection that observable commitments arrive in an order is E-M's own named open avenue, not a result either way.
- IN THE MODEL'S FAVOUR: E-M supply the mechanism the model's section 7 lacks for why the thermostat and parking cases stay out of the standoff: a commitment cost fixed in money is large relative to a small surplus and small relative to a large one.

## Tennenholtz 2004, "Program equilibrium", GEB 49(2) 363-373. [sure the result exists; collator's own knowledge, the text was not reached: ScienceDirect, Semantic Scholar and the Kellogg mirror all blocked or gone]
Two players each submit a program that can read the other's program; a program equilibrium is a pair of programs each a best response to the other. Main theorem: every feasible payoff profile that gives each player at least his one-shot minimax payoff is a program-equilibrium payoff (a folk theorem for the one-shot game), by programs of the form "if the opponent's program equals mine, play the agreed action, else punish". Consequence for the attackers' use of it: program commitment makes the standoff AVOIDABLE, not avoided; (3,3), (6,0), (0,6) are all program equilibria of the pie of 6, so it dissolves the necessity of cell D and reintroduces the selection problem. Critch, Dennis & Russell 2022 (open-source game theory) discuss exactly that [likely; not read].

## Young 1993, "An evolutionary model of bargaining", JET 59(1) 145-168. [sure the result exists; not re-read]
Adaptive play in the Nash demand game with bounded memory, sampling and rare mistakes: the stochastically stable divisions converge to the Nash bargaining solution as the demand grid becomes fine; symmetric players, the equal split. Bears on cell A (a derivation of the equal split from a learning dynamic rather than from a stipulation) and on cell D (the cold reader's point that conventions settle near the Nash split). E-M's footnote 7 cite Young for the destabilising role of a small failure probability.

## Miettinen & Vanberg 2025, AEJ Micro 17(1) 206-37, "Commitment and Conflict in Unanimity Bargaining". [sure; publisher abstract]
Abstract verbatim: "We theoretically investigate how the application of unanimity rule can lead to inefficient delay in collective decision-making. We do so in the context of a distributive multilateral bargaining model featuring strategic precommitment. Prior to each bargaining round, players can declare a minimum share that they must receive in return for their vote. Such declarations become binding with an exogenously given probability. We characterize the set of stationary subgame perfect equilibria (SSPE) under all q-majority rules. Our results suggest that unanimity rule is uniquely inefficient. All other rules, including all-but-one, are fully efficient."
Relevance: the river's k=n=3 is unanimity; commitment-driven inefficiency is specific to it; k=2 of 3 would be efficient in their model. Bears on the institutions-choose claim ("nothing else gets out of the standoff cell"): a voting rule short of unanimity does, in this model, at the price the essay already knows (k<n lets the factory drop a resident).

## Abreu & Gul 2000, Econometrica 68(1) 85-118. [sure; RePEc abstract + Wiley snippet]
Abstract: complete-information bargaining amended with "irrational types" inflexible in offers and demands. "Independence of procedures": outcome independent of protocol details if offers are frequent. Limiting continuous-time game: unique equilibrium, entails delay, hence inefficiency; Wiley snippet: "a war of attrition structure". As the probability of irrationality goes to zero, delay and inefficiency disappear; with a rich set of types on both sides, limit payoffs inversely proportional to rates of time preference.
Relevance: if "commitment" is reputational (types), two committed-looking sides produce delay and a split decided by patience, not a certain standoff; impasse probability vanishes with the type probability.

## Miettinen & Perea 2010 (SITE WP 08), "Commitment in Alternating Offers Bargaining". [sure; read the WP]
Stahl alternating offers with simultaneous visible commitments before each round, small costs increasing in committed share: "the availability of such commitment strategies never leads to inefficiencies"; immediate agreement; second-mover advantage; limit = reversed Rubinstein split. Intro: "Crawford (1982) showed that commitment strategies may lead to inefficiencies when information is incomplete."
Relevance: a counterexample to "symmetric cheap commitment -> standoff" under a dynamic protocol with complete information; the standoff needs the one-shot simultaneous structure or imperfect commitment.

## Crawford 1982, Econometrica 50(3) 607-37. [sure; read the JSTOR PDF Dan supplied, 2026-10-21]
Abstract (verbatim, in part): "It is demonstrated that in spite of this avoidable inefficiency, when successful commitment is uncertain and irreversible it can still be rational for individuals to attempt commitment and thereby risk an impasse; in a leading special case, the model reduces to a Prisoner's Dilemma game, in which only strategic-dominance arguments are needed to establish this conclusion. Further, making commitment more difficult, or changing the costs of disagreement in a way that makes available a wider range of settlements that are better for both bargainers than disagreement, need not always lower the probability of impasse, in spite of the conventional wisdom to the contrary."
Model (pp. 612-615): two stages. Stage 1: bargainers simultaneously decide whether to attempt commitment; an attempt is a demand plus "a draw from a probability distribution, whose realization is the cost ... that must be borne if the bargainer in question later decides to accept anything less than his demand". Stage 2: "each bargainer learns his own, but not his opponent's, cost of backing down" and decides whether to retreat. "bargainers are perfectly informed about everything except their costs of backing down." If neither attempts: a known Pareto-efficient compromise. If one alone achieves commitment: he gets his demand. If both achieve incompatible commitments: the disagreement outcome.
p. 611, on certainty: "With irreversibility alone, questions of timing take on primary importance ... A bargainer who knew he could be the first to communicate an irrevocable demand to his opponent would find it to his advantage to do so, ending negotiations on the spot. And if bargainers must communicate their demands simultaneously, there is great strategic uncertainty, in the form of multiple Nash equilibria ... Uncertainty is equally essential."
Solution concept (p. 616): Bayesian Nash equilibrium plus perfectness (sequential rationality). "The result obtained here, that impasses can occur with positive probability in equilibrium, does not stem from bargainers having irrational expectations or making suboptimal decisions." "no equilibrium can have neither bargainer attempting commitment"; with one exception, none has only one attempting.
p. 617: equilibria are either just-compatible commitments (Nash's demand-game equilibria) or incompatible commitments with cutoff rules (back down iff realised cost <= d^i); impasse probability = (1-F^i(d^i))(1-F^j(d^j)), strictly between 0 and 1 for continuous costs.
pp. 619-623, Bernoulli example (cost prohibitive with prob q^i, zero otherwise): incompatible-commitment equilibria always exist when q's are near zero; a parameter configuration (linear frontier, compromise 1/2, q=7/20) where ONLY incompatible-commitment equilibria exist.
Section 4 (pp. 623-627): with constant perceived probabilities p_i of not backing down, iterated deletion of dominated strategies reduces the game to a Prisoner's Dilemma: both attempt commitment to their most favourable position; impasse with probability p_1 p_2.
Section 5 (pp. 630-635): "two assumptions ... that the frequency of impasse is reduced by increases in the size of the contract zone ... and by increases in the difficulty of commitment, cannot be supported on theoretical grounds if commitment to incompatible demands is part of the explanation for the occurrence of impasses." Conclusion (p. 635): "Neither is there support for the common belief that making commitment more difficult (in the sense of lowering the probability of success) makes impasse less likely."
Consequences for the model file:
- "This is Crawford's 1982 formulation and it is the standard one" is wrong on the object: Crawford's revocation cost is a RANDOM draw, learned PRIVATELY and only AFTER the attempt; the model's r is chosen, published and verifiable. The uncertainty is, in Crawford's words, "essential".
- "Crawford 1982 has the same conclusion with revocation costs": his conclusion is impasse with POSITIVE probability (both stand firm), never certainty; under known costs his own p. 611 says the game is Nash's demand game with multiple equilibria, which is the cold reader's objection.
- Crawford's PD reduction supports the model's default-shift intuition (both attempt commitment although both prefer the compromise), but in a heuristic-expectations model and with impasse probability p_1 p_2 < 1, the same structure as Ellingsen-Miettinen's q^2.
- Crawford's comparative statics cut against the model's monotone story that easier commitment means more standoffs: in his framework the sign is ambiguous a priori.

## Chlaß & Miettinen 2026, "Commitment in sequential bargaining - An experiment", JEBO 242. [sure; abstract via RePEc]
Abstract (in part, verbatim): "observed responder commitments are less aggressive than proposals by proposers in the ultimatum game. In a simultaneous commitment game, proposers who cannot benefit from committing are nevertheless observed to commit. The observed within-treatment payoff-differences between the two parties do not comply with the theoretical predictions in the commitment variants of the game. Surprisingly in late rounds, allowing for pre-commitment yields almost 100 % efficiency while the standard ultimatum game continues to feature significant inefficiencies. We discuss four complementary behavioral explanations and find that reciprocity and concern for equality of opportunity are consistent with the observed patterns."
Relevance: the one human lab test with a two-sided (simultaneous) commitment stage; the behavioural reader's citation of it checks. Whether the near-full efficiency holds in the simultaneous treatment alone is not stated in the abstract [likely, per the behavioural reader].
