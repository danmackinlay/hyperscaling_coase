## Ngo 2026, "Economic efficiency often undermines sociopolitical autonomy" [@Ngo2026Economic]

Richard Ngo, *Mind the Future*, 2026-03-10. <https://www.mindthefuture.info/p/economic-efficiency-often-undermines>

A general critique of what he calls "econ-brained" reasoning, with Krier named once, in the section on AGI, as the author of "the very econ-brained idea that society could be revolutionized by AI-enabled Coasean bargaining at scale", by analogy with the view that AGI will come as tools rather than agents. Three foundations, then five cases.

- Economic frameworks are outcome-oriented and cannot describe the relation between individual interests and larger entities: sovereignty, social trust and national identity are hard to price yet predictive of prosperity.
- They "assume that people act in their rational interests at each point in time" and "therefore rule out adversarial dynamics like credible threats (and following through on commitments more generally)". Offensive and defensive commitments are how groups decide (punishing criminals at a loss; defending the Falklands). Political power depends on worst-case scenarios, not margins; control of the worst case shapes the whole landscape of marginal incentives. His technical aside: "changes in disagreement points can have big effects on the solutions of bargaining games—though mainstream bargaining theory hasn't accounted for how this incentivizes threats."
- They assume commensurability, which undermines the reliability that autonomy needs: leaders whose integrity is for sale, speech punished by estimated harm, territory conceded.
- Cases: prediction markets become corruption markets; land value taxes strip the autonomy of groups insulated from commercial pressure (churches, communes, the Amish) and erode property rights as a "Schelling fence" against state expansion; higher education as elite-class formation; free trade as a loss of national and regional autonomy; AGI forecasting, where he lists econ-brained ideas including Krier's. He wants a formal theory of "sociopolitical rationality", gesturing at Garrabrant's geometric rationality.

*Relation to our essay.* Ngo rejects the frame; we stay inside it and show it fails on its own terms. His second foundation is the nearest thing in print to our rebuilt line: commitment and disagreement points decide bargaining outcomes, and standard theory under-weights threats. Any sentence of ours saying that cheap commitment changes who gets the pie, or that the threat point rather than cleverness decides the split, reprises him and cites him. His "Schelling fence" is a bright-line rule that binds against later temptation, which is our "a rule differs from a deal in exactly that"; cite him there too. What he does not have: any model, any numbers, any statement of which commitment configuration produces which outcome.

## Snow 2025, "Beyond Automated Politics: A Response to Seb Krier's AI Agent Economy" [@Snow2025Automated]

A. E. Snow, *The Democratic Futurist*, 2025-12-10. <https://democraticfuturist.substack.com/p/beyond-automated-politics-a-response>

A social-democratic and deliberative response. Opens with a vignette: an agent closes 47 deals overnight, including opposing a bike lane on the owner's revealed preference for parking; overriding would force renegotiation of the whole bundle at an estimated cost of £120, so the owner approves the batch.

- Preferences are embryonic. Krier's agents do price discovery on revealed preferences before the citizen has formed a judgment; political judgment is formed through reasoning with others; outsourcing it means "the muscle atrophies from disuse". Citizens who never reason together "are just individuals who happen to live near each other and whose agents negotiate on their behalf."
- Bargaining clubs need everyone to opt in: "If even one necessary party refuses to join the club, they retain their veto power, and the mechanism recreates the very holdout problem it was meant to solve."
- Inferred minima encode leverage. Once the agent proposes your minimum acceptable price from behavioural data, "A system may infer that you will accept less because you are poor, or conflict-averse, or historically willing to acquiesce." The wealthy resident who can wait gets a high offer; the struggling one gets "a calculated lowball". Krier's reply, hire a tougher agent, fails because the other side's model computes your real capacity to walk away: "If a vulnerable resident's agent bluffs like a billionaire, the deal simply collapses". "You can change the strategy of the agent, but you cannot change the leverage of the user."
- The bargain becomes "models exchanging predictions"; the clearing price is an artefact of model architecture, features and training data; "what looks like a negotiated agreement is in fact a technocratic simulation".
- Even working perfectly, the mechanisms govern trivial, reversible matters; the questions that matter need shared reasoning and legitimacy. Proposal: agents as guides for individual reasoning, as scaffolds for collective deliberation (Taiwan cited), and as executors of democratically chosen aims; "the inverse of Krier's architecture".

*Relation to our essay.* Three of our findings touch his. The club's opt-in problem is our unanimity and holdout point; if we say the club recreates the holdout, we cite him, and our addition is the arithmetic (a common clearing price of at least 9 for harms of 2, 3 and 9, so 27 against a profit of 20) and the theorem that unanimity is the uniquely inefficient rule under precommitment. His inferred-minimum point is our cell B, "the better the factory's agent knows each homeowner's floor, the less it pays", and our capability-gap finding from Project Deal; our addition is the margin, its rate, the floor set by what cannot be measured, and the hump in expected surplus. His "leverage, not strategy" is our threat-point and commitment line stated for the individual; cite him alongside Ngo. His bundling vignette (override costs £120 because the bundle must be renegotiated) is a point we do not have: packaging deals into bundles is a commitment device on the agent's side, and worth a sentence with his name on it. What he does not have: a model, the numbers, the one-principal-versus-three asymmetry, or the finding that unanimity itself is the inefficiency.

## Petel 2025, "Price What You Can, Protect What You Must" [@Petel2025Price]

Mateo Petel, 2025-09-28, two days after Krier's essay, "to elevate the debate and complement Seb's work". <https://mateopetel.substack.com/p/price-what-you-can-protect-what-you>

A sympathetic constitutionalist response: agents can and should shrink the Coase gap, but "price what you can; protect what you must": rights and red lines first, continuous clearing underneath.

- Agents fix three frictions: discovery (who is affected, what they want), commitment ("the choreography of offers, counters, time limits, escrows, and contingencies that make a bargain real"), and verification. Problems that look like policy but are scheduling (the curb, household electricity) become markets. Ostrom's conditions, boundaries, cheap monitoring, low-cost conflict resolution, become computable.
- Limits: blocked exchanges (Walzer), public reason (Habermas), wealth and endowment effects that break Coase invariance ("A poor farmer may sell away air quality to a rich factory not because the trade maximizes welfare, but because cash now beats health later"); Harberger-style option taxes (Posner and Weyl) blunt ransom demands but presuppose identity, low collusion and honest oracles; without proof of personhood "a neighborhood of one thousand can flower into a swarm of ten thousand sock-puppet agents"; a bargaining club "can become a cartel with better UX"; measurement is political (Scott, Goodhart), and "the more we can enforce, the more we must sense"; adversarial agents will "drip micro-nuisances to extract rents" and "assemble flash coalitions to grief an unpopular but beneficial project", a racketeering layer whose equilibrium is "pay me or I make your life slightly worse"; catastrophic risk is for prohibition, not pricing; Acemoglu's political Coase theorem is "the hard stop": agents "can't bind the sovereign, only institutions can".
- The constitutional layer: Calabresi and Melamed's trichotomy of property rules, liability rules and inalienability made "unavoidable and visible"; "If we do not draw the line, platforms will draw it for us in their defaults." "Agentic markets naturally reach for liability rules because they are legible to software and pair well with escrow. That is a feature, but also a temptation." Property rules where consent must be obtained in fact; inalienability where the boundary is moral. Initial entitlements matter because wealth matters; the baseline allocation is a constitutional question on a slow clock; privacy is a right, not a setting.
- The minimal governance stack: identity and personhood ("one human, one principal, one accountable agent"); contracts that produce evidence, with two oracles and a human fallback and retention limits; monitoring with differential privacy and zero-knowledge proofs; tiered dispute resolution in which losing is affordable; interoperability so that exit is real and platforms do not become "planners in disguise"; safety rails, a no-trade registry, rate limits on automated negotiation where correlated mistakes are dangerous, red-team leagues, kill switches under due process.

*Relation to our essay.* He is the closest neighbour and the most sympathetic to Krier. His Calabresi and Melamed point is adjacent to our "institutions choose the cell" and to our Rule 2 versus Rule 4 finding on the reimbursing veto; when we say liability rules are what agent markets reach for, we cite him, and our addition is which cell each rule writes into law and that a liability rule changes the entitlement rather than assigning commitment. His treatment of commitment has the opposite sign to ours: for him commitment is a friction agents fix, with escrow "so that promises keep themselves"; for us cheap commitment on both sides is the problem, and the escrow is the institution. That disagreement should be stated with his name on it. His "one human, one principal, one accountable agent" bears directly on our one-decision-maker-versus-three asymmetry: without it the residents' side can be inflated by sock puppets, which is a new angle for us, cited to him. His "agents can't bind the sovereign" is our tabled contest over the rules. His wealth-effects paragraph overlaps the essay's existing aside on linear welfare. His flash-coalition griefing is Krier's flash coalition with the sign reversed, and our race finding, that the pact cannot form on the river's numbers, bears on both. What he does not have: a model, the river's numbers, or any result on when bargaining fails rather than on what to fence off.
