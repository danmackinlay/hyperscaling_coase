# What the seven critics said about the Coasean street stories

## 1. The short answer

All seven critics agree that the set holds together as a demonstration that agent bargaining needs rules it cannot write for itself, and most rate sketches 6, 8 and 9 as strong. The main thing wrong is that the sketches claim more than that demonstration supports. Most of their remedies are rules Krier's "framework guarantor" state already concedes, yet the sketches call them "a planner", and each sketch hands its remedy to a disinterested outsider whose existence it simply assumes. So Krier can grant most of the preconditions and keep his thesis. A second, smaller fault is that every person and agent is a fixed-preference payoff-maximizer, and three critics say this hides both the optimists' best defence and the critics' worst case.

## 2. Where the critics converge

Five critics (libertarian, Acemoglu, Ostrom, Segal, Bowles–Gintis) object independently to calling the remedies a planner. A lottery over proposers, unanimity, an identity registry, a court hearing third-party claims, a regional compact and state-aid rules are all rules of the kind Krier's guarantor state is meant to supply. Segal and Bowles–Gintis draw the same conclusion: aim the essay at the remedies Krier did not foresee or cannot grant. Segal names these as institutions that need local knowledge (the assessor in sketch 4) or that must override the distributional result of a bargain.

Five critics (libertarian, Acemoglu, Ostrom, Segal, Bowles–Gintis) want sketch 7's real point, whether a renter, child or trust counts as a party, merged into or tied to sketch 6, and Kahneman says it needs more space.

Five critics name sketch 1's lottery as random recognition of the proposer, a known legislative device (libertarian, Acemoglu, Segal, Papadimitriou, Bowles–Gintis). Three dispute that it is odd or that nobody would propose it: the libertarian says parties unsure of their future would agree to it, Kahneman calls turn-taking the oldest fairness rule, and Ostrom says resource users invent rotation by lot repeatedly. Three (libertarian, Kahneman, Papadimitriou) note that sketch 1 inverts Krier's own protocol, in which households post the price, and want that said outright.

Three critics (Kahneman, Ostrom, Bowles–Gintis) say the neighbours are modelled as strangers who cannot talk to or sanction one another, so sketch 2's pact collapses for want of what holds real pacts together. Kahneman and Bowles–Gintis add that in sketch 1 a responder carrying its principal's sense of fairness would refuse insulting offers, and that the sharper risk is the agent layer stripping out face-to-face sanction.

Four critics (libertarian, Kahneman, Segal, Papadimitriou) find sketch 5 weakest, since agents only speed up an old bidding war. Three (libertarian, Segal, Papadimitriou) find sketch 10 weak because Krier concedes its economics, though four praise its closing point about captured institutions. All seven accept sketch 8: the client cannot see what the agent could have got, so switching disciplines nobody. Three (libertarian, Segal, Papadimitriou) want sketch 9's four mechanisms split.

## 3. Where they disagree

The critics split on whether an outside rule-maker can be assumed. Acemoglu says no disinterested party exists: whoever runs the lottery or registry holds power that will be fought over. Ostrom says users and nested associations write such rules themselves, without the state. The libertarian and Segal treat most of these rules as legitimate framework, no defeat for Krier.

On sketch 2, the libertarian, Segal and Bowles–Gintis rank it among the strongest, and Segal stresses that the pact's collapse is a theorem that holds whatever the agents' quality. Kahneman and Ostrom rank it among the weakest, because that collapse depends on neighbours who cannot talk or sanction one another.

Sketch 3 divides the critics too. Kahneman, Ostrom and Papadimitriou rank it strongest, while Acemoglu ranks it weakest because it blames computation rather than power, and Bowles–Gintis call its second half weak. Within its supporters, Segal and Papadimitriou say the doubling table is a hard communication limit (Segal and Papadimitriou, under sketch 3), while Kahneman says the sixteen numbers do not exist stably to be recorded.

On sketch 4, Segal calls it the strongest against Krier, because the assessor needs the local knowledge Krier denies a planner. The libertarian says Carol consented and was paid her harm, and a tribunal is ordinary rule of law; Ostrom and Bowles–Gintis say the neighbours already know whose child has asthma.

On scoring, the libertarian rejects judging outcomes by the division of gains and wants them judged first by whether a right was crossed or consent was hollow; Segal and Bowles–Gintis accept a fairness measure but want sketch 10's criterion named.

Sketch 7 is strong for Papadimitriou, because identities cheapen as inference does, and weakest for Acemoglu and Bowles–Gintis. Sketch 1 is strongest for Acemoglu and Kahneman but weak for Segal, who says it rests on an unstated commitment assumption (Segal, under sketch 1).

## 5. Stories the critics say are missing

- The same street run through today's regulatory process, so that comparisons are not against an ideal bargain (libertarian, Acemoglu).
- Pricing a nuisance changes how much people mind it, or crowds out neighbourly goodwill (Kahneman, Bowles–Gintis).
- A self-governing street, with its own clauses, membership list and monitoring, meets the repeat-player company (Ostrom); relatedly, an agent that carries its principal's reciprocity (Bowles–Gintis, Kahneman).
- The enforcer reneges or is captured, testing Krier's transparency-and-auditing reply; and bargaining gains turn into political power that rewrites the protocol (Acemoglu).
- Hold-up: renegotiation by fast agents destroys the commitments investment needs (Segal).
- A company with correlated data across streets extracts the full surplus (Segal).
- Agent dynamics that fail to converge, or delivery firms' pricing agents colluding tacitly (Papadimitriou).
- Who monitors performance, and the power that gives them (Bowles–Gintis); how an entitlement arose (libertarian).

On the "Not yet used" question, the libertarian and Acemoglu both say the consultation should be the frame.

## 7. What to do next

1. Restructure: fold sketch 7's question of who counts into 6; say that sketch 1 inverts Krier's protocol and that its lottery is a known device; split sketch 9, perhaps moving its reputation half into 1 to supply the missing commitment (all seven critics).
2. Drop "planner" for framework rules; sort preconditions into those Krier's guarantor state grants and those it cannot (local knowledge, distributional override), and aim at the second (libertarian, Acemoglu, Ostrom, Segal, Bowles–Gintis).
3. Name the known result under each sketch, mark theorems apart from toy-model parameters, and replace "computation" with the actual resource where it is not computation (Segal, Papadimitriou; citation requests from the libertarian and Acemoglu).
4. Give the people, and at least one agent, social motives: rerun or re-narrate sketch 2 with neighbours who can talk and sanction one another, and add a crowding-out story (Kahneman, Ostrom, Bowles–Gintis).
5. Make the consultation the frame, compare each outcome against today's regulatory process as well as the ideal, and add a story in which the rule-maker is captured (libertarian, Acemoglu).

## 8. Added later: an incomplete-contracts read of Krier

This section comes from a different exercise from sections 1–7. One further reader, in the manner of an incomplete-contracts theorist (Segal, Hart), read Krier's essay itself, with the sketches and the prior-critiques file as background only. The full note is `hyperscaling_coase_incomplete_contracts_read.md`; headings below refer to its sections. It answers the hold-up story the Segal critic asked for in section 5.

**What cheap agents change.** Of the standard reasons contracts are left incomplete, cheap agents remove one, the cost of thinking through and writing every case, and the note says the theory had already shown that this cost is not the one that matters. Agents do not make a harm provable to a court: a sensor proves decibels, not that Carol slept badly. They make it harder to promise not to reopen a deal, because the cost of reopening was itself what held deals closed. Escrow secures payment; it will not refuse a release both parties ask for. The argument is under the note's heading 2.

**Who decides what the contract left unsaid.** The state decides the starting rights. In practice most of the rest falls to the platform that writes the protocol, which decides what can be agreed at all and who makes the final offer, and to the model developer, whose agent fills the gaps in its user's instructions (heading 3). This is the consultation again, reached from a different literature.

**Krier's proposal to price stability.** The note says it does not work: an agreement not to renegotiate is a contract the same parties can renegotiate, so the commitment has to come from something they cannot undo together (heading 4).

**The candidate thesis, narrowed.** The flexibility-against-commitment thesis survives only where someone must pay for something tied to the street before the price is fixed; for Saturday music, flexibility is pure gain. The strongest objection, that agents with escrow make commitment cheaper, holds for a household signing a fixed contract, but only by switching continual repricing off for the term, and it cannot bind later buyers or whoever writes the rules. That binding is what property law supplies, and Krier proposes to make baseline rights cheap to renegotiate. The note's own statement of the narrowed thesis is at the end of heading 5.

**The example.** Carol's double glazing, with one added move. Under continual repricing, her price for the night run falls once the glazing is in, so she pays and the company collects, and she does not glaze. A five-year fixed deal fixes that, until Bob sells to Dave, whose agent signed nothing and who can reopen the night slot with Carol's glazing already paid for (heading 6). The model check the note asks for: the glazing must benefit Carol herself, so that the fixed deal would have worked without Dave.

**How this bears on sections 1–7.** It supplies a precondition of the kind section 2 says to aim at: one Krier cannot grant as ordinary framework, since it needs the baseline rights to be hard to renegotiate, which the essay argues against. It also gives the single carrying example that none of the seven critics proposed.

**To check before citing.** The citations are from the reader's memory, with three confirmed to exist by search (Holden and Malani 2019, Che and Hausch 1999, Edlin and Reichelstein 1996). The note itself flags that Holden and Malani's exact claims need checking, and says it knows of no published critique of Krier along these lines, which is a statement about one reader's knowledge.
